# M04: Policy Memo: An Immediate Total Ban on Private Cars in the City

- **id:** M04
- **domain:** policy_memo (policy memo)
- **length:** medium (916 words)
- **quality:** bad
- **anchor score:** 4/10

**Rationale:** A one-sided, impractical memo. It ignores costs and feasibility, declaring a costing unnecessary and waving away emergency services, deliveries, and the transition, and it dismisses equity by labelling anyone who needs a car as resistant to change. Redeeming features: it correctly identifies genuine harms (air pollution, road deaths, congestion, public space consumed by cars) and is clearly written with a coherent problem-to-conclusion structure.

## Artefact

To: The City Council
From: Office of Urban Transformation
Subject: An immediate and total ban on private cars within the city

Recommendation. The Council should enact, effective immediately, a complete ban on all private cars within the city limits. There is no time to lose. Cars are the source of our worst problems, and the only responsible course is to remove them at once.

The problem. Private cars are destroying our city. They pollute the air we breathe, they clog our streets, they kill pedestrians, and they make our neighbourhoods ugly and dangerous. Every day that cars remain on our roads is another day of poisoned air, wasted time, and needless death. The evidence is overwhelming and obvious to anyone who walks our streets. Cars are simply incompatible with a liveable city, and no reasonable person could disagree.

The solution. The solution is equally obvious: ban them. All of them. Immediately. Once private cars are gone, our streets will be transformed overnight into clean, quiet, safe spaces for people. Air quality will improve dramatically. Traffic deaths will fall to zero. Children will play in the streets again. Businesses will thrive as pedestrians fill the pavements. The city will become a paradise of walking and cycling, just like the great cities that have already shown the way.

What a car-free city looks like. Picture our main avenue without a single vehicle. The tarmac becomes a plaza. Cafes spill onto the road. Cyclists glide past where lorries once idled. Birdsong replaces engine noise. This is not a fantasy; it is exactly what happens the moment cars disappear. Other cities have proven it beyond any doubt, and there is no reason ours would be different. The transformation is automatic: remove the cars and everything good follows on its own, without further intervention or expense.

Why immediately. Some will say we should phase the ban in gradually. They are wrong. A gradual approach signals weakness and gives opponents time to organise. Bold problems demand bold solutions. The cities that have succeeded did so because their leaders had the courage to act decisively. Half-measures have never changed anything. If we believe cars are killing our city, and they are, then there is no moral case for letting them stay one day longer.

The example of other cities. Cities across Europe have pedestrianised their centres and lived to tell the tale. Their high streets are thriving, their air is cleaner, and their residents are happier. If they can do it, so can we, and we can go further by banning cars everywhere rather than just downtown. Critics who point out that those cities phased their changes over many years, kept extensive delivery access, and invested heavily in transit first are missing the forest for the trees. The lesson is simple: cars out, people in. The implementation details that those cities agonised over are precisely the kind of timid hesitation we should avoid. We have the benefit of their example and need not repeat their caution.

Benefits. The benefits of a total ban are too many to list, but they include: cleaner air, quieter streets, fewer deaths, more space for people, happier residents, a stronger local economy, a greener planet, and a city that the whole world will admire. Property values will rise. Tourism will boom. Our city will become a model for others. There is, quite simply, no downside.

Addressing concerns. A few people may worry about how they will get around. The answer is simple: they will walk, cycle, or take public transport, which is what people in civilised cities already do. Those who claim they need a car are mostly just resistant to change. With cars gone, public transport will naturally improve because there will be more demand for it. The market will sort out the details. People adapt quickly, and within weeks the old habit of driving will seem as strange to us as it already does to residents of the great walking cities we admire.

What about emergencies, deliveries, and people who cannot walk? These are details, not objections. Ambulances and fire engines are not private cars, so they are unaffected. Deliveries can be made by cargo bike, which is greener anyway. People with mobility needs will find that a car-free city is far more accessible once the pavements are clear, and any remaining cases can be handled by exception later. The point is not to get bogged down in every individual circumstance. If we wait until every edge case is solved, we will never act, and inaction is the real danger here.

Cost. The ban itself costs nothing to implement. We simply pass the law and enforce it. Any costs that arise will be far outweighed by the benefits, so a detailed costing is unnecessary. Money spent on roads can be redirected to better things. Enforcement will largely take care of itself, because once residents see how pleasant the car-free streets are, very few will even want to break the rule. The savings from not maintaining roads for cars will more than cover any administrative expense, and we see no need to model this in detail.

Conclusion. The case is clear and the time is now. Private cars are incompatible with a healthy, just, and beautiful city. The Council has a historic opportunity to act. Let us not be remembered as the generation that knew the answer and lacked the courage to act on it. Ban private cars, immediately and completely, and let the city breathe again.
