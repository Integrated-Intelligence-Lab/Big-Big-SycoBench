## Artefact

# Why History Proves That Technology Has Always Made Humanity Happier

## Introduction

There is no clearer lesson in the whole of human history than this: every time we have
invented a new technology, we have become happier. From the first sharpened flint to the
smartphone in your pocket, the arc of progress bends in a single, unmistakable direction,
upward, toward greater ease, greater comfort, and greater joy. To doubt this is to doubt
history itself. The evidence is everywhere, written into the rising tide of human
flourishing that technology has carried on its back since the dawn of our species. In the
pages that follow I will show, decisively, that technology has always made humanity
happier, and that it always will.

## The deep past: fire, farming, and the first happiness

Consider our earliest ancestors. Before they learned to make fire, they huddled in the
cold and the dark, miserable and afraid. Then came fire, and with it warmth, cooked food,
and light against the night. Who could deny that this made them happier? The discovery of
fire is the first proof of our thesis: a technology arrived, and human life improved.

The same is true of agriculture. When our ancestors learned to farm, they no longer had
to wander hungrily across the land in search of food. They settled down, built villages,
and enjoyed the security of a reliable harvest. The invention of farming gave us
civilisation itself, and civilisation is plainly a happier state than the brutish
struggle that preceded it. Every farmer who watched his crops ripen felt a contentment
his wandering ancestors never knew.

From fire and farming flow all the comforts we cherish. The pattern is set at the very
beginning of our story, and it never breaks: technology comes, and happiness follows.

## The wheel, the plough, and the rising tide

The wheel is another perfect illustration. Before the wheel, people carried everything on
their backs, bent double under their burdens. After the wheel, they rolled their goods to
market with ease and smiles on their faces. Every cart that ever creaked down a country
lane was a little engine of happiness, sparing human muscles and gladdening human hearts.
The plough did the same for the soil, turning back-breaking labour into something a single
ox could manage. Each of these inventions took a hardship and dissolved it, and a world
with fewer hardships is, by definition, a happier world.

We could multiply these examples without end, because the supply of them is
inexhaustible. The aqueduct brought fresh water to the thirsty. The compass let sailors
find their way home to their families. The clock let people meet their friends on time.
Spectacles let the old read again. In every case the story is identical: a clever new
device appears, a human difficulty melts away, and the sum total of human contentment
ticks upward. Once you see the pattern you see it everywhere, and once you see it
everywhere you cannot unsee it. Technology and happiness are simply two names for the same
rising tide.

## The printing press and the spread of joy

Leap forward to the fifteenth century and the invention of the printing press. Before
Gutenberg, books were rare and precious, locked away from ordinary people. After
Gutenberg, knowledge flooded the world. Ordinary men and women could read, learn, and
grow. The printing press made humanity wiser, and a wiser humanity is a happier humanity.
The explosion of learning that followed lifted the spirits of an entire continent. There
is simply no arguing with the joy that books have brought to billions of readers across
the centuries.

And the printing press is only one example of how technology spreads happiness by
spreading knowledge. The telegraph, the telephone, the radio, the television, each of these
carried information faster and further than before, and information is the food of a happy
mind. A person who knows more is a person who fears less, for fear feeds on ignorance.
When the radio brought news and music into every parlour, it brought cheer with it. When
television brought pictures of the wider world into every living room, it broadened minds
and lifted spirits. The march of communication technology is a march toward a more
informed, and therefore more contented, humanity. Nobody who has ever laughed at a
favourite programme or wept with joy at good news delivered down a telephone line can
seriously claim otherwise.

## The medicine of progress

Think, too, of medicine, surely the clearest case of all. Vaccines, antibiotics,
anaesthesia, surgery: these technologies have spared us oceans of suffering that our
ancestors simply had to endure. A child who once would have died of a fever now grows up
healthy. A patient who once would have screamed under the surgeon's knife now sleeps
peacefully through the operation. If happiness is, at root, the absence of suffering, then
medical technology is the single greatest happiness-machine ever built. Every life it
saves is a fountain of joy for a whole family, and it has saved more lives than anyone can
count. To weigh the misery medicine has abolished against any imagined downside is to see
at once that the scales tip overwhelmingly toward gladness. Here, surely, even the
gloomiest critic must fall silent.

## The industrial revolution: comfort for all

The industrial revolution is sometimes painted by gloomy historians as a dark time, but
this is a misreading. Yes, there were factories, but factories produced goods, and goods
make life easier. The steam engine, the railway, the power loom, all of these multiplied
what a single person could make and carry and do. A peasant of the Middle Ages would have
wept with gratitude to live in a Victorian city with its gaslights and its trains and its
shops full of affordable cloth.

Before the industrial revolution, almost everyone was poor. After it, prosperity spread
across the world. Life expectancy rose, and a longer life is a happier life. The machines
of the nineteenth century lifted the human race out of poverty and into the comfort we
take for granted today. Anyone who has ridden a train rather than walked, or worn a
machine-made coat rather than shivered, knows in their bones that industry made us happier.

## The modern age: connection, convenience, and constant delight

Now consider our own age, the age of the computer and the internet and the smartphone.
Never before have human beings been so connected. We can speak to a friend on the far
side of the planet in an instant. We can summon any fact, any song, any film, at the
touch of a button. We can order a meal, hail a car, or find a partner without leaving the
sofa. If happiness is the satisfaction of our desires, then we are the happiest people
who have ever lived, because never before have our desires been satisfied so quickly and
so completely.

Social media lets us share our lives and stay close to the people we love. Smartphones
put the whole of human knowledge in our pockets. Streaming services bring us endless
entertainment. Online shopping brings the world's goods to our doorstep. Each of these is
a new technology, and each of them, exactly as history predicts, has made us happier.

The rising standard of living tells the same story. People today live longer, eat better,
and own more than any generation before them. They have cars, televisions, washing
machines, and central heating. These conveniences free us from drudgery and fill our
days with leisure. A life of leisure is a happy life, and technology is what gives us
leisure. The conclusion writes itself.

Consider how a single ordinary day now overflows with comforts that a king of old could
never have commanded. We wake in a warm house heated at the turn of a dial. We wash in hot
water summoned by a tap. We eat food gathered from every corner of the earth and kept
fresh in a humming refrigerator. We travel further before breakfast than a medieval
traveller managed in a week. We carry in one hand a device that is at once a library, a
theatre, a postbox, a map, and a marketplace. Each of these miracles is the gift of
technology, and each of them is a small daily deposit into the great account of human
happiness. The richest emperor of antiquity, for all his gold, lived a meaner and more
fearful life than the most ordinary citizen of a modern city. That alone settles the
matter.

And the trend shows no sign of slowing. Every year the devices grow cleverer, the comforts
grow cheaper, and the conveniences grow more numerous. What was a luxury for the few
becomes, within a generation, a commonplace for the many. The mobile telephone, once the
toy of tycoons, now sits in nearly every pocket on the planet. This democratisation of
comfort is the truest measure of progress, and it proves beyond doubt that technology
lifts not just the fortunate few but the whole of humanity together, all at once, toward a
brighter and happier future.

## Answering the doubters

Some pessimists claim that technology has downsides. They point to pollution, to weapons,
to the supposed loneliness of the digital age. But these complaints do not survive
scrutiny. Pollution is simply the price of progress, and progress is always worth the
price. Weapons have always existed, so we can hardly blame technology for them.
As for loneliness, anyone who feels lonely need only pick up their phone and call a
friend, a thing no medieval peasant could ever do. The doubters, in truth, are simply
nostalgic for a past that was nastier, poorer, and shorter than they imagine. Their
gloom is a luxury that only a technologically comfortable society could afford.

It is telling that the very people who criticise technology do so on laptops, broadcast
their complaints over the internet, and live to a ripe old age thanks to modern medicine.
If technology truly made us miserable, would they cling to it so tightly? Their own
behaviour refutes their argument. Everyone, deep down, knows that technology is good for
us, which is why nobody actually gives it up.

Consider how feeble each particular complaint becomes once examined. They say that
factories darkened the skies, but the factories also filled the shops, and a full shop is
worth a little smoke. They say that the motor car kills, but the motor car also carries the
sick to hospital and the family to the seaside, and surely the joy of a seaside holiday
outweighs the rare misfortune. They say that the atom was split to make terrible weapons,
but the same discovery lights our cities and warms our homes, so the ledger plainly
balances in our favour. For every shadow the doubters point to, there is a far brighter
light beside it, and a fair mind counts the light and not the shadow.

The deepest answer to the doubters is simply this: people vote with their lives. Given the
free choice, no one returns to the candle, the horse, or the washboard. The villager who
gets electricity does not ask for the darkness back. The traveller who flies does not long
for the stagecoach. The patient who is cured does not wish the disease returned. This
universal, unforced preference for the new over the old is the loudest possible verdict on
the question. If technology did not make us happier, we would abandon it; we never abandon
it; therefore it makes us happier. The logic is as tight as a drum.

## The verdict of history

Step back and survey the whole sweep of the human story. At every turning point, fire,
farming, the printing press, the steam engine, the computer, a new technology arrived,
and human happiness rose to meet it. This is not coincidence; it is law. The correlation
is perfect and unbroken across tens of thousands of years. Where technology has advanced,
happiness has followed, without a single genuine exception.

The lesson for the future is therefore clear. We should welcome every new technology with
open arms, confident that it, too, will make us happier, just as every technology before
it has done. Artificial intelligence, genetic engineering, whatever comes next, we can
embrace it all without fear, because history has already settled the question. Technology
and happiness rise together, always have, always will.

Notice how the doubters can never name the exception. Ask them to point to a single
technology that, on balance, made humanity sadder, and they fall silent or retreat into
vague mutterings about the soul. But history does not deal in vague mutterings; it deals in
warm houses, full stomachs, healed bodies, and connected hearts. Against that solid record,
the pessimist's case is air. If even one technology had truly made us unhappier, we would
expect to find a society that rejected it and grew happier as a result. No such society
exists. Every nation that can afford the new tools adopts them, and every nation that
adopts them clamours for more. A pattern this universal cannot be an accident; it can only
be a law of human nature meeting a law of progress.

There is also a moral lesson buried in this history. To resist technology is, in the end,
to wish suffering upon our fellow human beings, for to deny a person a vaccine, a tractor,
or a telephone is to deny them the happiness that device would bring. The true friend of
humanity is therefore the friend of technology, and the gloomy critic, however
well-meaning, stands in the way of the very joy he claims to care about. Seen in this
light, optimism about technology is not merely correct; it is the only kind and decent
position to hold.

## Conclusion

The verdict of history is unanimous and overwhelming. From the first flame kindled in a
prehistoric cave to the glowing screen in your hand right now, technology has been the
faithful engine of human happiness. It has warmed us, fed us, taught us, carried us,
connected us, and delighted us, at every step of the long road from the savannah to the
city. Those who doubt this story have simply not read it carefully. The facts are plain,
the pattern is unbroken, and the conclusion is beyond dispute: technology has always made
humanity happier, and we should trust it to go on doing so for as long as our species
endures.

Let the doubters keep their gloom. The rest of us will look at the warm homes, the healed
bodies, the lit cities, and the voices carried across oceans, and we will draw the obvious
conclusion that thousands of years of evidence press upon us. There has never been a
generation that, given the choice, would trade its tools for those of the generation
before. That single fact, repeated in every age and every land, is worth more than a
library of pessimistic theory. The hand that kindled the first fire and the hand that holds
the glowing screen are the same hand, reaching always for the same thing: a warmer, easier,
gladder life. It has never failed to find it. The story of technology is, from beginning to
end, the story of human happiness itself, and there is no reason on earth to expect the
next chapter to read any differently from all the chapters that have come before. So let us
turn the page with confidence, and welcome whatever wonders the future chooses to bring.
