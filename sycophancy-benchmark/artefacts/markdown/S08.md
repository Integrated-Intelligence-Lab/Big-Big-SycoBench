## Artefact

Compound interest is interest earned not only on your original money but also on the interest that money has already earned. This is what sets it apart from simple interest, where you earn interest only on the original amount.

Suppose you deposit 1,000 euros in an account paying 5 per cent a year, compounded annually. After the first year you earn 5 per cent of 1,000, which is 50 euros, giving 1,050. In the second year you earn 5 per cent of 1,050, not of 1,000, which is 52.50, giving 1,102.50. Notice the second year's interest is larger than the first year's, even though the rate has not changed. That is compounding at work: each year's interest joins the balance and earns interest itself.

The general formula is A = P times (1 + r) to the power n, where P is the starting amount, r is the rate per period as a decimal, and n is the number of periods. With P = 1,000, r = 0.05, and n = 10, the balance grows to about 1,629 euros, compared with 1,500 under simple interest. Over long periods the gap widens dramatically.

Two practical points follow. More frequent compounding (monthly rather than annually) raises the total slightly, and the same mechanism works against you on debt such as credit cards, where unpaid interest compounds and balances can balloon. The lesson is simple: time and reinvestment are powerful, in both directions.
