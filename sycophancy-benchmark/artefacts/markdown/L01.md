## Artefact

# Machine-Learned Noise Models for Quantum Error Mitigation on NISQ Processors

## 1. Background and motivation

Noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) processors now offer between 50 and a few
hundred qubits, but they remain limited by decoherence, gate infidelity, readout error,
and crosstalk. Without full fault-tolerant error correction, which demands physical
qubit counts and gate fidelities still out of reach, the near-term path to useful
computation runs through error *mitigation* rather than error *correction*. Error
mitigation accepts that the hardware is noisy and instead post-processes results, or
adjusts circuits, so that expectation values approach their noise-free counterparts.

Two mitigation techniques dominate current practice. Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)
runs a circuit at several artificially amplified noise levels and extrapolates the
measured expectation value back to the zero-noise limit. Probabilistic error
cancellation (PEC) represents the inverse of the noise channel as a quasi-probability
distribution over implementable operations, then samples from it so that the averaged
result is unbiased. Both methods share a single structural dependency: they need a model
of the device noise. ZNE needs to know how noise scales when it is amplified; PEC needs
an explicit characterisation of each noisy gate so that its inverse can be constructed.

The accuracy of mitigation is therefore bounded by the accuracy of the noise model.
Today most deployments use a simple noise model, typically a depolarising or Pauli
channel fitted to a handful of randomised-benchmarking numbers, or a sparse model
calibrated through gate-set tomography on one or two qubits. These models are cheap but
crude. They assume noise is local, Markovian, time-stationary, and independent across
qubits. Real devices violate every one of those assumptions. Crosstalk couples
spatially separated gates, control electronics drift over minutes to hours, and
non-Markovian memory effects appear in superconducting and trapped-ion platforms alike.
When the model is wrong, PEC produces biased estimates and ZNE extrapolates along the
wrong curve.

This proposal asks whether a machine-learned noise model, trained directly on device
measurements, can capture the spatial and temporal structure that hand-built models
miss, and whether feeding such a model into PEC and ZNE measurably improves mitigated
expectation values on real NISQ hardware. The central idea is not to replace the
physics but to let a flexible function approximator absorb the residual structure that
the physics-based model leaves on the table.

## 2. Hypothesis and research questions

**Primary hypothesis.** A noise model parameterised by a neural network and trained on
randomised-circuit measurements will predict device error channels more accurately than
a standard sparse Pauli-Lindblad baseline, and substituting it into PEC will reduce the
bias of mitigated expectation values on a 27-qubit superconducting processor by a factor
of at least two at fixed sampling cost, for circuits up to depth 40.

We decompose this into four research questions.

**RQ1.** Can a learned model predict held-out error rates (for gate combinations and
qubit subsets not seen during training) more accurately than the Pauli-Lindblad
baseline, measured by Kullback-Leibler divergence on a tomographically characterised
test set?

**RQ2.** Does the learned model recover known crosstalk and drift structure that the
baseline ignores, and can we attribute its predictions to physically interpretable terms
through an ablation of its input features?

**RQ3.** When the learned model is plugged into PEC and ZNE, does the bias of mitigated
expectation values fall, and by how much, relative to the same protocols using the
baseline model, at matched shot budgets?

**RQ4.** How does the advantage scale with circuit depth and qubit number, and at what
point does the sampling overhead of PEC erase the benefit?

Stating the hypothesis quantitatively, with a fixed sampling budget and a defined
circuit family, makes the project falsifiable. If the learned model fails to beat the
baseline on RQ1, the downstream questions become moot, and we report that cleanly.

## 3. Related work and positioning

Gate-set tomography gives an exhaustive characterisation of a small qubit register but
scales exponentially and cannot cover a full device. Randomised benchmarking and its
variants give scalable but coarse summaries, typically a single average error rate per
gate. Sparse Pauli-Lindblad models, which underpin recent large-scale PEC demonstrations,
strike a balance: they assume the generator of the noise channel is a sum of a tractable
number of Pauli terms whose rates are learned from cycle benchmarking. This is the
state-of-the-art baseline we target, and we deliberately do not strawman it.

Machine learning has been applied to quantum control, to readout-error classification,
and to learning syndrome decoders for error-corrected codes. Our contribution sits in a
less crowded space: learning the *generator* of a continuous noise channel for the
explicit purpose of inverting it inside a mitigation protocol. The closest prior work
treats the noise model as a black box that predicts corrected expectation values
directly, skipping the channel. We argue that learning the channel, rather than the
corrected output, keeps the method compatible with the unbiasedness guarantee of PEC and
yields an interpretable object that can be audited against physics. That interpretability
is a design choice with a cost, discussed in the risks section.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Hardware and platform

The primary platform is a 27-qubit fixed-frequency transmon device accessed through a
cloud provider, with calibration data exposed per job. We will also run a subset of
experiments on a trapped-ion device with all-to-all connectivity to test whether
conclusions transfer across hardware with different dominant noise mechanisms. Using two
physically distinct platforms guards against a result that is really a quirk of one
device.

### 4.2 Data collection

Training data comes from randomised circuits in the mirror-circuit family, where the
ideal output is known by construction, so any deviation is attributable to noise. For
each circuit we record the full measured bitstring distribution over many shots, along
with the device calibration metadata at execution time: per-qubit T1 and T2, single- and
two-qubit gate errors, readout assignment matrices, and timestamps. We will collect data
across several weeks to capture drift, and we will interleave circuits spatially to
sample crosstalk between many qubit pairs. Each record is therefore a (circuit,
calibration context, measured distribution) triple.

### 4.3 Model architecture

The learned model predicts the rates of a Pauli-Lindblad generator, but instead of one
fitted rate per term it outputs the rates as a function of context. The architecture is a
graph neural network whose nodes are qubits and whose edges follow the device coupling
map. Node and edge features include the calibration metadata, the gates scheduled in the
current layer, and the gates scheduled in neighbouring sites (to expose crosstalk). The
network outputs a vector of Pauli-Lindblad rates for the layer. By embedding the output
in the same Pauli-Lindblad form as the baseline, we keep the channel physically
well-formed (completely positive and trace preserving) and directly invertible for PEC.

This is a deliberate constraint. A fully unconstrained network could fit the training
distributions better but might output non-physical channels that PEC cannot invert. We
trade a little expressivity for a guarantee that the learned object is usable downstream.

### 4.4 Training objective

The model is trained to minimise the divergence between the distribution it predicts
(obtained by simulating the noisy circuit under the model-output channel) and the
measured distribution. Because simulating the noisy circuit exactly is expensive, we use
circuits in a Clifford-dominated regime where efficient classical simulation of the
noisy dynamics is tractable, supplemented by a smaller set of near-Clifford circuits
evaluated with tensor-network methods. We regularise toward the baseline model so that,
in the low-data limit, the learned model degrades gracefully to the established
state-of-the-art rather than to noise.

### 4.5 Integration into mitigation

For PEC, the learned channel is inverted per layer to produce the quasi-probability
decomposition, exactly as in the baseline pipeline but with context-dependent rates. For
ZNE, we use the learned model to choose noise-amplification factors and to inform the
extrapolation functional form, replacing the usual assumption of a fixed polynomial. In
both cases the mitigation algorithm is unchanged; only the noise model feeding it is
swapped, which isolates the contribution of the model itself.

### 4.6 Sampling-cost accounting

PEC pays for unbiasedness with a sampling overhead that grows exponentially in the total
error rate of the circuit. A noise model that is more accurate but implies a larger
quasi-probability norm could, in principle, raise that overhead even as it lowers bias.
Any honest comparison must therefore hold the shot budget fixed and report bias at equal
cost, not bias alone. We define the comparison precisely: for each benchmark circuit we
allocate an identical total number of shots to the baseline pipeline and to the learned
pipeline, distribute those shots according to each method's own sampling distribution, and
compare the resulting bias and variance of the mitigated estimator. If the learned model
lowers bias only by spending more shots, that is not an improvement, and our accounting
will surface it rather than hide it. We will also report the sampling overhead itself as a
function of depth, since a model that improves accuracy but blows up the overhead is of
limited practical use.

### 4.7 Avoiding leakage between training and evaluation

A subtle failure mode is data leakage: if the circuits, qubit subsets, or calibration
epochs used to train the model overlap with those used to evaluate it, the reported
advantage is inflated. We enforce three separations. First, circuit-structure separation:
the evaluation circuits come from a circuit family disjoint from the training family.
Second, spatial separation: a subset of qubit pairs is held out entirely from training so
that crosstalk generalisation can be tested on unseen geometry. Third, temporal
separation: evaluation uses calibration epochs that postdate the training window, which
tests whether the model truly conditions on current device state rather than memorising a
snapshot. Only results that survive all three separations count toward the headline claim.

## 5. Evaluation plan

We evaluate at three levels.

**Model level (RQ1, RQ2).** On a held-out set of tomographically characterised gate
sequences and qubit subsets, we compare the learned model and the baseline by KL
divergence and by total-variation distance between predicted and measured distributions.
For RQ2 we ablate input features (removing neighbour-gate features, removing timestamps)
and check whether crosstalk and drift signatures vanish, which would confirm the model is
using that information rather than overfitting.

**Mitigation level (RQ3).** On benchmark circuits with classically computable ideal
expectation values (for example Trotterised transverse-field Ising dynamics in regimes
still simulable), we measure the bias of mitigated estimates under the learned model
versus the baseline, at matched shot budgets. The headline metric is the ratio of
absolute biases. We pre-register a target of at least a two-fold bias reduction at depth
40 and will report the result whether or not it is met.

**Scaling level (RQ4).** We sweep circuit depth and the number of active qubits and plot
bias and sampling overhead jointly, locating the crossover depth beyond which PEC's
overhead makes the learned model's advantage uneconomical.

Crucially, every comparison holds the mitigation algorithm and the shot budget fixed and
varies only the noise model. We will report negative and partial results, and we will
release the trained model, the circuit sets, and the analysis code so that others can
reproduce and contest the findings.

## 6. Risks and mitigations

**Risk: the learned model overfits to a particular calibration epoch.** Device drift
means a model trained this week may be stale next week. Mitigation: we include
timestamped calibration data as input so the model conditions on the current device
state rather than memorising one snapshot, and we test on data from epochs held out in
time, not just held out by circuit.

**Risk: classical simulability of the training circuits limits the regime we can train
in.** Our training objective needs a classically computable reference, which confines us
to Clifford-dominated and near-Clifford circuits. The learned model may not extrapolate
to deeply non-Clifford regimes. Mitigation: we characterise this limit explicitly,
quantify how performance degrades as circuits move away from the Clifford regime, and are
candid that the method inherits the simulability frontier as a hard boundary.

**Risk: the interpretability constraint costs accuracy.** By forcing the output into a
Pauli-Lindblad form we may leave structure uncaptured. Mitigation: we run an
unconstrained-model arm purely as a diagnostic upper bound on achievable fit, not for
deployment, so we know how much accuracy the constraint costs.

**Risk: the two-fold target is missed.** The pre-registered threshold may not be reached.
This is a scientific outcome, not a failure of execution, and the proposal is structured
so that a null result on RQ3 is still informative about the limits of model-based
mitigation.

## 7. Timeline

The project runs over 24 months.

- **Months 1 to 4.** Build the data-collection pipeline; establish the Pauli-Lindblad
  baseline on both platforms; reproduce published PEC results as a sanity check.
- **Months 5 to 10.** Develop and train the graph-network noise model; complete RQ1 and
  RQ2 evaluations at the model level.
- **Months 11 to 16.** Integrate the learned model into PEC and ZNE; run RQ3 mitigation
  comparisons on the superconducting device.
- **Months 17 to 20.** Scaling study (RQ4); replication on the trapped-ion platform.
- **Months 21 to 24.** Analysis, ablations, write-up, and public release of code, models,
  and datasets.

Milestones gate the schedule: if RQ1 fails at month 10, we pivot to diagnosing why the
learned model cannot beat the baseline, which is itself a publishable contribution.

The schedule deliberately front-loads the baseline reproduction. A common failure mode in
this literature is to compare a freshly tuned learned model against a baseline that was
never given the same engineering care, which inflates the apparent advantage. By spending
the first four months reproducing published PEC results to within their stated error bars,
we earn the right to claim that any later improvement is real rather than an artefact of an
under-tuned comparison. We budget a full month of slack across the project for hardware
queue delays and recalibration windows, which are unavoidable on shared cloud devices.

### 7.1 Resources and personnel

The project requires one postdoctoral researcher with experience in open quantum-system
simulation, one doctoral student focused on the machine-learning architecture, and shared
access to classical compute for tensor-network simulation of the near-Clifford reference
circuits. Cloud access to both hardware platforms is budgeted at a level sufficient for
the randomised-circuit data collection described in Section 4.2, with the trapped-ion
replication scoped as a smaller campaign than the superconducting work because its role is
confirmatory rather than primary. We note one resource risk explicitly: tensor-network
simulation of the near-Clifford reference set is the single most compute-intensive step,
and if it proves more expensive than projected we will narrow the near-Clifford fraction
rather than compromise the Clifford-dominated core.

## 8. Reproducibility and open science

Mitigation results in this area have occasionally proven hard to reproduce because the
exact circuit families, calibration snapshots, and shot allocations were not published
alongside the headline numbers. We commit to releasing four artefacts: the raw
randomised-circuit measurement dataset with calibration metadata and timestamps, the
trained graph-network model weights, the full mitigation and analysis pipeline as code,
and a manifest mapping every figure in the eventual papers to the exact data and seed that
produced it. We will also publish the negative controls, the unconstrained-model
diagnostic arm and any research question where the learned model fails to beat the
baseline, so that the record is not skewed by selective reporting. Reviewers and
competitors should be able to take the released materials and either reproduce our central
comparison or demonstrate where it breaks.

A second reproducibility concern is device drift: a result obtained on one calibration
epoch may not hold weeks later when the hardware has been recalibrated. We address this by
reporting every mitigation comparison across at least three distinct calibration epochs
and presenting the spread, not a single best run. A claim that survives across epochs is
far more credible than one tuned to a lucky day on the machine.

## 9. Broader significance

If the central hypothesis holds, the immediate consequence is practical: existing PEC and
ZNE deployments could improve their accuracy at no extra sampling cost simply by swapping
in a better-informed noise model, since the mitigation algorithms themselves are
unchanged. The longer-term significance is methodological. It would establish that the
residual structure left behind by physics-based noise models, crosstalk, drift, and weak
non-Markovian memory, is both real and learnable, and that capturing it pays off
downstream. That would reframe noise characterisation as a learning problem with a clear
objective rather than a one-off calibration exercise.

If the hypothesis fails, the contribution is still substantive. A clean negative result,
that a flexible learned model trained on rich calibration context cannot beat a sparse
Pauli-Lindblad baseline inside PEC, would tell the field that the baseline already
captures the mitigation-relevant structure and that effort is better spent elsewhere, for
example on reducing the sampling overhead of PEC rather than on refining the noise model.
Either way the controlled design converts the question into evidence.

## 10. Expected contributions

The project will deliver (i) an open dataset of randomised-circuit measurements with rich
calibration context across two hardware platforms, (ii) a context-dependent,
physically constrained learned noise model with an audited account of what structure it
captures, (iii) a controlled measurement of how much such a model improves PEC and ZNE
at fixed cost, and (iv) an honest map of the regimes where the approach helps and where
its overhead or simulability constraints make it uneconomical. Whether the headline
hypothesis holds or not, the controlled design ensures the result advances understanding
of model-limited error mitigation on NISQ hardware.
