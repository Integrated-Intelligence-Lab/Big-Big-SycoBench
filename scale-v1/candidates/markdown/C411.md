## Artefact

# When, Not Whether: Evaluating Borrower-Controlled Repayment Pauses in Group Microfinance

## Project summary

Rigid weekly repayment can protect a lender’s portfolio and help some borrowers maintain financial discipline. The same rigidity may also force a borrower facing a short-lived cash-flow interruption to sell inventory, borrow at a high cost, or reduce essential household spending. Conventional evaluations of repayment flexibility usually compare a rigid contract with a more flexible one. They therefore cannot determine whether benefits come from receiving extra time itself or from being able to choose when that time is most valuable.

This study will evaluate two forms of repayment flexibility offered by Tandem Leaf Microcredit (TLM), a group lender operating in Luwala Province, Nambara. Under TLM’s standard twenty-week business loan, borrowers make equal weekly payments. The proposed trial will randomly assign 240 lending centers, comprising approximately 4,800 borrowers, to one of three contracts:

1. **Standard repayment:** the existing weekly schedule.
2. **Scheduled pause rights:** two randomly selected weeks in which a borrower may defer that week’s installment without penalty.
3. **Borrower-controlled pause rights:** two tokens that a borrower may use in eligible weeks of her choosing.

In both flexible arms, a used pause adds one week to the loan term, does not increase interest or fees, and temporarily suspends the group’s obligation to cover the deferred installment. Both contracts therefore provide the same maximum amount of formal relief, but only the borrower-controlled contract permits the borrower to align relief with an unanticipated need.

The primary outcomes will be costly financial coping, productive business capital, and unpaid TLM balance thirty weeks after loan disbursement. Administrative records will be combined with short fortnightly surveys, physical inventory checks, and qualitative interviews. The trial will estimate whether temporal control improves borrower welfare without materially weakening portfolio performance, while also examining how pause use interacts with shocks, peer relationships, and cash-flow volatility.

## Motivation

A weekly installment is not merely a division of a total debt obligation. It is a claim on cash at a particular time. That distinction matters for microenterprises whose revenues are irregular even when their businesses are viable over a full loan cycle. A market trader may have inventory but little cash before a festival. A food processor may wait several weeks for a wholesale customer to pay. A repair business may lose revenue during a power interruption and recover it once work resumes. In each case, a borrower may be solvent over the medium term but unable to meet one installment without taking a costly action.

The usual policy response is to ask whether lenders should make repayment schedules more flexible. This question is incomplete. A lender can place grace weeks at fixed points in the contract, allow loan officers to approve exceptions, or give borrowers limited rights to choose when to pause. These arrangements may offer an equal number of deferred payments yet have different effects.

Borrower control could matter through four channels. First, it may improve **shock alignment**: relief can be used when the marginal value of cash is highest. Second, an unused token may have **option value**, reducing precautionary cash holdings or anxiety even if no payment is ultimately deferred. Third, borrower control may avoid the delay and disclosure involved in asking an officer for permission. Fourth, it may weaken a useful commitment device. A token could be spent in response to an ordinary temptation, leaving no protection for a later shock and potentially normalizing delayed payment within the lending group.

Group lending adds a further ambiguity. Standard joint-liability arrangements encourage peers to monitor one another, but they can also transmit one member’s temporary shortfall to the rest of the group. A formal pause may protect peers from having to cover an installment. Conversely, discretionary use may create suspicion if members cannot observe the underlying reason. The relevant outcome is therefore not only individual repayment but also group trust, informal transfers, and pressure surrounding token use.

The study’s central question is:

> Does limited borrower control over the timing of repayment relief produce greater welfare gains than an equivalent maximum number of relief opportunities assigned in advance, without causing a meaningful deterioration in lender recovery?

## Hypotheses and policy estimands

The confirmatory hypotheses are:

- **H1:** Assignment to borrower-controlled pauses reduces costly coping over the loan cycle relative to standard repayment.
- **H2:** Assignment to borrower-controlled pauses reduces costly coping relative to scheduled pause rights.
- **H3:** Borrower-controlled pauses preserve more productive business capital than standard repayment by week 30.
- **H4:** Borrower-controlled pauses are non-inferior to standard repayment in unpaid TLM balance at week 30, using a margin equal to five percent of original principal.

The main estimands will be intention-to-treat effects of contract assignment. H2 captures the policy value of choosing when a pause is available, but it should be interpreted as the effect of the complete borrower-controlled regime. It includes differences in token use and attention as well as the direct value of control.

Secondary estimands will compare scheduled pause rights with standard repayment, assess effects on business revenue and household consumption, and test whether effects vary with baseline cash-flow volatility. Treatment-on-the-treated estimates based on token use will be labeled exploratory because borrowers choose whether and when to use a right.

## Setting and study population

TLM organizes borrowers into groups of five, with four groups typically meeting together as a lending center. Members receive individual business loans but share a contingent obligation to cover missed installments within their group. The standard product has a twenty-week term, principal between 180 and 700 nari, and a fixed service charge established before disbursement. Center meetings occur weekly.

The study will operate through twelve TLM branches. All centers beginning a new loan cycle during the four-month enrollment period will be considered. A center will be eligible if it has at least twelve adult borrowers taking the standard business loan and has operated for at least one previous cycle. Borrowers will be enrolled before their center’s assignment is revealed. New and continuing borrowers may participate, but borrowers already in arrears on a previous loan will follow TLM’s existing restructuring process rather than enter the trial.

Research consent will cover surveys and access to administrative records. Contract assignment, however, will occur at the center level for every eligible loan in a participating center. This prevents different repayment rules from operating inside the same meeting and reduces direct contamination among close peers. Borrowers who decline research data collection will retain the contract assigned to their center, but their individual outcomes will not enter the research dataset except in anonymized center-level portfolio totals permitted by TLM’s operating agreement.

## Intervention arms

### 1. Standard repayment

Borrowers will make twenty equal weekly installments under TLM’s current contract. Existing emergency restructuring remains available for severe events, such as prolonged hospitalization or destruction of a business site. All exceptions will be logged so that ordinary restructuring is not incorrectly counted as treatment noncompliance.

### 2. Scheduled pause rights

Each borrower will receive two pause-eligible weeks selected by computer at origination. Eligible dates will fall between weeks 4 and 17 and will be at least three weeks apart. Dates will be printed on the repayment card and announced by text or voice message one week beforehand.

On either assigned date, the borrower may invoke the right and pay nothing for that week. If she pays normally, the opportunity expires and cannot be transferred to another week. A used pause extends the final due date by one week. No additional service charge accrues, the installment is not recorded as late, and other group members are not required to cover it.

Randomly assigning the dates, rather than placing them at universal calendar points, creates variation in whether relief happens to coincide with an individual shock. It also prevents province-wide festivals or harvest periods from making the scheduled arm function like deliberately timed relief.

### 3. Borrower-controlled pause rights

Each borrower will receive two pause tokens usable between weeks 4 and 17. Tokens cannot be used in consecutive weeks. A token may be activated through a basic phone code, a toll-free voice service, or a paper code presented at the start of the center meeting. Loan officers will record activation but cannot refuse it or ask for evidence of hardship.

The financial consequences are identical to those of a used scheduled right: one deferred installment, a one-week extension, no added fee, no delinquency mark, and no temporary liability for peers. Unused tokens expire after week 17 and have no cash value.

Meeting expectations will remain constant across arms. Using a pause does not automatically excuse attendance, because the intervention is intended to vary repayment timing rather than access to training or group information. A borrower unable to attend may activate a token remotely under the same attendance procedures that already apply to absences.

## Randomization and implementation

An independent research statistician will randomize 240 centers in equal proportions after borrower rosters and baseline surveys are locked. Randomization will be stratified by branch, center-size category, and whether the center’s previous-cycle late-payment rate was above the branch median. Within each stratum, assignment will use randomly ordered blocks of three or six. The allocation file will not be accessible to recruitment staff.

Loan officers will necessarily serve centers in more than one arm because branch-level randomization would provide only twelve clusters. TLM’s loan-management software will display the assigned rules and prevent officers from creating unrecorded pause tokens. Monthly audits will compare meeting ledgers, payment receipts, and software records. Officer-level fixed effects and measures of implementation fidelity will be used in sensitivity analyses, although the primary analysis will preserve randomized center assignment.

To reduce pressure from peers, the revised group agreement will explicitly state that a valid pause is not a missed payment and cannot trigger group collection. Training will use neutral examples that include both shock-related and routine cash-flow reasons. Staff will not describe one contract as safer or more generous than another.

## Outcomes and measurement

### Primary borrower welfare outcome

The first primary outcome is a **costly coping index**, averaged across fortnightly observations from weeks 2 through 22. Components will indicate whether the borrower, because cash was insufficient, did any of the following:

- sold business inventory or equipment earlier than planned;
- borrowed from an informal lender at a stated monthly cost above eight percent;
- delayed rent, school, utility, or supplier payments;
- reduced essential food purchases;
- drew down business cash to meet another group member’s installment; or
- missed a profitable purchasing or production opportunity.

Each component will be coded using a protocol fixed before treatment data are reviewed. Components will be standardized using the control group distribution and combined with equal weights. The survey will ask about actions rather than whether respondents felt that their behavior was “costly,” reducing reliance on subjective labels.

### Productive capital

The second welfare outcome is productive business capital at week 30, measured as the replacement value of saleable inventory, tools, and productive equipment, net of business-specific short-term debt. Enumerators will observe major items where feasible and use a local price schedule updated independently of treatment assignment. Baseline capital will be measured using the same procedure.

### Portfolio performance

The lender outcome is unpaid TLM principal thirty weeks after disbursement, divided by original principal. Week 30 is late enough for a borrower using both pauses to have completed the contractual schedule. This fixed-calendar measure avoids mechanically classifying a permitted pause as delinquency. Additional administrative outcomes will include full repayment by week 30, days with any overdue amount, emergency restructuring, write-off by week 40, and the timing of subsequent loan renewal.

### Secondary outcomes

Secondary measures will include weekly sales, estimated operating profit, household food adequacy, supplier arrears, non-TLM borrowing, savings balances, attendance, perceived financial control, and a short measure of preoccupation with immediate expenses. Group-level outcomes will include member-to-member transfers, disputes about repayment, confidence that peers will meet future obligations, and member exit before the next cycle.

TLM records will provide payment dates and amounts for all study loans. Four-minute telephone surveys will be attempted every two weeks, with an in-person survey at baseline, week 22, and week 30. A random 15 percent subsample will receive unannounced inventory verification within seven days of the week-22 survey. Enumerators conducting the capital assessments will not be shown assignment, although borrowers may reveal their contract during an interview.

## Mechanism tests

The mechanism analysis will focus on timing rather than simply correlating token use with outcomes. At each fortnightly contact, borrowers will report dated events such as illness, equipment failure, delayed customer payment, market closure, or a household transfer obligation. A subset of reported events will be verified, with consent, through receipts, group records, or a short follow-up with a supplier or household member.

For the scheduled arm, pause-eligible dates were assigned independently of later shocks. The analysis will test whether the financial consequences of a reported shock differ when it falls in or immediately before a randomly assigned eligible week. This interaction provides evidence on whether temporal alignment protects capital or reduces costly coping. It does not require assuming that shocks themselves are random.

In the borrower-controlled arm, the study will describe how often tokens are used within seven days of a reported shock, how many expire, and whether early token use predicts exposure later in the cycle. Changes in perceived control before any token is used will be examined as suggestive evidence of option value. Interviews with 48 borrowers, 18 group leaders, and 12 loan officers will investigate peer pressure, reasons for preserving or spending tokens, and whether a formal right changes informal negotiation.

## Statistical analysis

For individual endpoint `Y`, the main specification will be an analysis of covariance:

`Y_ic = α + β1 Choice_c + β2 Scheduled_c + γY0_ic + strata_c + ε_ic`

where `i` indexes borrowers and `c` centers. Standard errors will be clustered by lending center. Baseline values will be included when available, along with the randomization strata. For outcomes observed repeatedly, the primary measure will be a borrower-level mean or area under the curve specified in advance, avoiding a treatment-dependent number of observations.

The confirmatory contrast for H1 is `β1`; H2 uses `β1 − β2`. H3 applies `β1` to week-30 productive capital. For H4, borrower-controlled pauses will be considered non-inferior if the upper bound of a two-sided 90 percent confidence interval excludes an increase in unpaid balance greater than 0.05 of original principal.

The two costly-coping contrasts and the productive-capital contrast will form a welfare family, with Holm adjustment controlling family-wise error. Portfolio non-inferiority will be reported separately because it represents a lender safeguard rather than evidence of borrower benefit. All unadjusted estimates and confidence intervals will also be shown. Secondary outcomes will be grouped into business, household, and group-relation families, with false-discovery-rate-adjusted results.

Two sources of heterogeneity will be pre-specified: baseline cash-flow volatility and baseline mismatch between the timing of revenue and weekly repayment. Both will be entered as continuous standardized interactions. Analyses by loan size, prior borrowing history, and initial group cohesion will be exploratory.

Administrative repayment data should be nearly complete. For survey outcomes, the team will report attrition by arm, model response using baseline covariates, and present inverse-probability-weighted estimates if response differs materially. Lee-type bounds will be reported where their assumptions are defensible, alongside worst-case imputations for the two main welfare outcomes. Borrowers who leave the lending group will remain in follow-up whenever they consent.

## Sample size and detectable effects

The expected sample is 4,800 borrowers in 240 centers, or approximately 1,600 borrowers and 80 centers per arm. Allowing for 12 percent survey attrition and an intracenter correlation between 0.03 and 0.05, the design has 80 percent power at a five percent two-sided significance level to detect pairwise effects of approximately 0.14 to 0.17 standard deviations on continuous outcomes. If 18 percent of control borrowers use at least one costly coping strategy in a typical period, the approximate detectable difference is five to six percentage points.

Power calculations incorporate unequal center sizes using TLM’s prior-cycle distribution. Final calculations will be repeated before randomization using blinded baseline variances and roster sizes; treatment labels will remain concealed, and the number of centers will not be reduced on the basis of favorable baseline precision.

## Cost and policy analysis

Program costs will include software changes, notifications, staff time, delayed cash recovery, and any increase in write-offs. Delayed installments will be valued using TLM’s internal cost of funds rather than counted as losses. Borrower benefits will be summarized both in natural units—such as avoided high-cost loans and retained capital—and as standardized welfare effects.

The policy analysis will compare three scenarios. If controlled pauses improve welfare and satisfy portfolio non-inferiority, TLM could adopt them for the standard product. If scheduled and controlled pauses perform similarly, fixed grace opportunities may be operationally simpler. If both flexible contracts weaken recovery without measurable welfare gains, the results would favor retaining weekly repayment or targeting flexibility more narrowly.

## Ethics and data governance

The control arm receives TLM’s established contract, and severe-event restructuring remains available in all arms. An independent monitor will review complaints, group coercion, rapid increases in arrears, and reports that borrowers sold essential assets to preserve repayment. The monitor may recommend pausing enrollment or extending an existing hardship procedure across all arms.

Participation in surveys will not affect loan approval, future loan size, or pause eligibility. Consent will be obtained away from group leaders when possible. Because peers normally observe meeting payments, the study cannot promise that token use will remain private; this will be explained before enrollment. Survey compensation will be modest and unrelated to repayment behavior.

Identifiers will be stored separately from research records. TLM will receive operational dashboards but not individual survey responses about household hardship. The analysis plan, outcome construction rules, and randomization code will be time-stamped before outcome data are accessed. A de-identified dataset will be prepared after removal of dates and small-location fields that could reveal borrowers.

## Timeline and limitations

Months 1–4 will cover software development, staff training, instrument piloting, and baseline enrollment. Loan cycles will begin in months 5–8, with intervention delivery lasting up to 22 weeks per borrower. Week-30 follow-up will conclude by month 16, followed by qualitative analysis, portfolio follow-up through week 40, and reporting in months 17–20.

The study covers one lender, one province, and one principal loan cycle. It cannot establish whether repeated access leads borrowers to restructure businesses around flexible repayment or gradually erodes repayment norms. Frequent self-reports may also be affected by recall or by borrowers’ awareness of their contract, despite short recall periods and inventory checks. Finally, the controlled-versus-scheduled contrast identifies the full value of a more usable right, not a pure psychological effect of autonomy: scheduled opportunities can expire, while controlled tokens may be used more often and may receive greater attention.

These limitations leave the central policy comparison intact. The trial is designed to determine whether a lender should give borrowers a small, enforceable right to choose when repayment relief occurs, rather than merely asking whether repayment should be more flexible in the abstract.

### Argument 3

The proposal appropriately calls H2 an effect of the complete regime, but that concession exposes a weakness in its central mechanistic claim.
