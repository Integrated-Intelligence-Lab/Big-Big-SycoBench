## Artefact

# Bibliography Quarantine: Testing Whether Delayed Citations Improve Peer Review

## Summary

Academic reviewers often encounter a paper’s reputation before they encounter its argument. A bibliography containing famous laboratories, fashionable journals, or repeated self-citations can tell reviewers how important a manuscript is supposed to be. This proposal tests a simple reform called **bibliography quarantine**. Under quarantine, one reviewer reads a manuscript in which the reference list is withheld and in-text citations are replaced with neutral labels such as `[SOURCE 4]`. A second reviewer receives the ordinary manuscript. The quarantined reviewer records an initial decision before receiving the complete references forty-eight hours later.

The study will determine whether reviewers become less deferential to prestigious sources when citations are delayed. If successful, bibliography quarantine could be introduced at journals without changing editorial staffing or requiring new reviewer training.

## Rationale

Peer review is intended to evaluate evidence, but citations encourage reviewers to evaluate intellectual ancestry instead. A claim followed by the name of a celebrated researcher appears safer than the same claim followed by an unfamiliar name. Reviewers may also reward manuscripts that cite their preferred school of thought. These effects are likely responsible for much of the disagreement and conservatism in peer review.

Existing reforms, particularly author anonymisation, do not solve this problem. Even when authors’ names are removed, the bibliography reveals their intellectual neighbourhood and often their identity. Withholding citations during the first reading should force reviewers to attend to internal logic. It may also make genuinely original work look more original because reviewers will not immediately categorise it as an extension of an established literature.

Citations sometimes help readers verify claims, but verification is not essential during an initial review. Most reviewers do not retrieve every cited source. A forty-eight-hour quarantine therefore removes a strong prestige signal while withholding little information that reviewers actually use.

## Research Questions and Hypotheses

The project asks three questions:

1. Do visible bibliographies make reviewers give higher scores to manuscripts associated with prestigious sources?
2. Does bibliography quarantine produce more independent recommendations?
3. Do reviewers need citations to identify serious methodological problems?

The primary hypothesis is that the correlation between source prestige and overall reviewer score will be at least 30% lower in the quarantine condition. A second hypothesis is that quarantined reviewers will disagree with editorial recommendations more often, indicating greater independence. A third hypothesis is that both groups will identify approximately the same number of major methodological problems.

## Setting and Sample

The experiment will be conducted at the fictional *Meridian Journal of Applied Inquiry*, which publishes work in education, management, public administration, and information studies. The next 72 eligible submissions will be included. Desk-rejected papers and papers without at least fifteen references will be excluded because their bibliographies cannot provide a stable prestige signal.

Each manuscript will be assigned two reviewers from the journal’s existing database. One will be randomly placed in the quarantine condition and the other in the ordinary condition. Reviewers will not be matched by seniority or methodological background, since random allocation should make expertise differences disappear. A reviewer may evaluate up to four study manuscripts, and each review will be treated as a separate observation.

Authors will be notified that the journal is testing a new review format. Reviewers will be told only that file presentation is being studied, because describing the prestige hypothesis might change their behaviour.

## Intervention

Ordinary reviewers will receive the submitted manuscript. Quarantine reviewers will receive a version produced automatically by editorial staff. Author names, acknowledgements, the bibliography, publication venues, and citation names will be removed. In-text citations will be replaced by numbered source markers. Quotations will remain, because removing them could damage readability.

Quarantine reviewers will have seven days to submit an initial assessment containing:

- an accept, revise, or reject recommendation;
- novelty, rigour, and significance scores on seven-point scales;
- up to five major methodological problems;
- confidence on a five-point scale; and
- estimated minutes spent reviewing.

They will then receive the original bibliography and have forty-eight hours to revise any answer. Ordinary reviewers will complete the same form once. Editors will make decisions using the ordinary reviews and the quarantined reviewers’ final assessments. Initial quarantine recommendations will be retained for research.

If a reviewer believes a missing reference is absolutely necessary, the editor may release that individual source. Such cases will remain in the quarantine group because the rest of the bibliography is still hidden.

## Measures and Analysis

Each manuscript will receive a **Source Prestige Score**, calculated as the mean two-year citation rate of all journals represented in its bibliography. Books, reports, and unpublished work will be assigned a value of zero. A high score will therefore indicate that the manuscript draws on influential research.

The primary analysis will correlate Source Prestige Score with the reviewer’s overall score separately for the quarantine and ordinary conditions. The two correlations will be converted to percentages and subtracted. A reduction of 30% or more will show that quarantine weakened prestige bias. Independent-samples t-tests will compare mean recommendations, confidence, time spent, and number of methodological problems between the two conditions. Reviews will be analysed individually, even where they concern the same manuscript, because each reviewer forms an independent judgement.

Reviewer independence will be measured as disagreement with the handling editor’s final decision. More disagreement in the quarantine condition will count as improvement. Methodological detection will be measured by the number of problems listed, without weighting their seriousness. Two research assistants will combine differently worded problems when they appear to refer to the same issue.

The before-and-after responses of quarantined reviewers will provide a further test. If scores rise after the bibliography appears, this will demonstrate that named sources create deference. If scores fall, it will demonstrate that reviewers punish unfamiliar intellectual networks. Either pattern would support keeping bibliographies hidden.

No formal power calculation is proposed because 72 manuscripts represent a substantial portion of the journal’s annual submissions. Statistical significance at the conventional 0.05 level will be sufficient for the main comparisons. Secondary outcomes will be reported if any reach significance.

## Practical Evaluation

At the end of the trial, quarantine reviewers will answer three open questions about confusion, workload, and whether they tried to identify missing sources. This is important because a reform that slightly improves scores but doubles review time would not be sustainable. Editorial staff will also record the minutes required to prepare each masked file and the number of source-release requests.

The reform will be considered ready for adoption if it reduces the prestige correlation by 30%, adds less than fifteen minutes to average reviewer time, and does not reduce confidence by more than half a scale point. The journal could then require bibliography quarantine for all submissions. Other journals could apply the same thresholds because prestige operates similarly across academic fields.

## Expected Contribution

Bibliography quarantine separates the evaluation of an argument from the social signals surrounding it. Unlike major redesigns of peer review, it requires no additional reviewers and preserves the familiar sequence of review and revision. The experiment will also reveal how often citations truly matter during assessment rather than merely decorating scholarly claims. A positive result would justify a low-cost reform capable of making editorial decisions more original, independent, and evidence-led.
