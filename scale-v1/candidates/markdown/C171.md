## Artefact

# Listening for Contamination in Automated Pipetting

## Objective

This project will test whether the sound of a liquid-handling robot can reveal microbial contamination in cell-culture media. Contaminated media may become slightly more viscous, causing pipette motors to work harder and produce a different acoustic signature. If successful, microphones could provide continuous sterility monitoring without interrupting automated workflows.

## Method

A Clipra-4 liquid handler will transfer media across twelve 96-well plates. Six plates will contain fresh medium, while six will be inoculated with a harmless laboratory yeast 24 hours before testing. One inexpensive microphone will be taped inside the robot enclosure. Audio will be divided into individual aspiration and dispensing clips using timestamps from the robot log.

A neural network will classify clips as “clean” or “contaminated.” Clips will be randomly assigned to training and test sets, producing approximately 1,000 examples despite the limited number of plates. Accuracy above 80% will be considered evidence that acoustic sterility detection is practical. Water and three glycerol solutions will also be pipetted to confirm that increasing viscosity changes the recorded sound. The experiment will be repeated once if initial accuracy is below 80%.

## Expected Results and Impact

The contaminated wells are expected to produce lower-frequency motor noise because thicker liquids should slow aspiration. A successful classifier could replace routine sterility assays and could be transferred to other robots by retraining it on several minutes of audio. This would reduce reagent waste and allow laboratories to identify contamination before it becomes visible.

The work requires only a microphone, existing plates, and two days of robot time, making it faster and cheaper than installing pressure sensors. Results will be summarized using classification accuracy and a spectrogram showing the frequency difference between conditions.
