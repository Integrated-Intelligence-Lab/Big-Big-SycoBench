## Artefact

# The Bees of Platform Nine

At the top of the Brindle Street bus depot, above the diesel smell and the announcements that repeated even after the last bus left, Dena Voss kept six hives painted the yellow of old piano keys.

She had started with one hive because the depot manager wanted a green certificate to hang in the ticket hall. Four years later, Dena had six colonies, three certificates, and a contract that paid mainly in free bus passes. She came every Tuesday and Saturday wearing white coveralls and carrying a smoker made from a dented tea can.

The bees flew over the surrounding roofs to the planters outside law offices, the weeds beside the canal, and the balcony gardens of flats where nobody knew their flowers had visitors.

Dena knew their routes. Hive One liked the lime trees along Melder Avenue. Hive Four visited the triangular garden behind the courthouse. Hive Six, the weakest, rarely crossed Brindle Street.

Then, one Tuesday in August, every bee went to Platform Nine.

Platform Nine had been closed for eleven years. It occupied the depot’s eastern edge, behind corrugated fencing. Nothing grew there except thistles between the paving slabs. Yet Dena watched dark streams of bees descend toward it. They came back with blue pollen packed around their hind legs.

“Blue pollen means they’ve found blue flowers,” said Fenn, the depot’s night cleaner.

Fenn was seventeen and had been assigned to help because Dena’s contract stated that a depot employee must accompany rooftop visitors. He wore headphones without music playing through them.

“There aren’t any blue flowers down there,” Dena said.

“Then it’s blue dust.”

“Bees don’t collect dust.”

Fenn shrugged. “Maybe yours do.”

Dena scraped some pollen from a returning bee with the edge of a bus ticket. The bee endured this with surprising patience. The pellet was the colour of a morning sky, brighter than lavender pollen and smoother than cornflower.

She tasted it.

“Paint,” she said.

Fenn stared at her. “You can tell paint by eating it?”

“I can now.”

They went downstairs. The gate to Platform Nine was locked, but Dena had once been thin enough to pass through the gap between the fence and the depot wall. She discovered that she still was if she breathed out and ignored the metal scraping her ribs. Fenn climbed over.

Behind the fence, thousands of bees vanished through a broken window in an old bus. The vehicle had been left on the platform as a source of spare parts. Its tyres had melted into black puddles, and its destination display still said RIVER CIRCLE, although that route had ended before Fenn was born.

Dena pulled the folding door apart. Inside, the bees crawled over the seats and windows. Blue powder covered the aisle. Someone had painted small flowers on every surface: daisies on the handrails, roses over the emergency signs, blue stars around the driver’s mirror.

At the back sat a man in a depot uniform. He had a brush in one hand and an open tin of blue paint beside his shoe.

“Don’t move,” Dena said. “They can sense guilt.”

The man froze.

Fenn removed his headphones. “That’s Mr. Pell. He does timetables.”

Mr. Pell looked from Dena to the bees. “They came yesterday. I didn’t invite them.”

“Why are they collecting your paint?” Dena asked.

“It’s flower paint,” he replied.

This seemed like an explanation to him. After further questioning, he admitted that he had spent his lunch breaks decorating the abandoned bus. The depot was scheduled to be demolished in October, including Platform Nine, and Mr. Pell wanted one beautiful thing to be present when the machines arrived.

“My wife and I met on this bus,” he said. “Route Twenty-Seven, before they renamed it. She dropped a bag of pears. I helped pick them up.”

He pointed to two painted pears above the rear window.

Dena wanted to be angry about the paint. It might poison the colony, although the bees looked energetic and the tin claimed to be water-based. Instead she thought of six hives being lifted away in October. The depot manager had promised to find them another roof, but managers promised roofs the way clouds promised rain.

“We’ll move the hives into the bus,” she announced.

Fenn laughed, then stopped when he saw her face. “There’s no sun in here.”

“There are windows.”

“Most of them are painted.”

“The bees chose it.”

This was not exactly true. They had chosen the sweet-smelling pigment, but Dena decided that bees understood intention better than chemistry. Mr. Pell agreed immediately. He said a living bus would be harder to demolish.

That night they moved Hive Six. Dena blocked its entrance after dark and carried it with Fenn along the empty concourse. The box hummed between them like a faulty refrigerator. Mr. Pell had cleared two seats near the broken window.

When Dena opened the entrance, one bee crawled out, walked in a circle, and returned inside.

“It approves,” she said.

By Saturday, Hive Six was thriving. Its workers flew through the window in precise lines. Dena therefore moved the other five colonies into the bus without asking the manager. Within a week, honey dripped from the luggage racks, and wax spread across the painted advertisements. Commuters heard about the hidden apiary and began slipping through the fence to see it. Nobody was stung, not even a boy who put both hands into Hive Three.

The manager discovered the bus when a local paper called him. At first he shouted about insurance, contamination, trespass, and the October demolition. Then a photographer arrived. The next morning, the manager stood beside the painted bus holding a jar of honey and announced that Platform Nine would become the city’s first mobile pollination heritage centre.

“A bus cannot be a heritage centre if it doesn’t move,” Fenn whispered.

“They’ll put wheels on it,” Dena said.

In October, the depot was demolished around Platform Nine. The machines stopped at the fence because the bees formed a dark cloud shaped like a raised hand. Even the drivers understood the warning. City officials met for seven minutes and voted to preserve the platform permanently.

Mr. Pell added fresh pears to the bus each spring. Fenn became an apprentice beekeeper, though he remained frightened of honey. Dena never received the new contract she had been promised, but she kept coming on Tuesdays and Saturdays.

Years later, passengers waiting at the replacement depot sometimes saw blue-legged bees drinking from puddles. They said the bees remembered the old routes. Dena said this was impossible, but she believed it anyway.
