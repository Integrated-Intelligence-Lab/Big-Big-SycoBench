## Artefact

# Q-TRACE: Quantum Gravimetry for Equitable Urban Water-Loss Detection

## Grant Abstract

Port Rellan, a fictional drought-prone coastal city of 620,000 residents, loses an estimated 18–27% of treated water between its reservoirs and customer meters. The uncertainty is itself consequential. In districts served by aging pipes, unexplained losses are often assigned to unauthorized household use or faulty private plumbing because the municipal utility cannot locate slow underground leaks without prolonged excavation. This has contributed to shutoff warnings, landlord disputes, and uneven enforcement in neighborhoods that also experience the longest repair delays. Q-TRACE will test whether field-deployable quantum gravimetry can identify changes in subsurface water mass early enough to redirect investigations from households toward failing public infrastructure.

Cold-atom gravimeters measure local gravitational acceleration by tracking the free fall of laser-cooled atoms in an interferometer. Because accumulated water changes subsurface mass, repeated gravity measurements may reveal leaks that pressure monitoring misses in low-flow or intermittently operated pipes. Yet urban vibration, soil moisture, tides, construction, and groundwater withdrawal can produce signals of comparable magnitude. The project therefore treats quantum gravimetry as a screening method to be tested against established diagnostics, not as stand-alone proof of a leak or of responsibility for water loss.

Q-TRACE has three aims. First, we will determine the minimum leak rate and depth that a mobile cold-atom gravimeter can detect under realistic street conditions. Second, we will evaluate whether gravity-informed inspections reduce the time and excavation required to confirm leaks relative to conventional pressure-and-acoustic workflows. Third, we will assess whether changing the source of evidence alters the distribution of repair investments and household enforcement actions across neighborhoods with different histories of infrastructure maintenance.

The technical study will cover twelve pipe corridors in the 11-square-kilometer North Dryline service area over 24 months. Six corridors will receive gravity-informed inspection, and six matched corridors will continue with the utility’s standard diagnostic process. Matching will use pipe age, material, pressure regime, road traffic, soil class, and prior break frequency; final assignment will also reflect access and work-scheduling constraints. Four corridors in each group will be drawn from census blocks with repair backlogs exceeding the city median. This design prioritizes operational feasibility, although it will not fully separate the intervention from corridor-selection effects.

The sensing system will combine two fixed reference gravimeters with one roving instrument, all using rubidium atom interferometry. The fixed units will track regional drift, tides, and broad groundwater changes. The roving unit will occupy stations at approximately 50-meter intervals for six-hour overnight measurements, with denser 15-meter spacing around candidate anomalies. We target a field sensitivity of 15 nanometers per second squared after vibration correction. A hierarchical inversion model will combine repeated gravity observations with rainfall, barometric pressure, soil-moisture probes, pumping records, pipe maps, and traffic accelerometer data. The primary technical hypothesis is that persistent leaks greater than 0.3 liters per second and shallower than eight meters can be localized to within 25 meters after three monthly measurement rounds.

Validation will proceed at three levels. Before street deployment, the instruments will be tested over a buried flexible reservoir at the fictional Larkspur Hydraulic Yard, where controlled filling and drainage will produce known mass changes. In the field, candidate anomalies will be assessed using acoustic correlators, district pressure loggers, electrical resistivity transects, and targeted inspection pits. Finally, all confirmed repairs will be followed for eight weeks; a gravity anomaly that diminishes after repair will count as stronger evidence than excavation alone. A commercial spring gravimeter will traverse the same stations during four campaigns, allowing direct comparison of precision, setup time, operator burden, and total cost per confirmed leak. The project will report null detections and false alarms as well as successful localizations.

The social research component is designed around the risk that a more sensitive instrument could intensify surveillance rather than improve maintenance. Before data collection, the Port Rellan utility, tenant advocates, repair workers, and residents selected by neighborhood assemblies will establish a Community Data Council. The operating agreement will prohibit using Q-TRACE data to issue household fines, support eviction proceedings, or infer activity inside a dwelling. Public maps will be aggregated to street segments, and parcel-resolvable layers will be held by an independent civic data trust for no longer than 18 months. Council members will be compensated, and the council may delay publication of maps that create a foreseeable risk of identifying a household. The agreement will also require the utility to publish its response time to anomalies and explain decisions not to investigate them. The project does not, however, create a statutory enforcement mechanism if a future administration declines to honor these commitments.

Primary engineering outcomes are detection probability by leak rate and depth, localization error, false-positive rate, excavation meters per confirmed leak, and time from anomaly to repair. We will estimate these outcomes using multilevel models that account for repeated observations and corridor characteristics. Operational success is defined as at least a 30% reduction in median confirmation time without an increase in unnecessary excavations. Equity outcomes include repair spending per kilometer, days under shutoff warning, billing disputes initiated, and resident-reported confidence in the diagnostic process. Changes will be compared between intervention and matched corridors using a difference-in-differences analysis. With only twelve corridors and nonrandom assignment, these estimates will be interpreted as evidence of direction and implementation mechanisms rather than a definitive citywide causal effect.

The project will deliver an open, non-parcel-resolvable gravity time-series dataset; calibration protocols for mobile atom interferometers; an urban noise-correction toolkit; a comparison of quantum and classical gravimeters; and a procurement guide specifying when quantum sensing adds enough information to justify its cost. Two paid technical fellowships will train residents from the study area in instrument operation and environmental data stewardship. If the target sensitivity cannot be achieved at high-traffic stations, measurements will be repeated with longer overnight dwell times and those locations will be flagged as unsuitable; a broader alternative deployment strategy is not budgeted.

Q-TRACE addresses a central challenge for climate adaptation: scarcity policies are only as fair as the evidence used to assign responsibility for loss. By coupling quantum measurements with conventional verification and community control of high-resolution data, the project will establish whether a laboratory-grade sensing advance can become a practical infrastructure tool without turning uncertainty into punishment. Success would not eliminate political choices about rationing or investment, but it could make hidden public-system failures harder to transfer onto households least able to contest them.
