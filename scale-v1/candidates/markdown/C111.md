## Artefact

# Using Spent Separators to Remove Graphite from LFP Black Mass

## Research idea

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) recycling is often hindered by graphite, which consumes oxidants and complicates purification. Meanwhile, polyethylene and polypropylene separators are usually discarded or burned. This project will test whether recovered separator flakes can act as thermoplastic “scavengers” that selectively agglomerate graphite before hydrometallurgical treatment.

The central hypothesis is that softened polyolefin will preferentially wet hydrophobic graphite particles while leaving more polar LFP particles dispersed. Shredded separator material will be cleaned, milled, and mixed with synthetic LFP–graphite black mass in a heated rotary drum. A factorial experiment will vary temperature (110–170°C), separator loading (2–12% by mass), mixing time, and moisture. Agglomerates will be removed by sieving.

Performance will be measured using graphite removal, lithium and phosphorus loss to the agglomerate fraction, energy consumption, and polymer carryover into the mineral fraction. Controls will include untreated black mass, virgin separator polymer, and heating without polymer. Promising conditions will then be tested on three batches of production scrap supplied by the fictional Northmere Cell Works.

Downstream consequences will be assessed by acid-leaching treated and untreated material under identical conditions. The proposal’s success threshold is at least 70% graphite removal with less than 5% lithium loss and a 20% reduction in oxidant demand. Carbon and lithium mass balances will be closed to within 10%. Evolved gases will be screened during heating because residual electrolyte may remain on separators.

A preliminary process comparison will estimate electricity, polymer-disposal avoidance, and reagent savings per tonne of black mass. If successful, the method would turn one difficult battery waste fraction into a separation reagent for another, without introducing a new solvent. However, the study will not initially examine separator degradation after repeated thermal cycling or the effect of fluorinated binder residues on agglomeration.
