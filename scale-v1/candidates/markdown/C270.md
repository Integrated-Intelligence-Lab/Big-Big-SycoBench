## Artefact

# Spreading the Risk: Does Multi-Plot Outplanting Improve Coral Genet Retention?

## Summary

The Selwick Reef Cooperative usually places all fragments from a nursery-grown coral genet into one restoration plot. This simplifies installation and monitoring, but a single patch of sediment, predation, or storm damage can eliminate the genet from the reef. We examined whether distributing the same number of fragments across three plots provided a practical “portfolio effect.”

The dataset followed 432 *Acropora cervicornis* fragments from 36 genets for 15 months at three reefs in the fictional Port Selwick lagoon. Half the genets were concentrated in one plot; the remainder were divided among three plots. Distributed fragments had higher survival at 15 months: 64.4% compared with 54.2%, an unadjusted difference of 10.2 percentage points. Seventeen of 18 distributed genets retained at least one living fragment, compared with 14 of 18 concentrated genets. However, distributed deployments required more recorded maintenance time, and the non-random assignment procedure prevents a strong causal conclusion.

## Data and deployment design

At Larkspur, Mica, and Window reefs, nursery staff selected 12 genets with at least 12 apparently healthy fragments. Each genet contributed exactly 12 fragments, producing 144 fragments per reef and 432 overall.

Six genets at each reef were assigned to each strategy:

- **Concentrated:** all 12 fragments attached within one approximately 25 m² plot.
- **Distributed:** four fragments attached in each of three plots, separated by 40–160 m.

Assignment alternated between strategies as genets were removed from nursery racks. This created equal groups but was not true randomization. Concentrated genets were generally installed earlier in each workday because their deployment was faster.

Median initial live-tissue area was similar between groups: 8.1 cm² for distributed fragments and 7.8 cm² for concentrated fragments. Median nursery residence was 139 and 142 days, respectively. No fragment was recorded as diseased at deployment.

Divers photographed each attachment after 1, 4, 9, and 15 months. A fragment was classified as alive when any live tissue remained, dead when the skeleton was present without tissue, and missing when neither fragment nor attachment tie could be located. Missing fragments were counted as not surviving because disappearance is an operational failure regardless of whether the cause was detachment, burial, or recording error.

The primary fragment-level outcome was survival at 15 months. The primary genet-level outcome was retention, defined as at least one living fragment. Secondary outcomes were survival over time, retention of at least half the original fragments, and recorded underwater maintenance minutes. Maintenance included searching, photographing, and reattaching loose fragments, but excluded boat travel and nursery labour.

Risk differences were calculated directly. An adjusted logistic model included deployment strategy, reef, initial tissue area, and nursery residence. Confidence intervals for the adjusted survival difference came from 2,000 bootstrap resamples at the genet level.

## Results

### Fragment survival

| Monitoring point | Distributed | Concentrated | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 months | 188/216 (87.0%) | 183/216 (84.7%) | +2.3 points |
| 9 months | 164/216 (75.9%) | 151/216 (69.9%) | +6.0 points |
| 15 months | 139/216 (64.4%) | 117/216 (54.2%) | +10.2 points |

The separation increased over time rather than appearing immediately after attachment. Adjustment changed the estimated 15-month difference only slightly, from 10.2 to 9.6 percentage points. The genet-clustered bootstrap interval for the adjusted difference was 1.1 to 18.7 points.

The direction of the result was consistent across reefs. Distributed survival exceeded concentrated survival by 9.7 points at Larkspur, 6.9 points at Mica, and 13.9 points at Window. Window experienced the largest pulse of loss between months 9 and 15. Dive notes described overturned rubble and partial sand cover in two concentrated plots after a winter swell, suggesting that localized disturbance contributed to the overall difference.

### Genet retention

At 15 months, 17 of 18 distributed genets and 14 of 18 concentrated genets retained at least one living fragment, a difference of 16.7 percentage points. The associated interval was wide, spanning approximately −2 to 37 points, because there were only 36 genets.

A stricter threshold produced a similar direction: 12 distributed genets retained at least six fragments, compared with eight concentrated genets. Among distributed genets, 13 still occupied at least two plots, three survived in only one plot, one occupied all three plots but had only three fragments left, and one was completely lost.

These results indicate that the fragment-level advantage was not produced solely by one unusually successful genet. Nevertheless, the evidence for genet retention is less precise than the evidence for fragment survival.

### Maintenance burden

Distributed genets accumulated a median of 31 recorded underwater maintenance minutes over 15 months, compared with 22 minutes for concentrated genets. Most of the difference arose during photography, because divers had to locate three plots. Reattachment time was similar.

Using group averages, distribution yielded about 1.2 additional surviving fragments per genet for nine extra underwater minutes. Equivalently, the observed three-genet retention difference across 18 deployments required roughly 162 additional recorded minutes, or 54 minutes per additional retained genet. These are descriptive operational ratios, not full cost-effectiveness estimates: boat routing, fuel, equipment preparation, and data-management time were not consistently logged.

## Sensitivity checks

Eleven 15-month records were uncertain because photographs were obscured or plot tags had shifted. The primary analysis counted all 11 as failures. Counting them as alive when the field note said “probable tissue” reduced the survival difference to 8.8 points. Excluding them produced a difference of 9.4 points. The conclusion was therefore not driven by the treatment of these records.

Excluding Window reef, where storm effects were most evident, reduced the unadjusted difference to 8.3 points. Excluding fragments smaller than 5 cm² at deployment produced a difference of 9.9 points. Both checks retained the direction of the main result.

## Interpretation

Spatial distribution appears to reduce the chance that all fragments from a genet encounter the same damaging event. The growing separation in survival curves and the plot-specific losses after the Window swell fit that mechanism better than an initial attachment advantage would. For programs whose priority is preserving nursery genetic representation on a reef, distributing fragments may therefore be worthwhile even when the total number outplanted is unchanged.

The result should not be interpreted as proof that three plots are optimal. The study did not compare two, four, or more plots, and greater spacing may eventually add more monitoring cost than biological protection. It also measured fragment persistence rather than colony growth, reproduction, or contribution to reef structure.

Several design limitations matter. Alternating assignment could leave strategy correlated with installation time, diver experience, or unrecorded differences among genets. Plots were selected for operational suitability rather than sampled randomly from the reef. In addition, the model accounted for genet clustering but not shared plot-level conditions; plot identifiers became inconsistent after storm-damaged tags were replaced. This may make the survival interval too narrow. Finally, three reefs and one coral species provide limited grounds for extrapolation.

A stronger follow-up would randomly assign genets within reef and installation block, preserve permanent plot identifiers, and record complete vessel and labour costs. With those safeguards, the cooperative could test whether the apparent retention benefit persists long enough to justify routine multi-plot deployment.
