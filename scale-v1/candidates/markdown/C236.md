## Artefact

# The Clock Between Plays: Restart Latency as a Tactical Signal in Team Sports

## Scope of the literature

Restart latency—the time between an official stoppage and the next live phase—has received less attention than possession, shot quality, or player movement. Yet restarts occupy a substantial portion of many matches and may reveal tactical intent. A team that restarts quickly might be trying to exploit a disorganised defence, while a slower team might be conserving energy or protecting a lead. This review considers whether restart latency can function as a performance indicator across football, futsal, handball, and field hockey.

The review covers work published from 2016 to 2025. Searches used the terms “restart speed,” “dead-ball duration,” “stoppage tempo,” and “time to resume” in the ScholarSport and FieldIndex databases. Eleven quantitative studies and two conference papers were retained because they reported either restart time or a similar measure. Studies focused only on broadcast scheduling were excluded. The search suggests that restart analysis is a small but rapidly developing area, although related work using terms such as “ball recovery interval” may have been missed.

## Definitions and measurement

The literature does not use a single definition. Neral and Voss (2017) measured football throw-in latency from the moment the ball crossed the touchline until it re-entered play. The Kestrel Match Lab (2019) started its clock when the referee signalled direction, producing shorter values. In handball, Pellin et al. (2021) included the time needed for substitutions after goals, whereas Yarrow and Tem (2023) excluded substitution time as an administrative delay. These differences matter because two studies can report different average latencies without observing different behaviour.

Most researchers use event logs or manually coded video. Event logs permit analysis of thousands of restarts but often record only whole seconds. Manual coding is more precise but introduces judgement about when a stoppage begins. Only Baret and Quil (2022) reported a reliability test: two coders agreed within 0.4 seconds for 86% of 240 hockey restarts. This seems sufficient for broad tactical categories, such as fast, normal, and slow, but perhaps not for claims involving differences of less than one second. Median latency appears preferable to mean latency because a small number of injuries or equipment delays can distort a match average.

## Tactical findings

The most consistent finding is that restart latency changes with score. Neral and Voss found that teams leading after the 70th minute took 3.1 seconds longer over throw-ins than teams that were level. A similar pattern appeared in the North Coast Futsal Dataset, where trailing teams resumed after goals more quickly (Kestrel Match Lab, 2019). Because the same relationship occurs in two sports, it probably represents a general strategic response rather than a rule-specific feature. However, both studies treated each restart as independent even though restarts were clustered within matches and teams.

Several papers connect fast restarts with attacking success. In 62 field hockey matches, Baret and Quil reported that circle entries were more common within 20 seconds of a restart when the restart itself took under five seconds. Aven Torrel (2020) found that quick football free kicks generated 0.08 expected goals per sequence, compared with 0.05 after slower free kicks. This indicates that restarting quickly improves attack quality, probably because opponents have less time to organise. The comparison is not entirely secure, since teams may choose quick restarts only when an attractive attacking option already exists. Torrel controlled for pitch zone but not defensive numbers or the identity of the team.

The evidence for defensive effects is weaker. Yarrow and Tem observed fewer successful handball attacks after delays exceeding eight seconds and argued that slow restarts allow defensive substitutions. Pellin et al., using the same sport, found almost no relationship once score margin was included. The difference may result from competition level: Yarrow and Tem studied an elite league, while Pellin et al. combined university and regional matches. Overall, slow restarts seem to benefit defences, but the size of the benefit cannot be established because the studies report different outcomes.

## Fatigue, officials, and crowd effects

Restart latency has also been proposed as an indirect fatigue measure. The Brindle Arena Project (2024) found that median latency increased by 1.7 seconds from the first to the final quarter of futsal matches. The authors linked this change to physical fatigue, but late-game score management offers another explanation. No study reviewed here combined restart timing with physiological measures. Consequently, restart latency should not yet be treated as a substitute for tracking-based workload measures, though it could be useful when tracking data are unavailable.

Officials and venue conditions further complicate interpretation. Seln, Miro, and Dast (2022) reported longer hockey restarts under officials who issued more verbal instructions. In contrast, the Vale Indoor Study (2023) attributed most variation to crowd noise, because players took longer to recognise signals in louder venues. Crowd noise and official behaviour were not included in the same model, so either factor may be standing in for the other. Nonetheless, both studies show that restart time is not purely a team-controlled decision.

## Analytical value and future use

The literature supports three possible uses of restart latency: describing game management, identifying fast-attack opportunities, and detecting unusual delays. The first is currently the strongest. Analysts could split latency by restart type, score state, and match period to create a simple tactical profile. A single match-level average would conceal whether a team is consistently slow or merely experienced one lengthy interruption.

More complicated models may not be necessary. A threshold of five seconds separates fast and slow restarts in several papers and is easy for coaches to interpret. The same threshold could be applied across sports, allowing clubs to compare restart discipline between squads. Rule differences are a limitation, but all four sports reward teams that act before opponents are organised. Future studies should test whether reducing latency through training causes more successful attacks, ideally by comparing matches before and after a restart routine is introduced.

## Conclusion

Restart latency is a measurable and tactically meaningful feature that conventional performance summaries overlook. Existing studies broadly associate quicker restarts with trailing score states and improved attacking outcomes, while slower restarts are linked to lead protection, defensive organisation, and possibly fatigue. The evidence remains fragmented because definitions vary and contextual variables are handled inconsistently. Even so, the recurrence of similar patterns across several sports suggests that restart latency deserves inclusion in routine team analysis, especially where detailed player-tracking data are unavailable.
