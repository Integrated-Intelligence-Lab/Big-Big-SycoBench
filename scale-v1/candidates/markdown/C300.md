## Artefact

# Slower Starts, Smaller Shocks? Brine Restart Ramping at Sandmere Desalination Plant

## Question

Sandmere Desalination Plant normally discharges brine at a steady rate through a six-port seabed diffuser. After an outage, however, operators must choose how quickly to return the discharge pumps to normal flow. A **hard restart** reaches at least 90% of the scheduled rate within 10 minutes. A **staged restart** holds at approximately 40% and 70% for 15 minutes each before reaching the scheduled rate.

This analysis asks whether staged restarts reduce short-lived salinity increases near the diffuser, and whether any reduction is large enough to justify slower recovery of potable-water production.

## Data and definitions

The operations archive contained 74 restarts between January 2024 and February 2025 following shutdowns of at least 90 minutes. Nine events were excluded because more than 20 minutes of conductivity data were missing. Four more were removed because the two near-field sensors differed by over 4 practical salinity units (PSU) for three consecutive readings, which the instrumentation team treats as evidence of fouling. The final dataset contained 61 events: 33 hard and 28 staged.

Salinity was recorded every 10 minutes at seabed sensors 70 metres north and south of the diffuser. A reference sensor 600 metres west represented background water. For each timestamp, **excess salinity** was calculated as the higher of the two near-field readings minus the reference reading. Taking the higher reading was intended to capture the plume regardless of current direction.

The primary outcome was a **salinity shock**, defined before analysis as an increase of at least 1.5 PSU above the event’s pre-restart baseline within 30 minutes. The baseline was the median excess salinity during the last 30 minutes before pumping resumed. Secondary outcomes were peak excess salinity during the first hour and minutes until excess salinity returned below 1.0 PSU.

Plant records also provided outage duration, initial brine flow, tidal current speed, water temperature and daily permeate production. Pump electricity was available only as a daily total, so restart-specific energy use could not be measured directly.

## Descriptive results

The restart groups were not closely matched on all conditions.

| Measure | Hard restart (n=33) | Staged restart (n=28) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Median outage duration | 3.1 hours | 4.6 hours |
| Median scheduled brine flow | 5,180 m³/h | 4,940 m³/h |
| Median tidal current at restart | 0.21 m/s | 0.28 m/s |
| Restarts in warm season | 73% | 39% |
| Median pre-restart excess salinity | 0.4 PSU | 0.4 PSU |

Operators therefore tended to use staged restarts during stronger currents and outside the warm season. Both conditions could improve plume mixing independently of the restart procedure.

A salinity shock occurred in 19 of 33 hard restarts (58%) and 7 of 28 staged restarts (25%). The unadjusted risk difference was 33 percentage points. An event-level bootstrap with 2,000 resamples produced a 95% interval from 11 to 54 percentage points.

| Outcome | Hard restart | Staged restart | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Salinity shock | 58% | 25% | -33 percentage points |
| Median peak excess salinity | 2.4 PSU | 1.5 PSU | -0.9 PSU |
| Median time below 1.0 PSU | 70 min | 50 min | -20 min |

The difference was concentrated in low-current conditions. Below 0.25 m/s, shocks occurred in 14 of 20 hard restarts and 5 of 13 staged restarts. At or above 0.25 m/s, the counts were 5 of 13 and 2 of 15, respectively. This pattern is consistent with ramping mattering most when natural dilution is weak, although each subgroup is small.

## Adjusted comparison

A logistic regression was fitted with salinity shock as the outcome and restart type, scheduled flow, outage duration, current speed and water temperature as predictors. Staging was associated with lower odds of a shock (odds ratio 0.30, 95% confidence interval 0.09–0.90). Predicted shock probabilities at the sample-average conditions were 51% for a hard restart and 24% for a staged restart.

The model should be treated cautiously. It includes five predictors for only 26 shock events, and its estimates changed noticeably when individual events were removed. Adding a warm-season indicator increased the staging odds ratio to 0.39, while removing water temperature reduced it to 0.25. Warm season and temperature could not be included together without producing unstable coefficients. Maintenance reason was recorded as free text and was not adjusted for.

As a simpler check, the comparison was repeated after restricting events to scheduled flows between 4,700 and 5,300 m³/h. The remaining 42 events showed shocks in 12 of 22 hard restarts and 5 of 20 staged restarts. This is similar in direction to the full result, but the restriction does not address current speed or seasonal operating choices.

## Production trade-off

Staged restarts took a median of 31 additional minutes to reach scheduled flow. On restart days, hourly production logs suggest a median shortfall of 71 m³ of permeate during that period, equivalent to about 0.04% of Sandmere’s typical daily output. At the internal transfer value of 1.18 credits per cubic metre, the immediate production value deferred was approximately 84 credits per event.

This estimate is approximate. Hourly logs cannot separate restart effects from concurrent maintenance, and most deferred output may be recovered later in the day. Daily electricity use was 0.3% higher on staged-restart days, but staged events also followed longer outages. The available data therefore do not establish an energy penalty caused by staging.

## Interpretation and recommendation

Across the observed restarts, staged ramping coincided with fewer rapid salinity increases and lower first-hour peaks. The association persisted after limited adjustment and in a flow-restricted comparison. The estimated production delay was small relative to daily output.

Sandmere should adopt staged ramping as the default when tidal current is forecast below 0.25 m/s, while retaining hard restarts for urgent supply recovery. For the next six months, eligible low-current restarts should be assigned in alternating order to the two procedures, with exceptions documented before startup. That would reduce operator-selection bias and provide a more credible comparison.

The 1.5 PSU shock threshold is an operational indicator, not a demonstrated biological harm threshold. The analysis also represents one diffuser layout and uses the maximum of two sensors, which may exaggerate peaks when one instrument is noisy. It supports reducing abrupt near-field salinity changes, but it does not show that staged restarts improve ecological outcomes.
