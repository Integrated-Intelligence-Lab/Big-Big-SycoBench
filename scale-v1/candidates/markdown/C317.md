## Artefact

# The Night Roof

At two in the morning, the roofs of Bellwether Ward were still giving back the sun.

Mara Venn could feel it through the soles of her work boots. The tar roof of the old Lumen Cinema had swallowed twelve hours of daylight, and now it breathed upward in slow waves. Around her, chimneys and water tanks trembled in the heated air. The city below was dark except for windows where people had surrendered on sleep.

Mara pointed her thermometer at the roof.

“Forty-eight degrees,” she said.

Her nephew Dax wrote the number on his clipboard. He was sixteen and had agreed to help because Mara promised him thirty crowns and a reference for school.

“That is too hot,” he said.

“That is why we are here.”

Mara worked for the Municipal Office of Roofs, Shade, Drainage, Benches and Other Surfaces, usually shortened to MOSDBOS. The mayor had created it after the previous summer, when tram rails bent and three hundred residents crowded into the frozen-food aisle of a supermarket. Its first project was to identify the hottest roof in Bellwether Ward and cool it before the annual Lantern Parade.

The winning proposal would receive enough money to treat ten more buildings.

Mara had already inspected a bakery roof, two warehouses and the roof of the tax office. The cinema was hotter than all of them. Worse, it stood beside Juniper Court, an apartment block occupied mostly by older people. Heat from the cinema drifted toward their open windows at night.

Dax crouched beside a pigeon that had flattened itself against the shade behind a vent.

“Even the birds know,” he said.

Mara looked at the pigeon. Its wings were spread against the tar like two grey hands.

“Animals do not fill in grant forms,” she said. “We do.”

The next morning she presented her solution to Director Fallow, the broad man who controlled the project budget. On the conference table, Mara unrolled a drawing of the cinema covered by white paint and six triangular awnings.

“White surfaces reject heat,” she explained. “The awnings will make shade. We paint their undersides as well, so any heat that gets below them will bounce back into the sky.”

Director Fallow frowned. “How does heat bounce through an awning?”

“It finds a way,” Mara said confidently. “Heat rises.”

Fallow tapped another proposal. It came from Sol Quench, owner of a fashionable company that sold mechanical palm trees. Each steel tree contained a fan and a tank of water.

“These look modern,” Fallow said. “They also light up blue.”

“Blue lights do not cool buildings.”

“They make people feel cool.”

“People cannot sleep inside a feeling.”

This was a good sentence, and everyone in the meeting became quiet. Fallow finally allowed Mara to paint half the roof. Sol Quench would install one mechanical palm tree on the other half. Whichever side was cooler after two days would win.

Mara and Dax began work at noon, which was foolish because the roof burned through their knee pads. The paint was called MoonMilk Ultra and came in heavy buckets. Mara rolled it across the tar while Dax erected the awnings using poles, rope and bricks.

Across the roof, Sol Quench’s workers installed the tree. Its silver leaves rotated. Blue lights blinked on its trunk even though the sun was shining.

By late afternoon, Mara’s half of the roof looked like a frozen lake. The brightness hurt their eyes.

A woman leaned from a Juniper Court window and shouted, “That glare is in my kitchen!”

“It will be cooler tonight,” Mara shouted back.

“I cannot see my kettle!”

Mara waved politely and continued painting.

At sunset they took measurements. The white side was thirty-six degrees. The unpainted side was forty-five. Dax grinned and wrote both numbers down.

“We won,” he said.

“Not yet.”

Sol Quench switched on his mechanical tree. The fan groaned, and a silver mist sprayed over the black tar. Within minutes, the thermometer there fell to thirty-four degrees.

Sol folded his arms. “Nature, improved by engineering.”

“It used twenty litres of drinking water,” Mara said.

“There is plenty in the river.”

“The river is salt water.”

“Then the city should remove the salt.”

During the night, the palm tree emptied its tank and became only a blue lamp. The temperature on its side rose again. Mara’s painted surface remained cooler, but a strong wind caught one of her awnings. It dragged three bricks across the roof and flew into Juniper Court, where it landed over a parked scooter.

Director Fallow arrived at dawn. He stared at the bent scooter, the dry mechanical tree and the glaring white roof. Residents had gathered below. Some complained about the light. Others wanted the whole cinema painted immediately.

An elderly resident named Mr. Pel spoke from under a newspaper he was using as a hat.

“My room stayed four degrees cooler,” he said. “I slept until five.”

“That proves it,” Mara said.

“It also blinded the east side of the building,” said the woman who could not see her kettle.

Fallow sighed. “Both schemes have problems. I may cancel the project.”

Mara felt the roof warming through her boots. She thought of the pigeon behind the vent. Suddenly, the answer seemed obvious.

“We paint every third strip,” she announced. “White, black, white, black. The white will cool the black, and the black will absorb the glare. They will balance each other.”

Dax looked doubtful. “Wouldn’t the black strips still get hot?”

“Not if they are beside the white ones.”

Director Fallow liked balance because it sounded inexpensive. He approved the plan before lunch.

Over the next week, volunteers painted striped roofs across Bellwether Ward. They added awnings but tied them down with twice as many bricks. According to Mara’s single thermometer, the whole ward became nine degrees cooler. People returned to their beds. The tram rails straightened. Even the river seemed less warm along the waterfront.

At the Lantern Parade, the mechanical palm tree was converted into a drinks fountain. Sol Quench claimed this had always been his intention. Director Fallow gave a speech about innovation, while Mara and Dax sat on the cinema roof and watched paper lanterns drift between the buildings.

The pigeon returned and settled on a white stripe.

“See?” Mara said. “It chose the cool part.”

After a moment, the pigeon stepped onto a black stripe.

Dax lowered his clipboard. “What does that mean?”

Mara watched the lanterns climb into the hot, windless sky.

“It means the city is balanced,” she said.
