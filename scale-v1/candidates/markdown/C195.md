## Artefact

# FinchWard Autonomous Dressing and Wardrobe Assistant

## 1. Product Summary

FinchWard is a compact mobile robot that selects clothing, brings it to an older resident, collects worn garments, and monitors changes in dressing habits. It is intended for assisted-living apartments where staff currently spend significant time opening wardrobes, finding matching clothes, and carrying laundry.

The robot does not dress the resident. Instead, it presents an outfit on a waist-height tray and verbally explains the order in which items should be put on. This preserves independence while removing the difficult parts of the task. FinchWard will also identify residents who may be experiencing cognitive decline by tracking repeated outfits, unusual colour combinations, or failure to change clothes.

The target selling price is £1,400, with no subscription required. One robot should serve up to twelve residents.

## 2. User Problem

Many older residents can put on their own clothing but struggle with wardrobe doors, high rails, small buttons, or remembering whether garments are clean. Staff therefore complete the entire dressing task even when only retrieval is needed. This can reduce independence and makes the morning shift busier.

Clothing is also personal. A resident may reject a technically suitable outfit because it feels formal, resembles an old uniform, or has an irritating seam. FinchWard must therefore offer choices rather than simply issuing the garment ranked highest by its software. The presentation tray will hold two options, with large fabric-covered handles labelled “this one” and “other one.” Labels must be readable from a seated position.

## 3. Goals

FinchWard shall:

1. Reduce staff time spent on clothing-related support by 70%.
2. retrieve any ordinary garment from any domestic wardrobe;
3. offer weather-appropriate and matching outfits;
4. separate worn clothing from clean clothing;
5. detect meaningful changes in dressing behaviour before staff notice them; and
6. operate all day without being recharged.

The product will not handle shoes because floor-level retrieval would require a larger arm. It will handle socks, despite socks also commonly being stored near floor level.

## 4. Physical Design

The robot will be 92 cm tall, 45 cm wide, and weigh no more than 9 kg so one staff member can carry it between floors. It will have two driven wheels, one stabilising caster, and a telescoping arm capable of lifting 12 kg at full extension. The arm must reach wardrobe rails up to 2.1 metres high while the base remains stationary.

A soft three-finger gripper will handle shirts, trousers, underwear, jewellery, folded knitwear, and garments on hangers. A single gripper design is preferred to avoid the expense of tool changing. The gripper shall not crease delicate fabrics and shall successfully separate stacked garments at least 98% of the time.

The front presentation tray will rise between 55 and 95 cm. Its edges will be rounded and padded. If a resident pulls on the tray when standing, the robot will lock its wheels and act as a temporary balance point. No further structural reinforcement is required because this use is expected to be brief.

An internal 80 Wh battery will provide twelve hours of normal operation, including locomotion, arm movement, computing, voice interaction, and garment freshening. Charging will take twenty minutes from an ordinary wall socket.

## 5. Wardrobe Setup

No special wardrobe fittings are required. During setup, a staff member drives FinchWard into a resident’s room and presses “Map Wardrobe.” The robot photographs every visible item, infers garment type, size, colour, cleanliness, sentimental importance, and preferred combinations, then creates a digital inventory. The process should take under three minutes.

Closed drawers will be opened using the gripper. If a drawer has an unfamiliar handle, the robot will learn the opening motion by trying several directions with gradually increasing force. A maximum force limit may be added after pilot testing.

Laundry labels, ownership tags, and previous selection history will improve classification. Garments without labels will be assigned to the most likely resident according to size and style.

## 6. Daily Interaction

At a configured wake-up time, FinchWard will enter the resident’s room and say, “Good morning. I have two choices for today.” It will use the building weather forecast, calendar, and previous outfits to choose options. The resident can respond by voice, touch a handle, point, or do nothing.

If there is no response after thirty seconds, the robot will select the warmer outfit and place it within reach. Silence counts as consent because some residents speak quietly in the morning. If the resident says “go away,” FinchWard will leave immediately and record that dressing assistance was refused.

The robot will describe garment orientation, including which side is the front. This is especially useful for dark garments whose labels are difficult to see. Instructions will be limited to one sentence at a time.

Used garments can be placed in the lower hamper. An odour sensor will decide whether each item needs washing. Lightly worn garments will pass beneath a six-second ultraviolet strip and then be returned to the clean wardrobe. This process is expected to remove odours and common pathogens without detergent.

## 7. Behaviour Monitoring

FinchWard will calculate a daily Wardrobe Stability Score from colour matching, outfit repetition, dressing duration, refusals, and the number of garments dropped. A decline of 15% over three days will create a yellow staff notification. A 30% decline will create a red notification and recommend a wellbeing review.

These thresholds apply to all residents because a common scale makes changes easier for staff to understand. The system does not need clinical review because it is a wardrobe appliance rather than a medical product.

Resident identity will be confirmed through gait recognition using a knee-level camera. Since faces are not recorded, there is no significant privacy issue. Video will be retained for ninety days in case an outfit decision must be audited. Family members may request access through the staff dashboard.

## 8. Safety and Exceptions

The arm will stop when motor current indicates an obstruction. Soft materials make additional finger or neck detection unnecessary. FinchWard will travel at walking speed in corridors and at half speed in bedrooms.

If a garment cannot be retrieved, the robot will offer another item rather than repeatedly pulling. If it drops underwear in a shared corridor, it will pick the item up before continuing. If the resident becomes entangled in clothing, FinchWard will call staff but will not attempt physical removal.

Fire alarms take priority over clothing tasks. During an alarm, the robot will return to its charging location so corridors remain clear.

## 9. Success Measures and Pilot

A four-week pilot will take place at Willow Nook Residence with one robot and twelve residents. The product will be considered ready for sale if:

- at least eight residents use it twice;
- morning staff report that it is helpful;
- no more than three garments are damaged;
- retrieval succeeds in 90% of attempts; and
- the Wardrobe Stability Score produces at least one useful alert.

Training will consist of a ten-minute staff demonstration and a laminated card. After the pilot, manufacturing can begin while software issues are corrected through updates.
