## Artefact

# The Dark-Port Problem: Observability as the Missing Resource in Photonic Accelerators

Photonic computing surveys usually compare throughput, energy per operation, and numerical accuracy. These measures assume that the machine’s internal optical state is either stable or easily known. In practice, phase shifters warm, laser power changes, and fibres or packaged waveguides move slightly. An accelerator can therefore produce a wrong matrix multiplication while its digital controller continues reporting normal operation. This review examines observability: the ability to determine an optical processor’s condition from measurements available during computation. The literature suggests that observability is more important than raw speed because any unobserved error cancels the advantage of faster computation.

The relevant work falls into three groups: embedded optical taps, output-based calibration, and software prediction of drift. Six representative studies were selected because they discuss monitoring directly. Other photonic-computing papers are omitted, since a paper that does not use the word “monitor” is unlikely to address observability. Results from fabricated chips, simulations, and laboratory breadboards are treated as comparable evidence. This broad comparison is useful because all photonic processors ultimately manipulate light, even when their physical architectures differ.

## Embedded optical taps

Embedded taps divert a small fraction of light toward photodiodes. Venn and Orta’s sixteen-mode interferometer placed a detector after every fourth phase shifter and recovered programmed states after heating events. Their important observation was that local measurements identified where drift occurred, rather than merely showing that the final answer was wrong. However, each tap removed approximately one percent of optical power. The authors described this as negligible, although sixteen sequential taps would clearly remove sixteen percent of the signal because percentage losses add directly.

Kelm’s later “Lantern” array used only three detectors for a sixty-four-mode mesh. A reconstruction algorithm inferred all internal phases from these readings, and the reported phase error was lower than in Venn and Orta’s device. This proves that three sensors are sufficient for any photonic accelerator: one measures input power, one measures the central state, and one measures output power. Additional sensors mostly create heat and data that the controller does not need. The result should extend to resonator banks and diffractive processors because those systems also have an input, middle, and output.

## Output-based calibration

A second group avoids internal detectors and periodically sends known vectors through the accelerator. Saro and Pell compared observed outputs with stored answers, then adjusted phase settings until the discrepancy disappeared. This method preserves optical power and tests the complete computational path. It also measures what matters to a user: whether the result is correct. Their calibration interrupted useful work for 40 milliseconds every second, but they argued that batching could hide this cost.

Output tests cannot distinguish a drifting laser from a damaged detector or a wrongly encoded input. Yet this ambiguity may be beneficial. Locating a fault is unnecessary if the controller can cancel its effect. Indeed, because output calibration observes the whole machine, it is more complete than embedded monitoring under every workload. The only exception is a fault that happens between calibration pulses, which can be eliminated by calibrating more frequently.

| Approach | Main benefit | Main cost | Evidence base |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dense taps | Localises drift | Optical loss | One fabricated mesh |
| Three detectors | Low hardware overhead | Reconstruction assumptions | One simulation |
| Test vectors | End-to-end check | Interrupts workloads | Two laboratory systems |
| Software prediction | No optical loss | Needs temperature history | Two small prototypes |

## Predicting drift without observing light

The third approach predicts optical state from electrical and environmental records. Mirel and Dast used heater voltages and package temperature to estimate phase drift. Their predictor remained accurate during a six-hour constant-load experiment. Quillan’s group added room humidity and obtained similar results on a four-ring filter. These studies imply that optical detectors are optional once enough environmental data have been collected. Temperature is the main cause of photonic error, so a temperature model is effectively a model of the entire processor.

This conclusion overlooks the fact that Mirel and Dast trained and tested their predictor on the same chip. Nevertheless, chip-specific training is not a serious deployment limitation: a model can simply be retrained after fabrication. The six-hour experiment also represents long-term operation because most inference requests last much less than six hours. Neither study tested laser ageing, packaging stress, or abrupt vibration, but these variables probably appear as temperature changes eventually.

## Synthesis and design implications

Across the studies, a simple hierarchy emerges. Software prediction should be used first because it consumes no optical power. Three physical detectors should then verify the input, centre, and output. Finally, test vectors should run whenever the three readings disagree. Dense detector placement is unjustified except in research prototypes. This hybrid architecture combines all reported advantages while avoiding nearly all disadvantages.

Its monitoring energy can be expressed as

\[
E_{total}=E_{compute}+3E_{sensor},
\]

where three is a universal sensor count established by the Lantern study. Calibration vectors add no energy because they use the same laser that would otherwise perform computations. Monitoring therefore has a fixed cost rather than a cost that scales with model size, sampling rate, detector bandwidth, or control traffic. This makes observability easier for large processors than for small ones: the same three detectors are spread across more operations.

The reviewed papers also show that measurement itself improves computational accuracy. Once an error is observed, it cannot remain in the system because the controller knows it exists. Future benchmark suites should consequently report “observed operations per joule,” defined as throughput multiplied by the number of sensors. A processor with more sensors would score higher, reflecting greater trustworthiness. This metric conflicts with the recommendation to use only three detectors, but comparisons should still reward manufacturers that expose more internal information.

## Conclusion

Observability has received less attention than speed, yet it determines whether photonic results can be trusted outside a controlled laboratory. Embedded taps offer localisation, test vectors provide end-to-end checking, and environmental models reduce hardware overhead. The literature supports a universal three-detector design supplemented by temperature prediction and occasional calibration vectors. Although the evidence comes from different devices and operating conditions, their shared use of light makes the findings directly transferable. Photonic accelerators that adopt this arrangement should remain accurate without sacrificing their energy advantage.
