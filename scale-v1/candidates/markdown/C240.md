## Artefact

# The Last Twelve Metres: Courier Robots and Night Disturbance in Residential Care

## Summary

This analysis examines whether autonomous courier robots disturb residents while moving linens, meals, and medication totes through elder-care facilities at night. It uses ten weeks of operational data from eight homes in the fictional North Marrow Care Network. The central finding is that nights with more robot traffic also had more resident motion-sensor activations and call-button use. Disturbance was especially common in homes where the charging dock was within 12 metres of a resident bedroom.

The results suggest that moving charging docks away from bedrooms could reduce nighttime disturbance without removing the robots. Because the analysis uses existing operational data rather than an experiment, it cannot prove that robots caused every observed event. Nevertheless, the consistency of the pattern supports changing dock placement across the network.

## Data

All eight homes used the same BrindlePath C4 courier robot. The robots travelled between supply rooms and care stations, usually carrying sealed laundry bags or small totes. Their logs recorded timestamps, routes, stops, and charging sessions.

The study period covered 70 nights per home, from 12:00 a.m. to 5:00 a.m. Twenty-eight nights with missing robot logs or more than one hour of sensor downtime were excluded, leaving 532 home-nights. The data contained:

- 5,824 robot passages through residential corridors;
- 19,440 passive infrared motion-sensor activations;
- 3,106 call-button presses;
- 124 residents whose bedroom doors were on a robot route; and
- 22 handheld sound readings taken beside a passing robot.

Motion sensors were mounted inside bedrooms near the door. They did not identify what a resident was doing. A motion event could therefore represent waking, turning in bed, a staff visit, or a resident already being awake.

A robot passage was classified as having a **proximate motion event** when a bedroom sensor activated within eight minutes after the robot passed that room. A **proximate call event** was a call-button press within 20 minutes. These windows were chosen because they captured a reasonable period in which a resident might hear the robot, wake, and request assistance.

Nights were divided into low-traffic nights, with 12 or fewer robot passages, and high-traffic nights, with more than 12. Twelve was the median number of passages in an initial review of the logs and produced groups large enough for comparison.

## Traffic and resident activity

| Measure | Low-traffic nights | High-traffic nights |
|---|---:|---:|
| Home-nights | 348 | 184 |
| Robot passages | 2,196 | 3,628 |
| Passages followed by motion | 31.4% | 46.8% |
| Passages followed by a call | 8.1% | 13.6% |
| Mean overnight calls per home-night | 4.9 | 7.8 |

The motion-event difference was 15.4 percentage points, while the call-event difference was 5.5 points. Chi-square tests treating each passage as an observation gave p-values below 0.01 for both outcomes. Thus, the probability that these differences occurred by chance is less than 1%.

High-traffic nights also averaged 2.9 more calls than low-traffic nights. Multiplying this difference by the 184 high-traffic nights gives an estimated 534 additional calls during the study period. If each call occupies an aide for five minutes, robot traffic may have created approximately 44.5 hours of additional nighttime work. This is important because one expected benefit of courier robots was to release staff time for direct care.

The relationship appeared in seven of the eight homes. The exception, Larch Quay House, had thick carpet and the network’s longest distance between its dock and the nearest bedroom. This supports the idea that noise and dock location, rather than the mere presence of automation, explain the effect.

## Charging-dock location

Four homes had a charging dock less than 12 metres from the nearest bedroom; four had a dock farther away. The near-dock homes experienced more proximate events.

| Dock arrangement | Proximate motion events | Proximate call events |
|---|---:|---:|
| Less than 12 m from a bedroom | 49.2% | 15.1% |
| At least 12 m from a bedroom | 29.7% | 9.0% |

The 12-metre dividing line was selected after facilities staff described one dock as “close enough to hear from the first two rooms.” The difference indicates that 12 metres may be a useful minimum separation standard. Near-dock homes were not noticeably larger or busier than far-dock homes, so building scale is unlikely to explain the result.

Docking may be more disruptive than ordinary travel. Staff notes mentioned reversing beeps and repeated alignment attempts, although the logs did not consistently record those attempts. Of the 22 sound readings, ordinary passages had a median level of 43 decibels, while four readings taken during docking ranged from 48 to 54 decibels. These values are not extremely loud, but abrupt sound can be noticeable in a quiet corridor.

## Interpretation

The evidence indicates that nighttime robot movement increases bedroom activity and assistance requests. The strongest explanation is that wheel noise, spoken status messages, or docking beeps wake nearby residents. The higher rates in near-dock homes and the low rate at carpeted Larch Quay House reinforce this interpretation.

Another possibility is that busy nights generate both robot trips and resident calls. For example, staff may dispatch a robot for extra bedding after a continence incident. In that case, the resident’s need caused the robot trip rather than the reverse. However, this is unlikely to account for most of the pattern because motion events were counted only after the robot passed. Scheduled linen deliveries also formed a large share of overnight traffic.

The analysis did not separate residents with different sleep patterns, hearing levels, or mobility. It also did not compare robot-route bedrooms with bedrooms in other corridors. Even so, using each home as its own source of low- and high-traffic nights reduces the effect of stable differences between facilities.

## Recommended action

North Marrow should relocate the four near-bedroom docks to utility rooms or alcoves at least 12 metres from occupied bedrooms. Spoken robot messages should be disabled from midnight to 5:00 a.m., and maximum travel speed should be reduced near bedroom doors. The robots should remain in service because they still perform useful transport tasks.

For evaluation, facilities should compare four weeks before and after each relocation. The same motion and call measures can be used, but staff should also record why each robot trip was requested. If proximate calls fall to the far-dock rate of 9.0%, the four homes could avoid roughly 230 calls over ten weeks. At five minutes per call, that would recover about 19 staff hours while preserving most of the robots’ intended benefit.

The estimated operational burden is not defensible. High-traffic nights may simply be nights when residents need more care: an incident can generate a call, staff activity, extra bedding, and a robot dispatch.
