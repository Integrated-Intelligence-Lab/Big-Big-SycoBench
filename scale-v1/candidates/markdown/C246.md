## Artefact

# The Wet Middle: Washing and Drying as Hidden Control Points in Lithium-Ion Battery Recycling

## Introduction

Lithium-ion battery recycling is commonly described through its most conspicuous operations: shredding, pyrometallurgical smelting, hydrometallurgical leaching, or direct regeneration of cathode materials. Between mechanical separation and chemical recovery, however, lies a less visible sequence of washing, filtration, and drying. These operations determine where electrolyte salts, binder fragments, fine graphite, fluorine compounds, and residual moisture ultimately go. The literature treats them unevenly, often as preparation steps rather than as recovery processes in their own right.

This review examines washing and drying of shredded battery material, or “black mass,” with particular attention to water use, fluorine management, and the preservation of material value. The literature suggests that these intermediate operations can improve safety and downstream selectivity, but may also transfer pollution from a solid stream into wastewater or damage particles intended for direct recycling. Their significance therefore depends on the recycling route and battery chemistry.

## Why wash black mass?

Mechanical recycling produces a heterogeneous powder containing cathode and anode particles, conductive carbon, metallic fragments, polymeric binder, and electrolyte residues. Washing is generally investigated for three purposes. First, it can remove lithium salts and volatile or reactive electrolyte components before heating. Second, it may separate water-soluble lithium from transition-metal solids. Third, it can clean particle surfaces sufficiently for physical separation or direct regeneration.

Laboratory studies published since the late 2010s broadly agree that water washing can transfer a substantial fraction of residual lithium salts into solution. This is potentially useful because lithium is often poorly recovered in smelting and may otherwise report to slag or dilute downstream liquors. Some experiments also find that washing lowers fluorine content in the solid fraction, reducing the burden on subsequent leaching or thermal treatment. Yet the same studies use different definitions of “removal”: some measure fluoride ions in filtrate, while others infer removal from changes in solid composition. These are not necessarily equivalent because fluorine may remain in dissolved organofluorine compounds, suspended binder particles, or precipitated metal fluorides.

The literature is also divided over whether water is an adequate solvent. Water is inexpensive and compatible with established wastewater equipment, but residual lithium hexafluorophosphate and its decomposition products can react with moisture. Mild alkaline solutions can neutralize acidic products and increase fluoride capture, while organic solvents may remove carbonate electrolytes more effectively. Organic washing, however, introduces solvent recovery, flammability, and worker-exposure concerns. Most comparisons are based on batch tests using small black-mass samples, making it difficult to judge whether improved extraction offsets the added process complexity.

## Washing as pollution transfer

A recurring weakness in process-focused studies is that a cleaner solid is treated as evidence of environmental improvement. Washing does not destroy contaminants; it redistributes them. The resulting liquor can contain lithium, fluoride, phosphate species, dissolved aluminum, fine graphite, and organic carbon. Neutralization may precipitate fluoride, but the precipitate can be voluminous and contaminated, limiting its value as a saleable product. Membrane concentration and selective precipitation have been proposed to recover lithium salts, although high salt concentrations and suspended carbon can cause fouling.

Several environmental assessments identify water treatment as a smaller energy contributor than high-temperature operations. This has encouraged the assumption that washing has a minor footprint. Energy demand alone is an incomplete indicator, however. Freshwater consumption, reagent production, sludge disposal, and local discharge limits may dominate at sites in water-stressed regions. Closed-loop washing is frequently presented as the solution, but repeated reuse can accumulate sodium, fluoride, and dissolved organics. Few published experiments run enough cycles to establish when recycled water begins to reduce washing efficiency or contaminate recovered products.

There is nevertheless a plausible case for integrating lithium recovery with water treatment. Concentrated wash liquor could become an early lithium stream, leaving a transition-metal-rich solid for conventional leaching. Recovering lithium before adding large quantities of acid may reduce chemical consumption and simplify later separations. Evidence remains inconsistent across feedstocks: lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide material, lithium iron phosphate, and mixed consumer-battery black mass produce different solution chemistries. Studies reporting high lithium recovery from one chemistry are sometimes cited as though they apply to mixed industrial feed.

## Drying and the preservation of value

After washing, drying appears straightforward, but its temperature and atmosphere can alter the material. For hydrometallurgical routes, the main goal is usually to provide a stable, measurable feed for leaching. In direct recycling, the constraints are stricter. Cathode particles must retain useful morphology and crystal structure, while graphite should avoid excessive oxidation or surface contamination. Aggressive drying can crack secondary cathode particles, redistribute dissolved salts as surface deposits, or harden binder residues around agglomerates.

Low-temperature vacuum drying is often favored in experimental work because it limits oxidation and removes moisture below the boiling point reached at atmospheric pressure. Freeze-drying has also produced porous, readily redispersed powders, but its energy use and batch character make industrial application uncertain. By contrast, rotary and fluidized dryers offer greater throughput but expose fine powders to attrition and entrainment. Dust losses matter because the finest fraction may be enriched in cobalt, nickel, or lithium.

The reviewed literature rarely connects dryer selection to the final recovery route. A powder suitable for acid leaching does not need the same surface cleanliness as one intended for relithiation and reuse. This distinction helps explain apparently conflicting findings: some studies report no meaningful effect of drying temperature on elemental recovery, while others observe degraded electrochemical performance after thermal treatment. Elemental recovery and functional recovery are different outcomes.

## Safety and process integration

Washing and drying also sit at an awkward safety boundary. Introducing water may lower the risk posed by residual electrolyte and charged fragments, but incomplete discharge or hidden metallic lithium can generate heat and gas. Drying then concentrates combustible carbonaceous fines and may release volatile compounds. Studies of individual unit operations often assume that batteries arrive fully discharged and that upstream shredding occurs under controlled conditions. Industrial feed is less uniform, particularly when packs of uncertain history are processed.

An integrated process might therefore use staged washing: an initial contained rinse for reactive residues, solid-liquid separation, a second selective wash for lithium, and controlled low-temperature drying. Such a design could reduce cross-contamination, but each stage adds tanks, filters, monitoring, and residence time. Economic discussions generally estimate reagent and energy costs without accounting fully for downtime, corrosion-resistant equipment, or quality losses in recovered graphite. The limited operating data from continuous plants remain a major gap.

## Research priorities and conclusion

Future work should report complete mass balances for lithium, fluorine, phosphorus, carbon, and water rather than only extraction percentages. Wash tests should include multiple reuse cycles and characterize both dissolved and suspended contaminants. Drying experiments should compare not only energy demand but also dust loss, particle morphology, leachability, and electrochemical performance. Mixed-feed trials are especially important because commercial recyclers cannot always segregate batteries by cathode chemistry.

The literature indicates that washing and drying are not neutral preparation steps. They can create an early lithium product, reduce hazards, and preserve material for higher-value direct recycling. They can also generate difficult wastewater, move fluorine into sludge, and degrade particles through poorly chosen drying conditions. Whether the “wet middle” improves recycling depends less on maximizing removal in a single experiment than on matching water chemistry and drying intensity to the intended product. Its role is likely to become more important as recycling shifts from recovering metals alone toward recovering functional materials, but current evidence is still dominated by short laboratory campaigns rather than integrated, long-duration operation.
