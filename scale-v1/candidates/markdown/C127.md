## Artefact

# The Thursday Edge

By breakfast, Nera Voss had erased Kestrel Island three times.

Each erasure began with one red node: a greenhouse where silver-vein orchids had developed rust-coloured lesions. Her model sent spores through the archipelago aboard nursery shipments, then gardeners’ gloves, then the municipal compost barge. Within six weeks, every island was red except Kestrel.

That was the problem. Kestrel had reported the first sick orchid.

Nera lowered the transmission rate. The outbreak slowed, but still travelled in the wrong direction. She added wind dispersal, humidity, uncertain reporting dates. Ten thousand simulations bloomed across her screen like mould. Kestrel remained epidemiologically innocent.

“You’ve forgotten Thursday,” said Olan, the greenhouse caretaker.

He stood in the doorway holding two mugs and smelling faintly of fertiliser.

“Thursday isn’t a parameter.”

“It is if you’re a plant.”

Every Thursday, he explained, the inter-island ferry carried schoolchildren to Kestrel’s propagation house. They potted orchid cuttings, then took their seedlings home. The ferry manifest listed the children as passengers, not the trays beneath their seats.

Nera built a new contact layer: twenty-three classrooms, five islands, one weekly pulse of movement. The model’s red nodes now unfolded outward from Kestrel with humiliating elegance.

At the emergency meeting, the nursery owners demanded a shipment ban. Nera showed them two projections. A ban would probably work, but closing the Thursday programme and sterilising its tools achieved nearly the same reduction, with wide uncertainty around both estimates.

“Which one is true?” the mayor asked.

“Neither,” Nera said. “They’re maps of what may happen next.”

Outside, Olan was loading clean trowels into the ferry. Children crowded the rail, each guarding an empty pot.

On Nera’s screen, Kestrel was still red. But for the first time, the colour stopped at the water.
