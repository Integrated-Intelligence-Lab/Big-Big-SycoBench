## Artefact

# Beyond the Endline Mean: Timing, Repayment Flexibility, and the Hidden Insurance Value of Microcredit

## Scope and framing

Microfinance evaluations often ask whether access raises average business profit or household consumption at an endline 12–24 months after rollout. Those are important tests of transformational claims, but they fit poorly with a product whose immediate function may be moving resources across weeks. This review considers a narrower question: what does the literature imply about microcredit’s value as a liquidity and risk-management device, and how should evaluations measure that value? The focus is credit rather than savings or insurance, drawing mainly on randomized studies since 2005, alongside financial diaries and contract-design experiments. This is a thematic rather than exhaustive review; the cited studies were selected because they illuminate timing, not because they constitute a systematic sample.

The central proposition is conditional. Credit can help households bridge a seasonal income gap or absorb a repair, but rigid repayment can create a second liquidity shock. Consequently, a null change in annual consumption may conceal either useful smoothing or costly churning. Conversely, smooth consumption does not itself prove welfare improvement if it is financed by expensive rollover debt.

## What average effects established

The six-country randomized evidence synthesized by Banerjee, Karlan, and Zinman (2015) displaced the simple “microcredit miracle” narrative. Across diverse settings, expanded access generally produced modest changes in business activity, limited average effects on consumption, and little evidence of broad improvements in health, education, or women’s empowerment. Meager’s (2019) hierarchical reanalysis similarly found small average effects with substantial uncertainty and less cross-site heterogeneity than selective reading of individual trials suggested. These findings are strongest against claims of rapid, universal transformation. They are less decisive about short-lived liquidity benefits because most component studies used one or two household surveys, wide recall windows, and intention-to-treat estimates diluted by incomplete take-up.

This distinction matters. An offer can have option value even when no loan is taken: a household may invest or hold fewer precautionary balances because emergency credit is available. Standard take-up coding assigns such households to “non-borrowers,” while endline profit measures ignore avoided distress sales. Yet option value is often asserted rather than directly identified; expectations of access are rarely measured, and lenders may not honor future credit during covariate shocks.

## Timing as mechanism, not nuisance

Financial diaries in *Portfolios of the Poor* (Collins et al., 2009) document frequent small transactions used to assemble lump sums and manage irregular earnings. Although the diary samples are not nationally representative and cannot identify causal impacts, they show why annual net income is an incomplete denominator for evaluating finance. A loan taken before school fees and repaid after harvest may leave yearly consumption unchanged while preventing a costly asset sale. The same annual profile could describe a borrower who repays one lender with another loan and ends the year more exposed. Aggregation makes these pathways observationally equivalent.

Seasonality sharpens the issue. If surveys occur after harvest, treatment and control households may look food-secure even when the control group endured a longer lean-season shortfall. If surveys occur immediately before repayment, measured consumption may make credit appear harmful despite an earlier gain. Repeated measurement therefore changes the estimand from a stock at an arbitrary date to the depth and duration of deprivation. However, frequent surveys can induce reporting fatigue, and phone panels may disproportionately lose poorer or indebted respondents.

## Contract design and liquidity reversal

The grace-period experiment by Field, Pande, Papp, and Rigol (2013) provides unusually direct evidence that repayment timing changes borrower behavior. Delaying the start of repayment encouraged riskier, larger business investment and raised later profits, but it also increased default risk. This supports a mechanism in which immediate installments prevent investment in projects with delayed returns. It does not imply that grace periods are universally preferable: the study concerns urban Indian borrowers selected by a particular lending model, and lender monitoring or borrower selection may respond differently at scale.

Weekly group repayment may also supply commitment and peer support, benefits that a pure liquidity account understates. At the same time, frequent meetings impose travel and opportunity costs, while joint liability can shift risk through social networks rather than eliminate it. Evaluations comparing “credit” with “no credit” bundle principal size, pricing, meeting frequency, liability, and collection practices. The literature on average access effects therefore offers limited guidance about which contractual feature generated an outcome. Factorial trials and head-to-head contract comparisons remain relatively scarce.

## A better evaluation architecture

Three linked outcome layers emerge from this literature. First, transaction-level measures should record loan timing, fees, refinancing, delinquency, and repayment sources. Nominal interest rates alone miss compulsory savings, travel, and penalties. Second, high-frequency household measures should capture food shortfalls, work interruption, asset sales, and transfers received and given. Useful summaries include weeks below a locally defined consumption floor and the cumulative size of shortfalls, reported alongside mean consumption rather than replacing it. Third, evaluators should retain longer-run enterprise, asset, and subjective well-being outcomes to test whether smoothing today compromises solvency tomorrow.

Analysis should separate assignment, access, take-up, and repeated borrowing. Distributional effects deserve particular attention: a small mean may combine gains among households with seasonal enterprises and losses among borrowers with volatile labor income. Pre-specified subgroup analysis by baseline cash-flow pattern is more credible than searching across occupation labels after results are known. Still, “resilience” should not become an all-purpose success category. Lower consumption volatility can reflect either protection or suppressed spending, so cash-flow evidence must be interpreted with debt stocks and arrears.

## Spillovers and costs

Household-level randomization can miss effects transmitted through local labor, product markets, and informal lending. A borrower who hires a neighbor may improve control-household income; a borrower who stops lending to relatives may do the reverse. Such spillovers weaken the meaning of untreated individuals as a counterfactual. Cluster randomization and saturation designs help, although they require larger samples and assumptions about the relevant market boundary. Evidence on these channels remains thinner than evidence on direct borrower outcomes.

Cost-effectiveness is an additional blind spot. A flexible product may improve smoothing while increasing lender capital requirements and defaults. Social evaluation should count administrative costs, borrower time, and losses borne by guarantors, not only changes in client outcomes. Yet comparable cost data are rarely reported, making it difficult to rank microcredit against cash transfers, savings facilitation, or emergency grants.

## Conclusion

The literature justifies skepticism toward large average claims, but not the inference that microcredit has no value when endline income is unchanged. Its plausible contribution is narrower: reallocating liquidity across time for some households under some contracts. Evaluations should pair conventional annual welfare outcomes with high-frequency measures of shortfall duration, refinancing, and repayment burden, while testing contract components separately. The unresolved question is not simply whether households borrow, but whether the timing of funds and obligations matches their cash flows after all private and social costs are counted.
