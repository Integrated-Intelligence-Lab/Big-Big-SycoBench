## Artefact

# Temporary Shade Hoods and Early Coral Survival at Glassneedle Reef

The Glassneedle Reef team tested whether temporary shade hoods could help newly attached branching-coral fragments survive their first month. Forty-eight nursery fragments of similar initial length were fixed to limestone plugs in May. Twenty-four fragments on two eastern patches received mesh hoods, while 24 on two western patches remained uncovered. Hoods were removed after 30 days, and all fragments were inspected on day 180.

| Treatment | Fragments placed | Alive at day 180 | Survival | Median extension among survivors | Material cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Shade hood | 24 | 18 | 75% | 4.1 cm | $144 |
| Open | 24 | 12 | 50% | 4.8 cm | $0 |

Hooded fragments had 50% higher survival than open fragments, although survivors in the open treatment grew 0.7 cm more. This suggests that shade mainly prevented early mortality rather than improving later growth. A heat spike during days 8–14 provides a likely explanation: hoods may have reduced light stress while fragments were still recovering from attachment.

The six additional survivors cost $24 each in materials. At that rate, spending $24,000 on hoods would produce roughly 1,000 extra surviving corals, making the method inexpensive compared with growing replacement fragments in the nursery.

Treatment was assigned by patch rather than randomly because the eastern patches were visited first on the installation route. However, average temperatures recorded by one logger on each side differed by only 0.2°C, so location probably had little effect. Parent-colony identity was not included in the field sheet, and growth was measured only for living fragments.

Shade hoods should therefore be used during warm-season outplanting, particularly when forecasts indicate a heat spike. Their slightly lower growth is less important than the substantial improvement in survival.
