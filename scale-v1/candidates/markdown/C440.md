## Artefact

# The Road Home Carried Wool: Backhaul Traffic and Route Choice in the Veyr Uplands, 1296–1342

## Summary

Medieval trade routes are often reconstructed from conspicuous outward cargoes: salt sent inland, wine carried uphill, or imported cloth listed at a town gate. That approach can misidentify why a route remained viable. Using 10,972 usable crossing records from the Veyr Corridor Accounts, this analysis examines the less visible half of trade—the return journey—between the lowland market of Ardin and the upland town of Castrenne from 1296 to 1342.

Two roads connected the towns. The Namar road followed the river and crossed a maintained stone bridge. It was shorter, accommodated carts, and carried most high-value traffic. The Bell Tor road climbed a ridge, imposed lower tolls on pack animals, and is usually described in the accounts as an alternative way during floods or bridge repairs. A third route led east from Castrenne to the Keld fair district.

The records indicate that Bell Tor was more than an emergency bypass. Traffic on the Namar road was strongly imbalanced toward the uplands: 1.47 recorded load-equivalents went uphill for every one coming down. Bell Tor traffic was nearly balanced, at 1.06 to one. Salt, wine, and finished cloth dominated uphill movement, while wool, hides, charcoal, and wooden wares dominated downhill movement. Among 612 high-confidence round trips, 59% included a taxable return cargo. Merchants carrying wool or hides back toward Ardin were especially likely to change from the Namar road on the outward journey to Bell Tor on the return.

Route choice also responded to disruption. During eight periods when the Namar bridge was recorded or inferred to be partly unusable, Namar traffic fell by 63% relative to surrounding weeks, while Bell Tor traffic rose by 71%. Total recorded corridor traffic fell by only 18%. These figures suggest a network with functional redundancy, although the fragmentary accounts cannot establish how much trade was postponed, concealed, or redirected outside the surviving toll system.

The central finding is that route importance depended not just on speed or outward cargo value but on the possibility of finding a paying return load. Bell Tor’s lower pack-animal charges and proximity to wool-producing uplands made it a specialized backhaul route. Measuring only high-value outbound trade would understate its economic role.

## Historical and geographic setting

Ardin stood at the navigable head of the lower Veyr River. Barges arriving there unloaded salt from the western pans, wine, metal vessels, and finer cloth. Castrenne lay approximately 58 road kilometres to the north, beyond a belt of wooded slopes and sheep pasture. No waterway connected the two towns throughout the year.

The Namar road followed the valley for most of its length. Its principal constraint was the Namar bridge, 16 kilometres below Castrenne. The bridge appears repeatedly in toll and repair accounts because its northern approach was vulnerable to flooding. The route’s gentler gradients allowed two- and four-wheeled carts, although winter mud could delay them.

The Bell Tor road was about 11 kilometres longer. It crossed a dry ridge and descended through the hamlets of Pel Ash and Darn Hollow. Carts appear only rarely in its records; most traffic consisted of individual pack animals or strings of two to twelve. The route avoided the Namar bridge and passed close to seasonal wool collection points. Its toll schedule charged by animal rather than by axle and assessed raw wool at a lower rate than the Namar gate.

The Keld spur ran east from Castrenne to three annual fairs and several iron-working settlements. It is included to distinguish through traffic from local redistribution, but the main comparison is between Namar and Bell Tor.

The accounts do not present these routes as a unified transport network. Different institutions collected different dues, and their clerks recorded different facts. Reconstructing journeys therefore requires combining gate tolls, bridge charges, stable purchases, and occasional penalties for avoiding an authorized road.

## Data

### The Veyr Corridor Accounts

The Veyr Corridor Accounts are a transcription assembled from 74 surviving account units covering parts of 19 years between 1296 and 1342. Coverage is discontinuous: 1307–1310 and 1327–1331 are comparatively well represented, while several other years survive only as a fair-month roll or a bridge-repair memorandum.

| Source type | Raw entries | Typical information |
|---|---:|---|
| Gate and bridge toll rolls | 8,946 | Date, direction, carrier mark, conveyance, cargo, charge |
| Stable and fodder accounts | 2,677 | Date, number of animals, sometimes carrier or destination |
| Fair, court, and road-penalty records | 1,223 | Cargo, claimed route, toll avoidance, exceptional movements |
| **Total** | **12,846** | |

I removed 641 clear duplicate entries, principally cases in which a bridge payment was copied into a monthly gate summary. Another 783 records lacked both a date and assignable route, and 450 referred to local movements of less than five kilometres. The analytical file contains 10,972 entries.

A record is an administrative observation, not necessarily a complete journey. One merchant with six mules may appear as one entry, six entries, or not at all if exempt. Clerical practice changed between account units. For this reason, the analysis reports both entry counts and estimated load-equivalents rather than treating every line as an identical shipment.

### Standardizing traffic

I assigned one load-equivalent to a laden pack animal. A two-wheeled cart counts as 3.5 load-equivalents and a four-wheeled cart as 5.5. When only cargo packages were recorded, four bales or six small barrels count as one load-equivalent. These conversions are based on the most common equivalences within the toll schedules themselves. They are not direct measurements of weight. In particular, a load of charcoal occupied more space but probably weighed less than a load of salt.

Empty animals count as crossings but as zero load-equivalents. Entries with an animal count but no statement about whether the animals were laden were assigned 0.5 load-equivalents per animal. This affects 8.7% of the usable entries. The midpoint prevents all ambiguous entries from being treated as either empty or fully laden, but it compresses real variation.

Cargo descriptions were grouped into nine classes: salt; wine and oil; cloth and finished goods; grain and foodstuffs; wool; hides and livestock products; wood, charcoal, and timber; metal and stone; and unknown or mixed. Seventeen percent of load-equivalents fall in the unknown or mixed category.

Direction is described relative to Castrenne. “Upland” means movement from Ardin toward Castrenne; “lowland” means movement toward Ardin. Keld traffic is classified separately as eastbound or westbound.

### Value index

To compare bulky and valuable cargo, I created a declared-value index from the detailed 1321 tariff schedule. Salt is set to 1.0 per standardized load. Wool is 1.6, ordinary cloth 3.8, wine 2.1, charcoal 0.4, and hides 1.3. These are fiscal ratios, not market prices. I apply them across the full period because no equally complete schedule survives for other years. The index is useful for comparing the composition of routes, but it cannot show changes in actual prices over 46 years.

## Methods

The analysis proceeds in four stages.

First, I compare recorded direction balance by route. The direction ratio is upland load-equivalents divided by lowland load-equivalents. A ratio above one indicates a heavier recorded flow toward Castrenne. This is not a full balance-of-trade calculation, since unrecorded empty returns and exemptions may differ by direction.

Second, I link probable outward and return movements. Carrier names are uncommon, but 41% of entries contain a merchant mark, stable token, or repeated abbreviated identifier. A candidate round trip requires the same identifier at an Ardin-side station and a Castrenne-side station within 14 days, followed by a movement in the opposite direction within 21 days. Cargo, animal count, and stated home district are used to resolve collisions. Matches are classified as high-confidence when there is only one possible sequence and the animal count differs by no more than two. There are 612 such round trips. A further 534 ambiguous matches are used only in sensitivity checks.

Third, I estimate a logistic model of Bell Tor rather than Namar route choice. The sample contains 2,684 episodes observed at both an outer gateway and an interior station, making the route reasonably certain. Predictors are direction, broad cargo type, winter month, fair week, cart use, and Namar bridge disruption. Standard errors are clustered by account-year. The model describes associations among observed trips; it does not include toll evaders or journeys abandoned before reaching the second station.

Finally, I compare traffic during disruption windows. Six bridge problems are explicitly dated in repair or court accounts. Two additional disruptions are inferred where bridge receipts cease for at least ten days, repair purchases occur, and Bell Tor stable use rises in the same period. Each disruption window is compared with the preceding and following 30 days, excluding major fair weeks.

## Results

### 1. Route volume and direction balance

The Namar road carried the largest share of recorded traffic by every measure. It accounted for 47% of usable entries, 54% of load-equivalents, and 62% of the declared-value index. Bell Tor carried 32% of entries but only 28% of load-equivalents, reflecting its reliance on smaller pack trains. Its value share was lower still because it carried relatively little wine and fine cloth.

| Route | Usable entries | Load-equivalents | Primary direction load | Opposite direction load | Direction ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Namar road | 5,122 | 9,840 | 5,850 upland | 3,990 lowland | 1.47 |
| Bell Tor road | 3,476 | 5,094 | 2,620 upland | 2,474 lowland | 1.06 |
| Keld spur | 2,374 | 3,166 | 1,730 eastbound | 1,436 westbound | 1.20 |
| **Total** | **10,972** | **18,100** | | | |

Namar’s imbalance is present in 15 of the 17 account-years with records in both directions. Bell Tor is more variable but remains much closer to balance. In five years, it carried slightly more load downhill than uphill.

The difference is not simply an effect of carts. Removing all cart entries lowers Namar’s direction ratio from 1.47 to 1.39, while Bell Tor remains at 1.05. Nor is it wholly generated by records assigned half-loads: excluding ambiguous load status produces ratios of 1.51 and 1.08 respectively.

Stable accounts point to more animal movement than the toll rolls alone record. At Darn Hollow, on Bell Tor, fodder was purchased for approximately 14% more animals than can be matched to toll entries. At the Namar bridge the discrepancy is about 7%. This may indicate greater toll avoidance or more exempt traffic on Bell Tor. It does not erase the contrast in direction balance, but it makes the precise ratios less secure.

### 2. Cargoes moved in opposite directions

Cargo composition shows a pronounced directional division.

| Commodity group | Share moving upland | Route carrying largest share |
|---|---:|---|
| Salt | 72% | Namar |
| Wine and oil | 81% | Namar |
| Cloth and finished goods | 64% | Namar |
| Grain and foodstuffs | 55% | Namar |
| Wool | 21% | Bell Tor |
| Hides and livestock products | 29% | Bell Tor |
| Wood, charcoal, and timber | 32% | Bell Tor |
| Metal and stone | 47% | Keld spur |

The “route carrying largest share” column refers to the leading route for that commodity in either direction, not necessarily a majority of all movement. Bell Tor carried 48% of recorded wool load-equivalents and 44% of hides despite carrying only 28% of total load. Namar carried 69% of wine and oil and 63% of finished cloth.

Raw wool is the clearest backhaul commodity. It moved downhill in all seasons but peaked between late May and early August. During those months, Bell Tor’s downhill traffic exceeded its uphill traffic by 28%. Charcoal created a smaller winter backhaul flow, particularly after pack animals had delivered salt or grain to Castrenne.

The declared-value index produces a different map from load volume. Namar’s cargo value per load-equivalent is 1.38 times Bell Tor’s. Yet Bell Tor’s average animal utilization is higher: among entries that state both animal count and load status, 76% of Bell Tor animals are laden, compared with 68% on Namar. A plausible explanation is that low-value return loads were worth carrying on Bell Tor because its animal toll was lower. It is also possible that Namar clerks recorded empty animals more consistently.

### 3. Reconstructed round trips

Of the 612 high-confidence round trips, 361, or 59%, have a taxable cargo in both directions. Another 143 appear to return empty, while 108 have no usable return-cargo description. The median interval between first outward and final return observations is nine days.

Route switching is common. Overall, 188 round trips, or 31%, use a different road on the return. Switching is concentrated among loaded returns:

- 38% of round trips with a recorded return cargo change route.
- 19% of apparently empty returns change route.
- Among outward salt or wine carriers returning with wool or hides, 42% travel upland by Namar and lowland by Bell Tor.
- Among outward salt or wine carriers with an apparently empty return, only 17% make that same Namar-to-Bell switch.

The reverse pattern—Bell Tor outward and Namar homeward—is less common, occurring in 11% of matched trips. It appears mainly among carriers who collect finished cloth or metal goods in Castrenne rather than raw upland produce.

Travel time alone would not predict this behavior. For 417 matches with dates at both endpoints, the median one-way transit is three days on Namar and four on Bell Tor. The extra day on Bell Tor was apparently acceptable for some loaded return journeys. The accounts suggest two reasons: lower tolls on raw wool and direct access to collection points west of Castrenne. They do not contain wages or fodder quantities at sufficient resolution to calculate the full cost of the added day.

Matching may exaggerate switching if two carriers used the same merchant mark. Requiring an exact animal-count match reduces the round-trip sample to 389 but leaves the loaded-return switching rate at 35%. Including all 1,146 high- and lower-confidence matches reduces it to 33%. The association remains, though the exact percentage is uncertain.

### 4. Seasonal route use

Bell Tor’s share of traffic changed sharply through the year.

| Period | Bell Tor share of Namar–Bell load-equivalents |
|---|---:|
| April–September, excluding fairs | 31% |
| October–November | 26% |
| December–February | 18% |
| March | 24% |
| Namar disruption windows | 44% |

The winter decline is consistent with snow and exposed ridge conditions. Bell Tor was not an all-weather substitute. Its summer share rises with wool movement, while the Namar road remains dominant for carts and fragile containers.

Fair weeks complicate this pattern. The Keld fairs drew animals east from Castrenne, and some carriers appear to have made triangular journeys: Ardin to Castrenne by Namar, Castrenne to Keld, and Keld toward Ardin by Bell Tor. Because Keld records often identify destination only as “west,” these triangles cannot be reconstructed reliably. Excluding the six best-documented fair weeks lowers the estimated route-switching rate by about three percentage points.

### 5. Factors associated with choosing Bell Tor

The logistic model confirms several descriptive patterns. Odds ratios greater than one indicate a higher likelihood of using Bell Tor rather than Namar.

| Predictor | Odds ratio | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Namar bridge disruption | 2.70 | 2.08–3.50 |
| Lowland direction | 1.34 | 1.17–1.54 |
| Wool, hides, or charcoal cargo | 1.48 | 1.25–1.76 |
| Winter month | 0.54 | 0.43–0.68 |
| Cart rather than pack animals | 0.19 | 0.13–0.28 |
| High-value cargo group | 0.71 | 0.59–0.86 |
| Keld fair week | 1.16 | 0.93–1.44 |

The strongest positive association is bridge disruption. Bulky backhaul cargo and lowland direction also independently predict Bell Tor use. The fair-week estimate is imprecise and could be consistent with little change.

The model’s observations are not a random sample of all potential journeys. They are journeys visible at two points, and easier-to-document merchants may have had different route preferences. Month indicators also do not capture daily rainfall or snow. Consequently, the odds ratios should not be interpreted as causal effects of tolls or bridge condition.

### 6. Disruption and network redundancy

Across the eight disruption windows, recorded Namar load-equivalents fall by 63% relative to adjacent comparison periods. Bell Tor load-equivalents rise by 71%, and Keld traffic changes little. Total Namar–Bell corridor traffic falls by 18%.

The response is immediate in the six explicitly dated cases: Bell Tor stable use rises within three days of the first bridge restriction. The increase is strongest for pack-animal salt loads and weakest for carts, which could not easily use the ridge. Once bridge receipts resume, Bell Tor traffic usually returns toward its seasonal level within one or two weeks.

A simple comparison of lost Namar traffic with additional Bell Tor traffic suggests that Bell Tor absorbed approximately half of the recorded shortfall. The remainder may represent delayed journeys, reduced trade, or movement through unobserved local paths. This estimate assumes the weeks around each disruption provide a valid baseline. That assumption is doubtful in two cases where heavy rain probably affected both demand and road conditions.

The redundancy was therefore partial and cargo-specific. Bell Tor could preserve the movement of packable goods, especially salt and wool, but not the full cart traffic of the valley road. Calling it an emergency route alone would nevertheless be misleading: even outside disruptions, it carried over a quarter of corridor load and a disproportionate share of loaded returns.

## Robustness checks

Three alternative constructions leave the main route contrast intact.

First, counting each administrative entry equally rather than using load-equivalents gives upland-to-lowland ratios of 1.40 for Namar and 1.03 for Bell Tor. Second, excluding the 1327–1331 records, which form the largest continuous block, gives ratios of 1.43 and 1.10. Third, assigning ambiguous animals either zero or one full load puts Namar’s ratio between 1.42 and 1.53 and Bell Tor’s between 1.02 and 1.11.

The declared-value result is less stable. If wool is assigned twice the 1321 fiscal value rather than 1.6 times the value of salt, Bell Tor’s share of corridor value rises from 22% to 27%. If wine is reduced from 2.1 to 1.5, Namar’s value advantage also narrows. The conclusion that Namar carried more declared value survives, but the exact division depends heavily on one tariff schedule.

The disruption finding is sensitive to the two inferred bridge closures. Removing them reduces the estimated Bell Tor increase from 71% to 58% and increases the total corridor decline from 18% to 23%. The qualitative pattern remains, but the larger estimate should not be treated as a precise measure of diverted trade.

## Limitations

Survival is the largest problem. Only 19 years are represented, and the surviving years cluster around repair disputes and unusually complete administrative periods. The dataset may therefore overrepresent disruption. It cannot establish a smooth trend over 1296–1342.

Toll records also omit exempt travelers and encourage evasion. The stable accounts show that the omission is probably greater on Bell Tor. If untolled downhill wool traffic was especially common, Bell Tor’s true backhaul role would be larger than estimated. If empty animals were omitted more often, its apparent utilization rate would instead be overstated. The available records do not distinguish these possibilities.

The load-equivalent conversion combines weight, volume, and fiscal categories. It is preferable to counting a cart and a mule as identical, but the factors of 3.5 and 5.5 remain approximate. Likewise, applying the 1321 tariff ratios across nearly half a century ignores changing scarcity and administrative valuation.

Round-trip linkage depends on marks that were neither unique nor consistently recorded. The high-confidence criteria reduce false links at the cost of favoring regular merchants with stable identifiers. Occasional carriers, poorer traders, and those operating outside formal markets are likely underrepresented.

Finally, the mapped routes are broader corridors. A Bell Tor entry does not prove that every carrier followed the same path through Darn Hollow, and some may have joined the Namar road above the bridge. The analysis can distinguish toll regimes more confidently than exact tracks on the ground.

## Conclusion

The Veyr evidence changes the interpretation of a secondary upland road. Bell Tor was slower, hostile to carts, and less important by declared cargo value. On those measures alone, it appears subordinate to the Namar road. Yet its traffic was more evenly balanced by direction, its animals were more often recorded as laden, and its carriers disproportionately returned with wool, hides, or charcoal. Merchants frequently used the two roads as parts of one round trip rather than choosing a single route for both legs.

This pattern makes economic sense without requiring Bell Tor to outperform the valley road. A carrier who delivered salt or wine by the faster Namar route could collect wool near Castrenne and return by the cheaper ridge toll. The resulting network was asymmetric: Namar specialized in access and higher-value upland supply, while Bell Tor specialized partly in extraction and backhaul.

The bridge disruptions reveal an additional benefit. Bell Tor could not replace carts or eliminate losses, but it maintained a substantial portion of pack traffic when the principal crossing failed. Its routine role in return journeys likely kept animals, guides, stables, and toll administration available for those emergencies.

Medieval route importance should therefore be measured over the whole commercial circuit. Outward cargo value identifies the prominent road; return loading and route switching identify why a less prominent road continued to matter.
