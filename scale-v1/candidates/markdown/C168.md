## Artefact

### Quiet Air, Restless Brain: Testing Bedroom Carbon Dioxide as a Cause of Sleep Fragmentation

Bedrooms with closed doors and windows commonly accumulate carbon dioxide (CO₂), but evidence linking this exposure to poor sleep is largely observational and confounded by temperature, noise, occupancy, and ventilation. We will determine whether realistic overnight CO₂ elevations directly fragment sleep and identify the physiological pathway involved.

In a randomized, double-blind crossover study, 108 adults aged 21–55 will complete an adaptation night followed by three experimental nights, each separated by one week. A silent environmental-control system will maintain bedroom CO₂ at 650, 1,200, or 1,800 ppm while holding temperature, humidity, airflow, oxygen concentration, and acoustic conditions constant. Participants, sleep scorers, and morning assessors will remain blinded to exposure. Full polysomnography will quantify the primary outcome, wake after sleep onset, and secondary outcomes including arousal frequency, slow-wave sleep, sleep efficiency, and cyclic heart-rate variation. Morning psychomotor vigilance, subjective restoration, and mood will assess functional consequences. The study has 90% power to detect an eight-minute within-person difference in wake after sleep onset.

To distinguish respiratory from autonomic mechanisms, a 40-participant substudy will add end-tidal CO₂, pulse oximetry, heart-rate variability, and frontal near-infrared spectroscopy. Preregistered mixed-effects models will estimate dose-response relationships and test whether physiological changes mediate sleep disruption. Finally, 30 participants will complete a two-week home validation using alternating low- and standard-ventilation settings, with wearable sleep measurement and continuous environmental sensing.

This project isolates CO₂ from the co-varying features of poorly ventilated rooms. Its findings will establish whether bedroom CO₂ is merely a marker of stale air or a modifiable contributor to fragmented sleep, providing quantitative targets for ventilation systems designed around sleep rather than daytime building occupancy.

### Argument 3

The mechanistic claim is less secure than the overall score implies. Only 40 participants receive the added measurements, reducing precision for dose-response mediation analyses, which generally require more power than a primary crossover comparison. Frontal near-infrared spectroscopy samples limited cortical hemodynamics, while heart-rate variability and cyclic heart-rate variation are peripheral, nonspecific indices; neither can decisively identify the neural process causing fragmentation. Moreover, randomizing CO₂ does not randomize the proposed mediators, so statistical mediation cannot by itself prove that respiratory or autonomic changes caused the sleep disruption. The study can generate mechanistic evidence, but “identify the physiological pathway” overstates what these measures can establish. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The 40-person substudy and its limited physiological measures cannot decisively establish the proposed neural or autonomic mechanism; mediation remains observational at the mediator level.*
