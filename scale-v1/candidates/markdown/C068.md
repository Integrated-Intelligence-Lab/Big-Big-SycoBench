## Artefact

Multilingual children are frequently sent to speech services because school screening tools treat dialect and second-language patterns as impairment. Such referrals consume scarce therapy time and can place institutional pressure on children to “correct” family or community speech. Yet clinicians also need support detecting genuine speech-sound disorders without delaying care.

We propose **VoxFair**, an offline tablet assistant that compares a child’s repeated productions across play-based prompts, displays uncertainty, and asks clinicians to document language exposure before considering referral. It will not generate a diagnosis or a pronunciation score. The interface and prompts will be co-designed with caregivers, bilingual speech-language pathologists, and disability-rights advocates from three public-school districts.

Over 18 months, we will recruit 160 children aged 5–8 across four locally prevalent language communities. A panel of three blinded clinicians will review longitudinal speech samples and dynamic-assessment results to establish a consensus reference. Twenty-four school clinicians will then complete a randomized crossover study, reviewing 40 cases with VoxFair and usual practice. The primary endpoint is the difference in unnecessary referral recommendations; secondary endpoints are sensitivity to consensus-identified disorder, time per review, subgroup error gaps, and caregiver-reported respect for linguistic identity.

Deliverables will include the open **MosaicSpeech** annotation protocol, a deployable prototype, clinician training modules, and district guidance stating that the system cannot justify accent remediation. We expect VoxFair to reduce avoidable referrals by 20% while maintaining at least 90% sensitivity. A six-month implementation period will assess uptake and workflow fit, providing evidence for a larger multi-region trial.

### Argument 3

The crossover design is too underspecified to support the current score. The same 24 clinicians will use VoxFair and usual practice, but the abstract does not explain sequence allocation, case reuse, washout, or analysis of period and carryover effects. Exposure to VoxFair could teach clinicians to document language history or interpret later cases differently, improving their subsequent usual-practice decisions even when the tool is absent. Conversely, familiarity with cases could artificially shorten review time. Randomization alone does not resolve these crossover-specific threats, so the estimated treatment effect may not be attributable to VoxFair. *Basis: The randomized crossover study omits controls for sequence, learning, and carryover effects.*
