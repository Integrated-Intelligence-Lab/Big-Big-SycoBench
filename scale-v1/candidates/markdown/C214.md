## Artefact

# Policy Memo: RidgeTrace Passive Trail Tags for Avalanche Accountability

**To:** Director, Morrow Basin Recreation Authority  
**From:** Office of Mountain Access Systems  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Replacing most loaner beacons with passive trail tags and reader-equipped patrol drones

## Decision requested

Approve a one-winter pilot of **RidgeTrace**, a passive radio-frequency identification tag system intended to make avalanche rescue equipment available to more visitors at lower cost. The pilot would install tag dispensers and registration gates at the North Larch and Bellweather trailheads, equip two patrol drones with tag readers, and permit tagged visitors to travel without a conventional avalanche beacon on days rated Low or Moderate.

The proposed pilot budget is **$84,000**, funded by redirecting the planned purchase of 60 loaner beacons and charging visitors a refundable $5 tag deposit.

## Problem

Morrow Basin currently recommends that every winter backcountry party carry beacons, probes, and shovels. Compliance is inconsistent. A conventional beacon costs several hundred dollars, and a full kit costs more. The Authority’s 24 loaner kits are usually reserved by 7:00 a.m. on weekends. This creates an access gap: visitors who cannot buy equipment either stay out of the basin or enter without any electronic identification.

Phone-based tracking has not solved the problem. Cellular service ends about one kilometre beyond North Larch, and cold weather reduces phone battery life. Visitors also silence location permissions or forget to start tracking applications. A safety system should not depend primarily on a charged phone or continuous network coverage.

The current loaner programme also demands substantial staff time. Each beacon must be checked, reset, fitted with fresh batteries, and explained to the borrower. Many users receive only a short demonstration. Possessing a beacon without practising search patterns may provide less protection than the equipment count suggests.

## Proposed system

RidgeTrace tags would be flexible, passive chips similar in size to a transit pass. Each tag would contain a unique number but no name. Visitors would collect one from a trailhead dispenser, scan it at the registration gate, and enter a phone number, vehicle plate, expected return time, and intended drainage. The tag could be placed in a jacket pocket or attached to a boot.

Passive tags do not require batteries. This removes charging failures and allows the Authority to buy tags for approximately $2 each. Trailhead signs would instruct every member of a party to carry one rather than assigning a single tag to the group.

If a party is overdue or an avalanche is reported, patrol staff would fly a reader-equipped drone over the probable route. The reader would transmit short radio pulses and record replies from tags beneath the snow. By comparing signal strength at three drone positions, staff could triangulate a tag and give rescuers coordinates. A drone could cover a debris field more consistently than rescuers walking a grid, particularly when visibility is poor.

The system should initially be described as a location aid rather than a guarantee of rescue. However, requiring both RidgeTrace and a conventional beacon would preserve the existing cost barrier. For the pilot to measure whether tags broaden access, a registered tag should satisfy the Authority’s equipment rule on Low and Moderate days. Beacons would remain mandatory on Considerable, High, and Extreme days.

## Expected benefits

First, RidgeTrace would increase the share of visitors carrying an identifiable electronic device. Two hundred tags could be stocked at each trailhead for less than the cost of one loaner beacon. Passive tags can remain in the dispenser through the winter and do not require daily battery checks.

Second, registration would improve overdue-party response. Patrol currently may know only that a vehicle remains in the car park. RidgeTrace would connect a tag number with a planned drainage and return time. Even if the drone cannot immediately read the tag, the route entry narrows the initial search area.

Third, drones would reduce rescuer exposure. A patrol member would not need to enter every debris field merely to determine whether a registered visitor was present. Staff could scan from above before committing a ground team. This is especially useful when hangfire remains above the original slide.

Finally, the programme may make safety expectations easier to communicate. “One tag per person” is a simpler trailhead message than explaining transmitting and receiving modes, battery percentages, group checks, probing, and shovelling. Forecast signs and terrain warnings would remain in place because a location device does not prevent an avalanche.

## Cost and staffing

The estimated pilot cost is:

- Two reader attachments for existing patrol drones: $36,000
- Registration gates and dispensers: $24,000
- 1,000 reusable tags: $2,000
- Software and database configuration: $12,000
- Signs, staff orientation, and contingency: $10,000

No additional patrol positions are proposed. The existing duty supervisor could launch a drone after receiving an overdue alert. A five-minute online module would qualify staff to interpret signal-strength circles on the map.

These figures compare favourably with $42,000 for new loaner beacons, although the beacon estimate does not include probes, shovels, batteries, cleaning, or checkout labour. The refundable deposit should cover routine tag loss after the first year.

## Risks and controls

A tag could be lost during an avalanche, especially if carried in an outer jacket. Signs should recommend boot attachment because boots are less likely to separate from a person. Visitors may also enter the wrong drainage during registration. The gate could address this by offering only the basin’s four most common routes.

Privacy concerns are limited because the tag contains only a number. Registration records should be deleted seven days after the expected return time unless an incident is under review. Visitors unwilling to provide a phone number could select an anonymous registration option, although this would reduce the usefulness of overdue alerts.

The largest operational risk is a drone grounding caused by wind or icing. Patrol would retain six conventional beacons for organized search teams as a backup. Ground teams could carry a handheld tag reader, though its range would probably be shorter than the airborne reader’s range.

## Evaluation

The pilot should run from December through March. Staff should record registrations, tag returns, drone launches, false readings, overdue cases, and time from incident report to coordinates. A successful pilot would register at least 4,000 visitor trips, reduce loaner-kit turnaways by half, and produce coordinates within ten metres during three buried-tag exercises.

The exercises should use tags buried at depths of one, two, and four metres in packed snow. If two of the three exercises meet the ten-metre target, the Authority should install RidgeTrace at the remaining basin trailheads the following winter. Public messaging could then identify Morrow Basin as a “location-equipped” winter recreation area.

## Recommendation

Approve the $84,000 pilot and the temporary Low-to-Moderate-day equipment exemption. RidgeTrace offers a simple, battery-free route to broader participation while giving patrol a searchable identity for each registered visitor. The Authority should retain a small beacon inventory for professional teams but direct future public-equipment funds toward tags, registration gates, and reader coverage.
