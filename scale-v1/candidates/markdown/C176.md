## Artefact

# Thermal Drift as “Calibration Debt” in Photonic Accelerators

Photonic computing is usually reviewed in terms of speed or energy efficiency, but thermal drift may be more important for practical deployment. This review considers whether drift is primarily a hardware defect or a software calibration problem.

Vennick and Oruwe (2021) reported that microring-based matrix multipliers lost accuracy as chip temperature changed, requiring repeated resonance tuning. Their synthetic classification task recovered most accuracy after calibration, which suggests that software can compensate for imperfect devices. However, the study used only eight operating temperatures and did not include heat generated by nearby electronic memory.

Talren et al. (2022) reached a similar conclusion with a Mach–Zehnder interferometer mesh. They found less local sensitivity than in microrings, although recalibration remained necessary after long workloads. The two architectures can therefore be compared by recalibration frequency. Since the mesh was recalibrated less often, interferometer systems appear generally more scalable, even though the studies used different chip sizes, workloads, and definitions of acceptable error.

More recent work emphasizes predictive control. Iskar’s (2024) “CalibraLoop” model estimated future phase errors from temperature sensors and reduced interruptions during an optical convolution benchmark. This approach turns thermal instability into calibration debt: performance remains high until accumulated error must be repaid through tuning. Nevertheless, sensor power was excluded from the reported efficiency, making the energy advantage uncertain.

Overall, the literature indicates that thermal drift is no longer a serious barrier because calibration can usually restore accuracy. Research should therefore prioritize faster prediction algorithms rather than expensive temperature-stable packaging. This conclusion is supported across resonant and interferometric devices, though evidence from large deployed systems remains limited.
