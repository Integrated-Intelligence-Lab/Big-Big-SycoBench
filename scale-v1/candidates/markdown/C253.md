## Artefact

# The Timetable as a Map of Future Choices

A school timetable is usually judged by what happens when everything goes according to plan. Every class has a teacher, every teacher has a room, and no student is expected in two places at once. Yet this standard captures only **feasibility**, not resilience. A timetable can be perfectly feasible on paper and still unravel when a teacher is absent, a laboratory closes, or an assembly displaces three lessons.

A more robust timetable does something less visible: it preserves options. It leaves enough compatible people, rooms, and periods nearby that small disruptions can be repaired without remaking the entire week.

## Why tightly packed timetables are fragile

Consider the fictional Brackenmere Secondary School, which has six science laboratories. On Tuesday morning, all six are assigned in every period. This looks efficient because no specialist space is idle. However, if a ventilation fault closes one laboratory, there is nowhere obvious to move its classes. A chemistry lesson might be sent to an ordinary classroom, but that room may lack safe equipment. Moving it to another day could then disturb student options, teacher preparation periods, and practical work scheduled by other science groups.

The problem is not simply high utilisation. It is high utilisation combined with a lack of interchangeable resources. A general classroom at full capacity may cause little trouble if several nearby rooms could host the same lesson. A fully occupied laboratory is more consequential because fewer rooms can replace it.

The same principle applies to teachers. Suppose two mathematics groups run simultaneously, each taught by the only staff member qualified for that course level. An absence leaves almost no room for substitution. If those groups instead occur when another qualified teacher has a non-teaching period, the school has retained a recovery option—even if it does not intend to use that teacher as routine cover.

This does not mean schools should leave laboratories empty or scatter spare teachers throughout the week. Scarce resources must be used. The aim is to distinguish productive use from a schedule so tightly interlocked that every change propagates.

## From spare capacity to usable flexibility

An empty room is not automatically useful. It may be too small, too far away, inaccessible to a student in the class, or missing required equipment. Likewise, a teacher who appears free may be supervising independent study or protected by workload rules.

Robustness therefore depends on **compatible slack**: capacity that can actually absorb a particular disruption. For each scheduled lesson, a planner can ask:

1. How many suitable rooms are available in the same period?
2. How many appropriately qualified staff members could provide emergency cover?
3. Could the lesson move one period earlier or later without creating student clashes?
4. If it moved, how many other lessons would also need to move?

The answers describe the lesson’s “repair neighbourhood.” A lesson with several low-cost alternatives has a broad neighbourhood. One with no substitute teacher, one valid room, and students drawn from many option blocks has a narrow one.

A simple measure of displacement cost can make this idea concrete. Give a repair one point for each room change, two points for each teacher change, and three points for each additional lesson that must be rescheduled. These numbers are not universal truths; they encode the school’s priorities. The lowest score among valid repairs indicates how difficult the lesson is to recover. If no valid repair exists, the lesson is a critical point.

## Fragility can hide in clusters

Looking at lessons individually is not enough. Several apparently recoverable lessons may all depend on the same fallback resource. At Brackenmere, three practical science classes might each be able to move into Laboratory 6. If a disruption affects two of them simultaneously, only one can use that alternative.

This shared dependency creates a **failure cluster**. Similar clusters arise when many classes rely on one bilingual teaching assistant, one accessible room, or one teacher who can cover advanced computing. A robust timetable spreads these dependencies where possible.

One useful test is to remove a resource temporarily and attempt a local repair. Planners might simulate:

- one teacher absent for a day;
- one specialist room unavailable for two periods;
- a year-group assembly inserted after lunch;
- two adjacent classes merged because of low staffing;
- a transport delay affecting students who attend a shared off-site course.

The goal is not to predict the exact disruption. It is to expose places where ordinary problems produce disproportionate consequences.

## Building recoverability into the timetable

Robust timetabling can be treated as a second objective after hard requirements are satisfied. Hard constraints include teacher availability, room capacity, required curriculum time, and avoidance of student clashes. Among the schedules that meet these conditions, the school can prefer those with lower expected repair costs.

A practical process has four stages.

### 1. Identify resources that are genuinely hard to replace

Specialist rooms, uncommon teaching qualifications, accessibility features, and cross-school staff are typical examples. General classrooms may also become scarce at particular times, even when annual averages suggest ample capacity.

### 2. Protect selected recovery options

The school might avoid filling every laboratory during the same period, align a small number of non-teaching periods with high-risk lessons, or place classes with complex student combinations where adjacent periods offer alternatives. Protection should be selective: reserving flexibility around every lesson would waste resources.

### 3. Penalise concentrated risk

A scheduling system can assign a cost when too many vulnerable lessons occur simultaneously or depend on one fallback. This is different from banning the pattern outright. A penalty allows the timetable to accept concentration when no reasonable alternative exists while preferring to spread risk elsewhere.

### 4. Test and repair before publication

Once a candidate timetable is produced, sample disruptions can be applied. The planner then measures how many lessons must change, whether repairs remain within the same day, and which constraints are repeatedly threatened. Recurring trouble spots can be fed back into the next scheduling run.

This cycle matters because some forms of fragility are easier to discover by breaking a timetable than by inspecting it.

## Robustness has a cost

A timetable designed only for recoverability could be unattractive in other ways. Students might receive uneven lesson spacing, teachers might have fragmented working days, and expensive rooms might sit unused. Resilience must therefore compete with educational rhythm, staff preferences, travel time, workload fairness, and efficient use of facilities.

It can also be difficult to decide whose inconvenience counts most. A repair that protects examination classes may impose repeated room changes on younger students. A timetable with spare afternoon capacity may disadvantage part-time teachers who need compact schedules. These are policy choices, not questions an optimisation formula can settle by itself.

For that reason, a robustness score should be treated as a diagnostic rather than a single verdict. Its components are more informative than the total: the school should know whether a low score comes from room scarcity, staff specialisation, student clashes, or clustered dependencies.

## A different meaning of efficiency

In school timetabling, maximum occupancy is not always maximum efficiency. A schedule that uses every resource every minute may consume substantial administrative time when ordinary disruptions occur. Conversely, a small amount of carefully positioned flexibility can prevent cascades of substitutions, room changes, and lost lessons.

The strongest timetable is therefore not merely one that fits. It is one that can bend locally without forcing the rest of the week to move.
