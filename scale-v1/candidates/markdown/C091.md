## Artefact

# The Repair Line as Language Infrastructure

**Aim.** Avenki is heard in Marrowport’s publicly owned housing towers but is largely absent from maintenance systems, where tenants must use Dorran. This project asks whether allowing residents to request essential repairs in Avenki increases everyday language use and improves service parity. It tests preservation as civic utility, not only cultural documentation.

**Design.** Over 18 months, 12 towers—about 240 households—will enter a stepped-wedge pilot at three-month intervals. Each receives an Avenki voice-note and text channel staffed by paid bilingual residents, while existing Dorran routes remain available. Before and after rollout, researchers will collect channel choice, acknowledgement and repair times, repeat use, and code-switching rates. Surveys and interviews with adolescents, adults, and elders will examine confidence using Avenki for technical matters and whether repair vocabulary travels between generations. Not-yet-enrolled towers provide contemporaneous comparisons; models will adjust for building condition and request type.

**Outputs and governance.** With separate consent, selected requests will feed a community-owned glossary for plumbing, heating, and electrical faults. A tenant council will set access rules, approve quotations, and may withhold terms judged private or politically risky. Participants will be paid; raw audio will be deleted after 30 days; immigration status will not be collected. The city housing office will receive aggregate findings only.

**Evaluation.** The primary outcome is the share of requests made partly or wholly in Avenki six months after launch. Secondary outcomes are service-time gaps and self-reported cross-generational vocabulary use. Rollout order will follow operational readiness rather than random assignment, so causal estimates may retain selection bias. Additional bilingual staffing may itself speed repairs, making language access hard to isolate. Eighteen months also cannot establish long-term language survival. Even so, the study can show whether an essential public service becomes a practical site of linguistic participation rather than another pressure to assimilate.

### Argument 2

A lower score is warranted because the intervention does not isolate the mechanism it claims to investigate. Towers receive both an Avenki channel and additional paid bilingual residents who staff it. Those staff could improve intake accuracy, tenant trust, routing, follow-up, or response speed independently of whether requests are made in Avenki. Because the existing Dorran routes do not receive an equivalent staffing enhancement, even strong before-and-after service gains would identify the effect of a package, not language access itself. Statistical adjustment cannot disentangle components that always arrive together. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Language access is bundled with paid bilingual staffing, so observed service improvements cannot be attributed to language choice alone.*

### Argument 3

The stepped rollout looks stronger than it is because entry depends on operational readiness rather than random assignment. Readiness may track management competence, tenant organization, existing bilingual capacity, or other factors that also affect channel adoption and repair performance. The stated adjustments for building condition and request type do not address all of those differences. Moreover, three-month rollout intervals expose comparisons to seasonal repair demand and broader changes in housing-office performance, yet no calendar-time specification is described. Not-yet-enrolled towers therefore may not provide a credible counterfactual, materially weakening causal interpretation. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Operational-readiness sequencing is endogenous and may be confounded with tower capacity, tenant organization, and calendar-time effects.*
