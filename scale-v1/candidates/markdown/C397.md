## Artefact

# The Bell at Mooring 397

At four in the morning, when the tide turned under Lathwater, every floating house changed address.

The houses did not travel far. Their iron guides slid along chains fixed to the harbour floor, and the eastern rows swung north while the western rows rotated towards the old sea wall. Nevertheless, a kitchen that had faced the bakery at midnight might face a net-mender's porch by breakfast. Children crossed to school over bridges that had not existed the evening before. Neighbours became strangers and then neighbours again.

Ena Marr had grown up believing all towns behaved this way. Only when she left to study epidemics at the Inland Institute did she discover that most streets stayed where surveyors put them.

She returned to Lathwater during the first week of lantern fever.

A customs skiff carried her past shuttered tea stalls and gardens planted in blue barrels. The harbour smelled of tar, fennel and the green mud exposed beneath the pontoons. On several decks, strips of yellow cloth warned visitors away. Ena counted eleven strips before the skiff reached the infirmary.

Director Halden Crowe met her at the landing. He was a wide, sleepless man wearing a rain cape over striped bedclothes.

"Thirty-one confirmed cases," he said before greeting her. "Eighteen yesterday, thirteen since midnight. Two patients cannot stand. No deaths, but the whole Fold thinks breathing harbour fog causes it. Half the fish market has fled upriver."

"How are cases confirmed?"

"Fever, dry cough and halos around lamps. That last symptom is why they named it."

"Laboratory test?"

Crowe looked offended. "Our laboratory washed away in spring."

He led Ena into the infirmary's chart room. A paper map of Lathwater covered one wall. Red pins marked patients' homes. They formed an impressive cluster around the central market.

"There," Crowe said. "The source."

Ena did not answer. The map showed where the houses had been on the morning the pins were placed, not where they had been when their occupants became infected. By tomorrow's tide, the cluster would stretch into a crescent. By the next, it might look like two outbreaks.

"I need the mooring ledger," she said. "And the ferry scans, bridge changes, market rosters, water deliveries and symptom times. Not admission times."

Crowe blinked. "All that before you tell me whether to close the harbour?"

"Especially before that."

This answer sounded firmer than Ena felt. At the institute she had built models from clean tables with rectangular columns. Lathwater offered notebooks swollen by damp, ferry tickets used as shopping lists and a harbour clerk who recorded the tide by drawing either a happy or a disappointed moon.

Her father was waiting beside the ledger cabinet.

Oris Marr had piloted the cross-harbour ferry for thirty-four years. His beard had gone white, but his hands still appeared carved from the same dark rope as the mooring lines. He embraced Ena awkwardly, as though she were luggage he had been warned was fragile.

"You came with numbers," he said.

"That is what they asked for."

"Last fever, the numbers said close the south pontoons. Your mother was on the north pontoon."

"I was sixteen. They weren't my numbers."

"Numbers don't remember whose they were."

Crowe cleared his throat and left them with the ledgers.

Ena opened the newest volume. Each vessel had a row, each mooring a number, and each tide a column. At least this part was careful. A house might be at 118 during one tide, 204 during the next and 397 after that. Public bridges were recorded with green ink. Shared water hoses were blue. Ferry stops were black triangles.

Static maps were useless here, but contacts might still be reconstructed. Ena began copying.

By noon, she had covered the chart-room floor with cards. Each card represented a household. She joined cards with coloured string whenever two households had shared a bridge, ferry bench or adjacent mooring during the previous eight days. The resulting web climbed across chairs and snagged on the door handle.

Crowe stepped over it with a tray of soup.

"Is that the model?"

"It is the part I can see."

"And the part you can't?"

"Almost everything important."

For a moment, this honesty pleased him. Then he remembered that the harbour council expected a forecast by sunset.

## The Number

Ena entered the records into her institute calculator. She labelled households susceptible, exposed, infectious or recovered, though she had no reliable way to distinguish exposed people from susceptible ones. She set the incubation period at two days because the first three patients remembered feeling ill two days after the Lantern Fair. A fourth patient said five days, so Ena marked his account uncertain and left it out.

She divided the thirty-one cases by the previous day's eighteen, adjusted for the estimated length of infectiousness, and obtained a basic reproduction number of 2.73.

"Each patient creates exactly two point seven three more patients," Crowe announced when Ena showed him the result.

"On average," she corrected.

"Exactly on average, then."

The council gathered in the net loft. Its seven members sat far apart, although they continued passing the same kettle from hand to hand. Ena stood before a chalkboard and explained compartments, transmission rates and contact networks. She drew four boxes joined by arrows.

"People begin in the susceptible box," she said. "After contact, they enter the exposed box. Then infectious, then recovered. If we reduce the links between households, the epidemic should shrink."

Councillor Venn, who owned the floating cinema, raised his hand. "Which box is my wife in? She coughed once at breakfast."

"I can't tell you."

"What is the use of boxes that can't hold a person?"

No one laughed, and Ena suspected it was not a joke.

The council wanted a single action. Close the market, stop the ferries, untie the Fold and tow every house into open water—anything that could be stated on a notice. Ena asked for another six hours.

She returned to the string web. Most infected households had many coloured connections, which was unsurprising because most people in Lathwater did. Market sellers rode ferries. Ferry pilots ate at stalls. Children crossed three or four bridges to school. The network was so dense that every answer appeared to be the market.

Then Ena noticed an empty place.

Six cards formed a loose ring beside the window. They represented vessels that had spent at least three of the previous eight tides near Mooring 397. None had reported illness. Their strings were fewer and shorter than those around other central moorings.

Mooring 397 stood near the middle of the Fold, not at its edge. It had a public water hose, a ferry stop and a bridge connection. By all ordinary measures it should have been dangerous.

Ena asked her father about it.

"Three-nine-seven?" Oris said. "The bell."

Every numbered mooring had once carried a bronze fog bell, but most had been removed when electric horns arrived. The bell at 397 remained because its bolts had fused into the post. It was badly balanced and rang even on calm water.

"No one likes waiting there," Oris continued. "You can't hear yourself complain. Passengers stay aboard until the bridge drops. Neighbours don't chat. Children won't play near it."

Ena looked again at the six cards. The bell did not change the number of bridges or ferries. It changed how long people remained together.

Her institute models treated a contact as a contact. Two passengers sharing a bench for twenty seconds created the same line as two passengers sharing it for an hour. At Mooring 397, the ledger showed many lines, but the bell made them thin.

She began again. This time she assigned each connection a duration. Where duration was unknown, she estimated it from the timetable. Where the timetable was missing, she used her childhood memory of how long it took to buy bread, gut a fish or argue over ferry fares. She gave conversations at 397 one quarter of their normal weight.

The red cluster loosened.

At midnight, the calculator produced a new result: if average shared time fell by thirty-one percent, the reproduction number would drop from 2.73 to 0.94. Because 0.94 was below one, Ena concluded that no further household would become infected after the reduction began.

Crowe read the page twice. "You want me to close the market?"

"No. I want bells."

## The Bell Plan

By dawn, the council had requisitioned every bell in Lathwater.

They hung school bells over ferry benches, goat bells beneath market awnings and dinner bells beside the public pumps. A brass gong from the cinema was suspended over the eel stall. The harbour clock was ordered to strike every seven minutes, although it frequently struck eleven times because its mechanism was faulty.

The notices called it the Bell Plan. They instructed residents to conduct necessary business but leave any shared place before the next ring. Ena had proposed a more exact system based on four-minute contact windows. Crowe shortened it to: **WHEN IT RINGS, MOVE ALONG.**

The first morning was chaos. Each bell followed a different rhythm. A baker abandoned a tray halfway across a bridge. Ferry passengers stepped ashore at the wrong stop. Two families left the water queue at the same time and collided in a narrow lane. Children treated every ring as permission to run.

Ena watched from the infirmary roof, holding a stopwatch. She counted gatherings and estimated their duration. The gatherings were shorter, but there seemed to be more of them. She entered only the shorter duration into the model because gathering frequency was not one of the adjustable columns.

At noon, her father arrived with five ferry logs. He placed them on the table but kept his hand on the top one.

"The passengers hate this," he said.

"That may mean it is working."

"Pain is not proof, Ena."

She pulled the ledger from beneath his hand. "Neither is disliking arithmetic."

Oris sat down. Up close, she saw sweat shining beneath his eyes despite the cold room.

"When did your fever begin?" she asked.

"I don't have one."

"When?"

He looked towards the window. The gong at the eel stall sounded, followed by a thin scattering of bells. People moved across the bridges like seeds shaken in a tray.

"Night after the fair," he said.

That was six days earlier. Oris had worked every ferry shift since then. His household card sat near the centre of Ena's web, connected to almost every district.

"Why didn't you report it?"

"Because a cough makes a man a red pin, and a red pin isn't allowed to pilot."

"You could have infected half the Fold."

"Or I could have caught it from half the Fold. Your strings don't show which way anything travels."

The statement was true, but Ena hated him for making it. She called an orderly and had him taken to an isolation berth.

Before leaving, Oris nodded towards her calculator.

"Put me in the right box," he said.

His hidden illness altered the case history. Ena moved the beginning of the outbreak back by two days, but she did not recalculate the reproduction number because the council had already printed 2.73 on its emergency notices. Instead, she added Oris as an extra initial case. The projected curve rose sharply for the next morning, then bent down under the effect of the bells.

At dusk, the infirmary admitted nine new patients.

Crowe found Ena asleep beside the calculator. "Nine," he said. "Your model said none."

"They were already exposed before the bells."

"You said no further households."

"The intervention needs time."

"How much?"

Ena remembered the two-day incubation period she had chosen from three interviews. "Until tomorrow."

Crowe wanted to suspend the Bell Plan. Ena showed him the weighted network, tapping the pale threads around Mooring 397.

"This is the only naturally protected cluster in Lathwater," she said. "The only difference is the bell."

"There are six households there."

"Six households without illness."

Neither mentioned that two of those households were net-menders who spent most days alone beyond the harbour, one was an empty holiday vessel, and another had failed to submit its health card. Those details were recorded in separate ledgers on the other side of the room.

Crowe looked down at the moving harbour. "You have one more tide."

## The Curve

The next morning brought seven cases.

Ena called it a decline. The infirmary nurse pointed out that three families had not yet sent their reports, but Ena plotted the number before those forms arrived. On the graph, the epidemic curved down exactly where the model predicted.

The harbour council rang the bells twice as often.

By afternoon, four additional patients arrived. Ena assigned them to the previous day on the grounds that their symptoms had begun before noon. The curve remained unchanged.

Lathwater became a town measured by interruptions. At every ring, conversations broke apart. Cups were left half-filled. Buyers walked away before receiving change. The usual harbour noises—saws, gulls, engines and shouted names—were buried beneath bronze clamour.

Ena visited Oris in the isolation berth. He sat under a grey blanket, looking across the water at his unattended ferry.

"Seven today," she said.

"Eleven. I heard the nurse."

"Four began yesterday."

"Did the sick people know which day you needed them on?"

"Cases are assigned by symptom onset. That is standard."

"And when does your curve say I became sick?"

Ena did not answer.

Oris's fever was already falling. Lantern halos still clouded his vision, and he mistook a coil of rope for a sleeping dog. Ena poured water for him.

"Your mother hated that bell," he said. "At 397. Once, during a storm, she tried to stop it with a scarf. The bell swallowed the scarf and rang louder."

"I don't remember."

"You were away learning boxes."

The cruelty of this was so plain that it seemed rehearsed, yet his expression immediately softened.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"So am I."

They embraced, resolving the six years in which they had spoken only through birthday notes and harbour invoices. Ena felt the anger leave her as completely as water draining from a basin.

Outside, the bells rang. Oris covered his ears.

On the third morning, there were three cases. No late forms arrived. On the fourth, there was one. The patient was a boy from a house that had never visited the market but had shared a water hose with two infected families. Ena considered adding water transmission to the model. Doing so would require rebuilding the network, and the curve was already falling, so she classified the hose as an ordinary social contact.

On the fifth morning, no new yellow cloth appeared.

Crowe convened the council at Mooring 397. The six supposedly protected households stood on their decks while photographers from the upriver newspapers recorded the bell. Ena was asked to explain how the model had saved Lathwater.

She spoke of weighted contacts and mobile networks. She explained that place alone did not spread disease; people met through time, and duration could matter as much as distance. Those listening nodded whenever the bell allowed them to hear.

Councillor Venn asked whether the epidemic was over.

Ena looked at her latest page. The model predicted 0.18 cases during the next week. Since a fraction of a person could not become ill, she rounded this to zero.

"Yes," she said. "It is over."

Crowe ordered the market reopened without restrictions. The bells were taken down except for the one at 397, which no tool had managed to loosen.

## The Harbour After

Lantern fever did not return that summer.

No one discovered its cause. The infirmary nurse suspected a respiratory virus brought by traders at the fair. The water engineer blamed algae in a storage tank. Oris believed half the cases had been common colds renamed by frightened neighbours. Ena's model did not distinguish among these possibilities, but its predicted decline matched the final chart, and the council stored both in the town archive.

The Bell Plan became a festival. On the anniversary of the outbreak, shopkeepers rang brass chimes every seven minutes while children ran from stall to stall. Visitors bought small models of Mooring 397. The cinema produced a play in which an actress portraying Ena defeated a black-robed Fever by striking a silver gong.

Ena stayed in Lathwater as Director of Tidal Epidemiology, a title Crowe invented for her. She built a new office above the ferry terminal and covered one wall with a permanent network of cards and strings. Whenever the tide changed, an assistant moved the cards.

She and Oris ate breakfast together each week. He never apologised for hiding his fever again, and she never asked him to. Their reconciliation had become another fact stored without its uncertainty.

Years later, Ena sometimes woke before dawn and walked alone to Mooring 397. Rust had opened holes in the old bell. Its note was no longer loud, but the tide could still draw a low sound from it.

From there, Lathwater looked orderly. Houses rested in rows, bridges joined their proper neighbours, and ferry lamps travelled along fixed lanes. Only the tide revealed that the order was temporary.

Ena carried the original graph folded in her coat. Its line rose through thirty-one cases, climbed again, and then descended cleanly to zero. She never added the missing reports, the unoccupied vessel or her father's earlier symptoms. Those details belonged to other pages, and other pages made other shapes.

At sunrise, the houses began to swing. Chains tightened below the water. Kitchens separated from workshops; bedrooms approached the market. For one minute every bridge lay crooked between two possible maps.

Then the bell sounded, and the town moved on.
