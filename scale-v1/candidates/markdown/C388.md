## Artefact

# The Last Period Problem: Climate-Just School Timetabling in Overheating Classrooms

## Abstract

School heat adaptation is usually treated as a problem of buildings: install shading, improve ventilation, replace roofs, or add mechanical cooling. Those measures are essential, but they are capital-intensive and slow. Meanwhile, the timetable already distributes heat exposure every day. It determines which pupils occupy a west-facing room after lunch, whether a double mathematics lesson coincides with a building’s thermal peak, who can use the limited cooled rooms, and whether teachers repeatedly move between hot spaces without recovery time. These allocations are rarely visible as policy decisions. They emerge from software and local practice designed chiefly around staffing, room capacity, curriculum coverage, and examination requirements.

This project will investigate whether school timetabling can become a practical and accountable form of climate adaptation without disguising the need for building investment. We will develop and evaluate **TEMPO-H**, a timetable decision system that combines room-level thermal forecasts with pedagogic, staffing, accessibility, and fairness constraints. Its purpose is not simply to minimize the average temperature experienced across a school. Average optimization can leave a small group carrying a disproportionate burden, reserve the coolest rooms for already advantaged courses, or disrupt pupils whose learning depends on stable routines. TEMPO-H will instead seek feasible schedules that place a floor under protection for every pupil and staff member, reduce the worst individual heat burdens, preserve legally and educationally necessary provision, and expose cases where no timetable can compensate for an inadequate building.

The research will be conducted over 42 months with 24 state-funded secondary schools in the Merehaven Learning Region, involving approximately 18,000 pupils and 1,100 staff. It integrates environmental sensing, timetable analysis, participatory governance, robust optimization, and a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized evaluation. The project asks four questions:

1. How much of the unequal heat burden within schools is produced by timetable allocation rather than by the building alone?
2. Which definitions of fairness are legitimate and operationally useful when cooler rooms and cooler periods are scarce?
3. Can uncertainty-aware optimization reduce severe and unequal exposure while preserving curriculum quality, staff workload protections, accessibility, and timetable stability?
4. Does deploying heat-responsive timetables improve classroom conditions, attendance, comfort, and short-term learning persistence on hot days?

The primary contribution will be a tested method for treating timetables as distributive infrastructure. Outputs will include an auditable optimization engine, a synthetic benchmark dataset, school-facing heat-allocation reports, implementation guidance, and “infeasibility certificates” showing when acceptable protection cannot be achieved without capital works or additional staffing.

## Need and significance

Two pupils in the same school can experience markedly different thermal conditions because their schedules route them through different rooms and subjects. Room orientation, floor level, shading, ventilation, equipment, occupancy, and thermal lag can create substantial variation within one building. The hottest lesson may not be the final period: a lightweight room can heat quickly, while a masonry room may peak after outdoor temperatures have begun to fall. A static rule such as “move difficult subjects to the morning” therefore cannot capture the interaction between weather, rooms, occupancy, and learning requirements.

Nor is exposure randomly distributed. Higher-attaining groups may receive priority access to specialist or recently refurbished rooms. Pupils receiving additional support may have less flexibility because they require particular staff, equipment, low-stimulation spaces, or step-free routes. Vocational classes may be tied to workshops with high internal heat loads. Teachers of practical subjects can spend most of a day in one warm room, while colleagues in other departments rotate through cooler spaces. Pupils using school transport may be unable to arrive earlier when a school shifts lessons. Families with fewer resources may also have less capacity to compensate for fatigue or lost study time after a hot school day.

These patterns make heat-responsive timetabling socially consequential. Yet a poorly framed optimization could intensify rather than relieve inequality. Maximizing aggregate comfort might continually assign the same pupils to the least desirable rooms because moving them is administratively cheap. Equal rotation could appear fair while exposing pupils with heat-related access needs to conditions they cannot tolerate. Prioritizing examination cohorts could protect measured attainment while transferring heat to younger pupils. Conversely, making every protected characteristic a scheduling input could create intrusive data flows and contested differential treatment.

The project therefore treats fairness as a governed set of rights, constraints, and trade-offs rather than as a single mathematical score. It also rejects the use of scheduling as an austerity substitute for safer buildings. If every feasible schedule leaves some pupils above an agreed burden ceiling, the system will report the structural shortfall rather than present the least harmful schedule as an adequate solution.

## Research programme

### Work package 1: Measuring timetable-mediated heat inequality

During the first school year, we will construct a thermal and scheduling baseline. Each participating school will receive calibrated sensors in teaching rooms, libraries, dining areas, staff workrooms, and selected circulation spaces. Sensors will record air temperature, relative humidity, and carbon dioxide at five-minute intervals. A stratified subset of rooms will also receive globe-temperature sensors to estimate radiant effects. Outdoor reference stations will be installed at six sites across the region. Sensor drift will be checked quarterly against travelling reference instruments, and implausible or missing readings will be flagged through a published quality protocol.

We will combine these measurements with room characteristics, anonymized timetable records, class size, and occupancy estimates. We will not use live pupil location tracking. A pupil will be treated as present according to the school’s ordinary attendance and timetable systems, with sensitivity analyses for late arrival and authorized absence. Thermal burden will be represented through several measures rather than a single threshold: cumulative degree-minutes above an adaptive comfort boundary, number of high-burden periods, consecutive exposure without a cooler interval, and exposure during lessons identified in advance as requiring sustained concentration or protective equipment.

A hierarchical thermal model will estimate how each room responds to outdoor conditions, prior indoor temperature, solar exposure, occupancy, and ventilation practice. Because a timetable can itself change room temperature by changing occupancy, TEMPO-H will not assume that room forecasts remain fixed after pupils are moved. The model will update predicted conditions for each candidate occupancy pattern and attach uncertainty intervals to those predictions.

To isolate the timetable’s contribution, we will compare observed schedules with feasible counterfactual schedules that preserve teachers, class memberships, required contact hours, specialist-room needs, and accessibility constraints. We will estimate the avoidable share of exposure: the portion that could have been redistributed or reduced without cancelling teaching or violating these requirements. Results will be reported at individual, class, staff, subject, year-group, and school levels. Group comparisons will include economic disadvantage, disability or additional-support status, care experience, ethnicity, and gender where data quality and governance permit. Small cells will be suppressed, and sensitive attributes will not appear in teacher-facing pupil reports.

This work package will produce a “thermal timetable anatomy” for each school. The report will distinguish three problems that are often conflated: a universally hot building, an avoidable allocation pattern, and a scarcity problem in which a small stock of cooler rooms cannot meet legitimate needs. That distinction is necessary for choosing between timetable reform, operational changes, and capital investment.

### Work package 2: Governing fairness under scarcity

We will establish a compensated deliberative panel comprising pupils aged 11–18, parents and carers, teachers, timetable officers, facilities staff, additional-support specialists, school leaders, and representatives of recognized staff bodies. Recruitment will oversample participants whose schedules are commonly inflexible, including pupils requiring specialist spaces, pupils dependent on school transport, practical-subject teachers, and families using income-related support. Separate pupil sessions will reduce the pressure to agree with adults, and accessible formats will be provided.

Participants will work through concrete scheduling cases rather than being asked whether they support “fairness” in the abstract. Cases will include allocating one cooled room among examination classes and support groups; deciding whether to rotate an unavoidable hot period; protecting a teacher assigned to one room all day; and choosing between a stable weekly timetable and weather-triggered room swaps. Facilitators will document disagreements as well as areas of consensus.

The resulting **Heat Timetable Charter** will specify non-negotiable protections, acceptable reasons for unequal allocation, limits on disruption, and conditions requiring escalation to building management. Candidate principles will be tested against historical schedules and revised when they produce perverse outcomes. For example, a simple equality rule may rotate all pupils through the hottest room, increasing total exposure without helping those most at risk. A pure priority rule may protect one group indefinitely while overlooking cumulative burdens on others.

The charter will govern the order of TEMPO-H’s objectives. Safety, accessibility, mandated learning provision, and agreed staff protections will be hard constraints. The system will then minimize the worst predicted individual burden, followed by disparities between comparable groups, total exposure, pedagogic disruption, and operational cost. Sensitive demographic variables will be used by default for auditing outcomes, not for making individual allocations. Aggregate group constraints may be activated only where the panel and school governance body approve a clearly stated remedial purpose.

### Work package 3: Developing TEMPO-H

TEMPO-H will generate two linked products. The first is a heat-aware base timetable for use across a term. The second is a small set of pre-validated overlays that can be activated on forecast heat days. An overlay might swap rooms between two classes, move a double lesson, alter the order of periods, or open a designated cooler space. Pre-validation is important: wholly dynamic daily rescheduling would create confusion, transport difficulties, staff workload, and particular harm for pupils who rely on predictable routines.

The optimization engine will combine mixed-integer programming with scenario-based robust optimization. Scenarios will represent uncertainty in weather, room response, attendance, and whether windows or mechanical ventilation are available. A solution will be rejected if it works only under an optimistic temperature forecast. Instead, schools will choose a documented level of protection, such as requiring all hard constraints to hold across at least 95 percent of calibrated scenarios while minimizing severe exposure in the remaining tail.

Core constraints will include teacher availability, curriculum contact time, room capacity, specialist equipment, class non-overlap, travel time between sites, step-free access, support-staff availability, limits on consecutive teaching, safeguarding arrangements, and stability requirements. Schools may add local constraints, but each must have an owner, rationale, and expiry review. This prevents inherited preferences—such as a department’s customary claim on a particular room—from silently acquiring the status of an educational necessity.

The objective hierarchy will prevent improvements in average conditions from being purchased through extreme burdens on a minority. First, TEMPO-H will seek schedules satisfying all rights and provision constraints. Second, it will minimize the highest individual excess-burden measure. Third, it will reduce repeated exposure, including consecutive hot periods and recurring assignment to the same thermally poor room. Fourth, it will reduce disparities identified through the governance process. Only then will it optimize aggregate comfort, timetable compactness, room changes, and energy implications. Schools will be able to inspect a Pareto frontier showing, for example, how much disruption is required to obtain a further reduction in worst-case exposure.

Every recommendation will include a plain-language trade-off certificate. Rather than saying that a schedule is “optimal,” it will state which constraints are binding, which groups gain or lose relative to the current timetable, how results change under warmer-than-forecast conditions, and which alternative was rejected. Timetablers can lock a requirement and rerun the model, but overrides will be logged with a reason. No automated change will be sent directly to pupils or staff.

Where no schedule satisfies the charter’s burden ceilings, TEMPO-H will generate an infeasibility certificate. This will identify the smallest changes that could restore feasibility, such as shading two rooms, adding a support-staff period, permitting a class split, or reducing occupancy in a workshop. The certificate is intended to strengthen rather than weaken claims for resources by quantifying what scheduling alone cannot solve.

The software will be tested through three stages: reconstruction of historical timetables, adversarial synthetic cases, and supervised live planning. Adversarial cases will probe failure modes including missing sensor data, a cooled room becoming unavailable, correlated heat across an entire building, conflicting accessibility requirements, and objectives that improve group parity by making everyone worse off. A separate audit team will attempt to identify hidden burden transfers before field deployment.

### Work package 4: Stepped-wedge evaluation

In years two and three, the 24 schools will enter the intervention in four randomly ordered groups of six. Randomization will be stratified by baseline thermal risk, school size, and the proportion of pupils receiving income-related support. All schools will retain their existing heat safety procedures, and no school will be asked to keep using a schedule judged unsafe. Before crossover, schools will receive sensor feedback but not TEMPO-H recommendations. After crossover, they will use the base timetable and may activate approved overlays when a preregistered forecast trigger is met.

The primary environmental outcome will be pupil-level excess thermal burden on eligible heat-event days, analyzed by intention to treat. Co-primary distributional outcomes will be the 95th percentile of individual burden and the gap between pupils in the highest- and lowest-burden deciles at baseline. Staff exposure will be analyzed separately to avoid treating improvements for pupils as sufficient if they transfer heat to employees.

Secondary outcomes will include absence by period, early departure, visits to the school wellbeing room, self-reported thermal comfort, perceived concentration, timetable disruption, staff workload, and curriculum time lost. Short curriculum-aligned retrieval tasks will assess immediate learning persistence in a rotating sample of classes. These tasks will be designed by teachers before treatment assignment and scored without access to exposure data. We will not treat short-term test performance as the sole or definitive educational benefit; it is a secondary mechanism measure.

The main analysis will use mixed-effects models with school, room, class, and calendar effects, supplemented by randomization inference at the school-sequence level. We will estimate intervention effects separately for forecast-triggered days and for unexpectedly hot days when overlays were not activated. Predefined subgroup analyses will examine whether benefits reach pupils with additional-support needs, economically disadvantaged pupils, practical-subject classes, and staff with high baseline room persistence. We will also test for burden displacement across year groups, subjects, lunch periods, and staff.

Power simulations using three years of partner-supplied weather records indicate that 24 schools should provide 80 percent power to detect a 12 percent reduction in pupil excess-burden minutes if at least 14 eligible heat-event days occur across the two intervention seasons. The study is not powered to detect small changes in attainment or rare health events. If few hot days occur, environmental estimates will be supplemented by preregistered room-response simulations and controlled overlay exercises, but these will not be presented as equivalent to field evidence.

Implementation fidelity will be studied rather than assumed. We will record whether recommendations were accepted, modified, or rejected; the notice given to families; room changes that failed in practice; and the staff time required. Interviews will explore whether transparent trade-off information improves trust or instead creates new conflict over scarce rooms. The final effectiveness analysis will therefore be paired with an implementation account explaining what schools could realistically sustain.

## Ethics, privacy, and political accountability

The project will process timetable, attendance, support, and demographic data that could reveal sensitive patterns even without names. Identifiable records will remain within a secure regional data environment. The research dataset will use rotating pseudonymous identifiers, and the optimization service will receive only the scheduling attributes needed for a run. A pupil requiring a particular environmental or accessibility provision can be represented by an eligibility token; the solver does not need access to a diagnosis. Public reporting will use disclosure controls, and no league table of “hot schools” will be produced.

A data and equity board with pupil, family, staff, privacy, and school representation will approve new variables, review overrides, and have authority to pause deployment. Pupils may decline comfort surveys without affecting their timetable or support. Because room allocation affects whole classes, individual opt-out from the environmental intervention is not generally possible; this limitation will be explained through school consultation and ethics review.

The project will also guard against adaptation being used to normalize unsafe estates. Reports will show absolute burden alongside relative improvement. A school that reduces unequal exposure while remaining excessively hot will not be described as having solved the problem. Infeasibility findings will be shared with facilities planners and staff representatives, and the final implementation guide will specify when timetable action should be accompanied by or deferred to physical remediation.

## Outputs and impact

By month 42, the project will deliver:

- the TEMPO-H planning engine with documented constraints, audit logs, and uncertainty reporting;
- a synthetic, privacy-preserving benchmark of school timetable and thermal scenarios;
- a validated protocol for measuring timetable-mediated heat burden;
- the Heat Timetable Charter and facilitation materials for local adaptation;
- school-facing thermal allocation reports and capital-planning infeasibility certificates;
- evidence on environmental, distributional, educational, and workload effects;
- procurement guidance requiring explainability, override logging, and burden-displacement tests in commercial timetable systems;
- training for timetablers, facilities teams, pupil councils, and school leaders.

The interdisciplinary team combines school operations research, building physics, education evaluation, participatory methods, data governance, and implementation science. Each school will nominate both a timetable lead and an equity lead so that deployment is not owned solely by technical staff. A regional community of practice will meet each term to compare failures as well as successful adaptations.

The project’s immediate beneficiaries will be pupils and staff in schools where rooms cannot be improved quickly enough to meet intensifying heat. Its wider contribution is a transferable way to ask who bears an operational burden, whether that burden is avoidable, and when optimization should produce a schedule versus evidence that more resources are required.

## Scope and limitations

The evaluation is concentrated in secondary schools within one urban and peri-urban region. Primary schools, rural transport networks, and climates with substantially different humidity or building traditions may require different constraints and comfort models. Two intervention summers also leave the field trial dependent on the occurrence and timing of hot weather, while schools cannot be blinded to visibly different schedules. Finally, participatory governance cannot eliminate disagreement about whose disruption counts most, and administrative categories may conceal important variation within and across groups. We will publish these boundaries, retain multiple distributional measures, and avoid presenting the charter’s chosen weights as universally valid.
