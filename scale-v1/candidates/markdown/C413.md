## Artefact

# The Archive Is the Rehearsal: Designing Digital Storage Around Recovery

Long-term digital storage is often described as a problem of finding durable media. This encourages questions such as whether tape lasts longer than hard drives, whether archival optical discs are worth their price, or whether a cloud provider can preserve an object for a century. Those questions matter, but they begin too late in the story. A storage medium can retain every bit entrusted to it while the information still becomes unusable.

A more useful starting point is the recovery event. Imagine that the original staff have left, the normal software is unavailable, and one copy has already failed. Can a new custodian locate the right material, verify it, interpret it, and produce a usable result without guessing? Long-term storage is the maintenance of that complete recovery path.

This perspective changes what counts as preservation work. Copying files is necessary, but so are documenting units, retaining transformation code, testing credentials, recording rights, and periodically asking someone to restore material under constrained conditions. The archive is not merely the collection of stored objects. It is also the organization’s repeatedly demonstrated ability to recover them.

## A marsh archive that outlived its instruments

Consider the fictional Merewick Tidal Office, which has monitored an estuary since 1978. Its collection includes handwritten observations transcribed into spreadsheets, voltage readings from obsolete conductivity probes, photographs of gauge installations, calibration certificates, database exports, and recent high-frequency sensor streams. Researchers want to use the record to study long-term changes in salinity.

The office’s oldest digital files are still readable. That is not enough. A column marked `S2` contains values between 0 and 4,095, but no surviving file says whether these are salinity measurements or raw readings from a 12-bit converter. A later dataset records timestamps without a time zone. One period was adjusted using a calibration curve stored in an employee’s personal analysis folder. Several photographs show instruments, but their filenames do not identify which station they depict.

Nothing in this example requires dramatic bit decay. The files can remain perfectly intact while the evidence they once carried slowly separates from its context. Conversely, an archive can survive the loss of a particular disk if it has redundant copies, trustworthy manifests, and enough documentation to reconstruct meaning.

Merewick therefore treats every important dataset as a recoverable package rather than as a loose set of files. The package includes data, descriptions, checksums, provenance, and, where needed, executable transformations. The office also performs recovery exercises in which a staff member who did not create the package must use it.

## Four layers of survival

A practical preservation plan has to protect at least four layers.

### 1. Bits

The bytes must remain available and unchanged. Media failure, accidental deletion, malicious alteration, interrupted transfers, and silent corruption all threaten this layer. Replication and fixity checking primarily protect bits.

### 2. Objects

The bytes must still form identifiable files, database records, disk images, or other bounded objects. A raw stream copied from a damaged disk may preserve bytes without preserving the original directory structure. A database export may omit attachments stored in another directory. Object-level preservation asks what belongs together and whether the set is complete.

### 3. Meaning

A future user must know what the objects represent. Units, coordinate systems, time zones, missing-value codes, character encodings, calibration histories, and relationships between tables can be more important than the choice of medium. Meaning also includes distinctions such as whether a file is an original observation, a corrected derivative, or a presentation copy.

### 4. Authority

The user must be able to judge where the material came from and whether it is an acceptable version. Provenance, chain of custody, signatures, access restrictions, and records of later transformations belong here. Two files can contain identical-looking tables while having very different evidential value.

These layers are related but not interchangeable. A checksum can establish with high confidence that an object matches a prior bit sequence, but it cannot establish that the column labels are accurate. A detailed catalogue cannot compensate for the loss of every data copy. A successful opening of a file does not prove that it is the approved version.

## Begin with a threat model, not a shopping list

Storage systems fail in different ways, and extra copies help only when they do not share the same failure. Three replicas in one synchronized account may all receive the same mistaken deletion. Two tape sets stored in one room may be lost in the same flood. A cloud copy and an office server may both depend on credentials held by one departing administrator.

A modest archive should consider at least these threats:

- physical media failure;
- silent changes to stored data;
- accidental deletion or overwriting;
- ransomware or misuse of privileged accounts;
- fire, water, heat, theft, and building access failures;
- loss of encryption keys or account credentials;
- unsupported hardware interfaces and software formats;
- incomplete transfers and undocumented processing;
- supplier closure, contract disputes, and unaffordable retrieval fees;
- disappearance of the people who understand the collection.

The aim is not to predict one disaster. It is to avoid arrangements in which one event can disable every recovery route. This is why independence matters more than the raw number of copies. Independence can mean different buildings, administrative accounts, media types, operating systems, or responsible staff. Not every copy needs every kind of independence, but the collection should not have a single invisible hinge.

Threat modelling also prevents exaggerated attention to bit rot. Random media decay is real, yet routine human actions often create faster losses. A well-meaning employee can empty a synchronized folder in seconds. An export script can silently omit records for years. A preservation plan that buys premium media but does not test exports is badly balanced.

## Inventory before preservation

An institution cannot preserve material it cannot enumerate. An initial inventory should record more than filenames and sizes. For each collection, it should identify an owner, a responsible custodian, the approximate volume, file types, sensitivity, retention requirement, dependencies, current locations, and the consequences of loss.

The inventory does not have to describe every item at first. Merewick begins at collection level: gauge photographs, raw probe output, laboratory assays, calibration records, and published annual summaries. It then adds item-level descriptions where distinctions affect recovery.

This process often reveals that the apparent archive is a mixture of sources and outputs. A rendered chart may be reproducible from raw data and code, but only if the code and parameters survive. A database backup may look comprehensive while excluding files stored outside the database. Temporary working data may contain unique annotations despite having an unimportant name.

Priorities should follow value rather than volume. Merewick assigns material to three broad classes:

1. **Irreplaceable evidence:** original observations, calibration records, and signed reports.
2. **Costly but reproducible material:** cleaned datasets and derived products whose source and transformation survive.
3. **Convenience copies:** caches, duplicated downloads, and outputs that can be generated automatically.

The distinction determines replication and testing effort. It also gives staff permission to delete material that adds cost without adding evidence. However, value can change, and seemingly minor files sometimes explain major datasets. Classification therefore needs periodic review rather than a single disposal campaign.

## Build bounded preservation packages

Loose files are difficult to move and verify because nobody knows where the collection ends. A preservation package gives the material a boundary. A simple package might contain:

- a stable package identifier;
- the preserved data objects;
- a machine-readable file manifest;
- checksums for every object;
- a short human-readable description;
- provenance and acquisition notes;
- format, encoding, unit, and time-zone information;
- known omissions and defects;
- rights or access conditions;
- transformation code and parameters, if derivatives matter;
- a record of preservation actions.

The manifest should use relative paths so that the package can move without rewriting every reference. It should also avoid making the checksum file validate itself, which creates a circular definition. A second, separately stored catalogue can record the expected checksum of the package manifest.

Packages should be large enough to preserve meaningful relationships but small enough to transfer and inspect. Merewick uses a working ceiling of 50 gigabytes for most packages, dividing larger sensor collections by station and year. This is an administrative rule rather than a technical boundary, but it makes failed transfers less expensive to repeat and gives recovery exercises manageable units.

Compression can reduce storage and transfer costs, but a single huge compressed archive increases the blast radius of corruption and can make partial retrieval difficult. For collections with millions of tiny files, packaging may still be worthwhile because filesystems and object stores handle small-object overhead poorly. The correct choice depends on access patterns: frequently retrieved items benefit from individual addressing, while deep archival sets may benefit from grouped containers.

## Fixity: knowing whether the bits moved

A cryptographic checksum is a compact value calculated from an object’s bytes. If an object changes, recalculating the checksum will almost always produce a different value. SHA-256 is a sensible default for preservation manifests because it is widely implemented and accidental collisions are for practical purposes negligible.

Checksums are most useful when created near acquisition, verified after every transfer, and checked periodically afterward. Generating a checksum only after a damaged file has spent ten years in storage merely records the damaged state. The checksum manifest must itself be protected and versioned; otherwise an attacker or faulty process could replace both a file and its expected checksum.

A mismatch proves that two observed bit sequences differ, but it does not by itself identify the correct one or explain the cause. The archive needs a known-good copy, provenance records, or an earlier trusted manifest to repair the damage. Matching checksums establish continuity of bits, not truth of content.

Merewick verifies all newly written packages immediately. It checks its online holdings quarterly and samples offline holdings annually. This creates a regular signal without requiring every tape or disc to be mounted each quarter. The office treats a failed check as an incident: it preserves the suspect version, compares replicas, records the repair, and verifies the replacement.

## Copies must fail differently

The familiar advice to keep three copies on two kinds of storage with one copy off-site is a useful memory aid. It is not a complete design. Media diversity is less important than failure diversity. Two brands of hard drive connected to the same compromised server are not meaningfully independent.

Merewick’s irreplaceable class has three principal copies:

- an online copy in the office storage system for access and routine verification;
- a cloud object copy in a separate administrative account with version retention and deletion delays;
- an offline tape copy stored at a regional records depot.

The catalogue and checksum manifests also exist outside the main storage account. At least two people can retrieve credentials through a documented escrow procedure. The cloud account does not use the same identity provider as the office network, reducing the chance that one account compromise disables both.

Offline storage is valuable because it interrupts automatic propagation. A tape in a depot cannot immediately receive a ransomware encryption operation or synchronized deletion. Yet offline copies create their own risks: they are harder to inspect, slower to retrieve, and easily forgotten. An offline copy with an unreadable label or an unavailable drive is more comforting than useful.

Geographic separation should reflect plausible regional events. Moving a copy to another room protects against a disk failure but not against a building fire. Moving it across town may not protect against a flood affecting the whole estuary. Greater distance, however, raises transport, legal, and coordination costs. Independence is therefore an engineering trade-off, not a ritual.

## What the media can and cannot do

No storage medium is archival by itself. Its useful lifetime depends on manufacturing quality, environmental conditions, write quality, handling, interfaces, and the institution’s ability to inspect and migrate it.

### Hard-disk systems

Hard drives offer fast access and low initial cost. In an actively managed array, failed devices can be detected and replaced without waiting for a scheduled archive visit. This makes disk suitable for frequently used material and routine fixity checks.

A powered array is nevertheless an operating service, not a shelf object. Controllers, firmware, power supplies, filesystems, and administrator actions are part of its risk surface. Redundant array layouts can maintain availability after a drive failure, but they are not backup: deletion and corruption can be replicated across the array.

### Data tape

Tape offers high capacity and low power consumption while stored. It can provide a strong offline layer and is economical for collections retrieved infrequently. Its disadvantages are operational. Drives are expensive, cartridges must be tracked, and generations of tape technology eventually lose convenient support. Restoring one small object may require locating and mounting a whole cartridge.

Tape is best treated as a continuing programme. Merewick assumes that cartridges will be copied to a supported generation about every seven years. Actual timing should respond to drive availability and error reports rather than to the calendar alone, but a scheduled cycle prevents indefinite postponement.

### Optical media

Recordable optical discs can be useful for small, closed collections. Finalized write-once media resist ordinary remote overwriting and offer straightforward physical separation. Capacity is limited, large sets become labour-intensive, and quality varies among discs and writers. Claims of extremely long laboratory life do not guarantee that a future office will possess a compatible drive.

Optical media are therefore a niche layer rather than Merewick’s primary store. The office uses them for a small set of annual legal deposit packages, with duplicate discs and periodic test reads.

### Solid-state storage

Flash drives and consumer solid-state drives are convenient transfer devices but weak unattended shelf archives. Charge retention, controller failure, and uncertain behaviour after long periods without power make their future readability difficult to predict. Enterprise solid-state systems can be reliable when monitored, but that is different from placing a portable drive in a drawer for fifteen years.

### Cloud object storage

Cloud services can provide geographic replication, integrity features, versioning, access logs, and rapid scaling. They transfer some hardware operations to a specialist provider. They do not transfer preservation responsibility. The customer must still maintain accounts, pay bills, configure retention correctly, preserve metadata, and test exports.

Cloud failure may take the form of a policy change or an inaccessible account rather than a broken disk. Retrieval charges can make a technically recoverable collection financially awkward to leave. A useful cloud plan includes an exit procedure, an estimate of full-export time, and a copy of the object inventory held elsewhere.

## Preserve the read path, not every old machine

A file’s read path is the chain needed to turn stored bytes into intelligible content. It may include a container format, codec, application, runtime, operating system, license service, hardware architecture, credentials, and external reference files. Recording this chain exposes dependencies that a file extension conceals.

There are two main responses to format obsolescence. Migration converts material into a currently supported format. Emulation recreates enough of the old environment to run original software. Migration improves ordinary access but can alter features. Emulation can preserve behaviour but requires its own technical knowledge and may depend on old licenses or undocumented hardware.

For common tabular data, Merewick retains the original file and creates a normalized export with documented character encoding, explicit missing values, and unambiguous dates. For a specialized instrument database, it preserves the original backup, a set of open table exports, the database schema, screenshots of important views, and a virtual-machine image containing the reader software.

The virtual machine is not assumed to be permanent. It is another object that must be stored and tested. A future hypervisor may not open it directly, and network-dependent licensing may fail. The open exports provide a simpler fallback even if they do not reproduce every feature.

Merewick reviews formats every five years and migrates any format that has lost ordinary support. This interval creates a predictable responsibility, although some formats may remain safe for decades while a proprietary service can become inaccessible within months. Significant supplier changes should trigger an earlier review.

## Recovery exercises reveal hidden dependencies

Routine checksum verification answers whether selected bytes still match. A recovery exercise asks whether the archive can actually deliver useful information. The distinction resembles checking that emergency equipment is present versus conducting an evacuation drill.

A useful exercise begins with a request rather than a storage device. For example: recover all raw and corrected observations from Station Alder for March 1996, identify the calibration applied, and produce a table of salinity values with timestamps in coordinated universal time. The operator receives the catalogue and documented credentials but no help from the original data creator.

The exercise should record:

- time required to locate and retrieve the package;
- whether every expected object was present;
- results of checksum verification;
- hardware, software, and accounts used;
- ambiguities encountered in documentation;
- whether a usable output could be produced;
- corrective actions, owners, and deadlines.

Merewick restores at least one percent of its preservation packages each year, weighted toward high-value and technically unusual holdings. New formats are included early, and older untested packages receive priority. The office also performs a complete catalogue-and-credential exercise after major staffing or supplier changes.

Sampling cannot prove that every package is recoverable. It can, however, expose systemic defects such as expired accounts, missing tape drives, malformed manifests, or instructions that make sense only to their author. The sample should rotate and should not be selected solely from easy packages. A superficially high success rate is meaningless if difficult holdings are always excluded.

An external or newly appointed participant is especially valuable. Experienced custodians unconsciously supply missing context. If a package works only because one person remembers that negative values mean a sensor was out of water, the documentation has failed even though that person can complete the task.

## A failed drill can be a preservation success

In one Merewick exercise, a technician retrieves a 2004 conductivity package from tape. Its checksums match, the comma-separated tables open, and the calibration document is present. The final salinity calculation still cannot be reproduced because the package refers to `curve_final.dat`, which is absent.

An employee eventually finds the file in an old project directory. Investigation shows that the original packaging script included only files with three approved extensions. The missing calibration table used an unrecognized extension and had been excluded from forty-two packages.

The office repairs the affected packages, changes the script to work from explicit dependency lists, and adds a completeness report to future ingest. The tape did not fail. The package definition did. Only a task-oriented recovery exercise revealed the problem.

This illustrates why drills should not be judged solely as pass-or-fail performances. Their purpose is to discover dependencies while repair is still possible. An archive that reports no recovery problems may be exceptionally well run, or it may be asking questions too narrow to reveal them.

## Storage economics includes human attention

Media prices are easy to compare because they have visible units. Staff time, retrieval delays, audit work, and dependency maintenance are harder to price but often dominate a long-lived archive. A very cheap offline copy can become expensive if every request requires manual cartridge handling. A convenient cloud service can become expensive when a full migration incurs large retrieval charges.

A basic cost estimate should include acquisition, replication, monitoring, catalogue work, environmental control, replacement hardware, migrations, test restores, and eventual exit from each supplier. It should also include growth. Keeping three copies of a collection that doubles every two years is a different commitment from preserving a closed historical deposit.

Merewick reduces cost by applying service levels. Irreplaceable evidence receives three independently managed copies and annual sampled recovery. Reproducible derivatives receive two copies and can be regenerated when practical. Convenience material may have only short retention. This arrangement is less simple than declaring every byte equally permanent, but it concentrates effort where loss would matter most.

The classification has limits. Regeneration costs can rise, source software can disappear, and future research value is difficult to foresee. For that reason, the office records disposal decisions and keeps small contextual files even when it discards bulky intermediate outputs.

## A compact operating cycle

Merewick’s preservation work follows a repeating cycle:

1. **Acquire:** transfer material into a staging area without altering the source copy.
2. **Inspect:** identify formats, scan for obvious hazards, and compare the transfer with the source.
3. **Describe:** record provenance, meaning, rights, dependencies, and known defects.
4. **Package:** create bounded objects, manifests, and checksums.
5. **Replicate:** write copies into distinct failure domains and verify each write.
6. **Monitor:** check fixity, account status, capacity, media errors, and supplier changes.
7. **Exercise:** restore selected packages and answer realistic questions with them.
8. **Repair or migrate:** correct defects, replace endangered media, and update documentation.
9. **Review:** reconsider value, access needs, costs, formats, and assigned responsibilities.

Each action creates a record. This does not require an elaborate preservation platform. A small institution can use ordinary files, scripts, a controlled catalogue, and clearly assigned roles. Automation helps with repeated calculations and reports, but automated success messages should not substitute for checking usable outputs.

The cycle also needs ownership. Someone must receive failure alerts, authorize repairs, and ensure that a departed employee’s responsibilities are transferred. A preservation policy without named operational roles tends to become a statement of hope.

## The durable object is a relationship

Digital storage is sometimes presented as a contest among tape, disk, optical media, and cloud systems. In practice, each can participate in a sound design, and each can preserve unusable material. Durability comes from the relationship among copies, descriptions, software, credentials, people, and repeated verification.

A recoverable archive can answer five questions: What do we have? Which version is authoritative? Have its bits changed? What is required to interpret it? Can someone other than its creator recover it now?

The last question gives the others practical force. Recovery exercises turn an archive from an assertion into a demonstrated capability. They do not eliminate uncertainty, and a successful sample cannot guarantee the whole collection. They do reveal whether the institution’s assumptions still hold.

Long-term storage is therefore less like placing an object in a vault and more like maintaining a route through changing terrain. Copies are bridges, metadata is the map, checksums are survey markers, and migration repairs sections that are becoming impassable. The route survives only if someone walks it before it is urgently needed.
