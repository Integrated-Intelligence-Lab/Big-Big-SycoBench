## Artefact

# DriftCradle: Passive Recovery System for Storm-Detached Coral

## 1. Product summary

DriftCradle is a portable mesh collector intended to recover living coral fragments that become detached during storms. Restoration crews deploy the system along the down-current edge of a shallow reef before forecast rough weather or immediately afterward. Water passes through the collector while fragments are directed into removable padded baskets. Recovered fragments can then be transferred to nurseries or attached to damaged reef areas.

The product addresses a gap between storm damage and organized restoration. Detached fragments often remain alive for several days, but teams usually collect only the pieces visible near established survey routes. DriftCradle expands the collection area without requiring divers to search every reef crevice.

The first release is designed for sheltered reefs between 1 and 8 metres deep. It is not intended for surf zones, deep reefs, or collection during an active severe storm.

## 2. Goals

- Capture at least 70% of coral fragments larger than 4 cm that drift through the deployed collection corridor.
- Allow a two-person team to deploy a 20 m system in under 45 minutes.
- Keep collected fragments separated from hard frame components.
- Remain unattended for up to 14 days.
- Reduce the time divers spend searching for viable fragments by half.
- Use replaceable components so that a damaged section does not require disposal of the entire product.

## 3. Non-goals

DriftCradle does not grow coral, diagnose disease, identify coral species, or select genetically diverse specimens. It is not a permanent reef barrier and should not be used as a public swimming enclosure. The system does not determine whether collected material is legally eligible for transplantation; operators remain responsible for local permissions.

## 4. Intended users

The primary users are reef restoration organizations with trained divers and access to a small boat. Secondary users include resort conservation teams and coastal park staff. One person on each deployment must be familiar with local currents and reef access.

No specialist engineering training is required. Each kit includes a waterproof placement card showing recommended layouts for straight reef edges, semicircular bays, and isolated coral heads.

## 5. System components

A standard DriftCradle kit contains:

1. **Six collector panels:** Each panel is 3.5 m long and 0.8 m high. The lower 0.5 m uses flexible plant-fibre mesh with 30 mm openings. The upper section uses buoyant 50 mm mesh to prevent fragments from passing over the panel.
2. **Five recovery baskets:** Soft-sided baskets attach to gaps between panels. Flexible vanes direct fragments toward each opening. A basket holds approximately 35 medium fragments.
3. **Seven helical anchors:** Anchors are driven into sand or dead rubble beside the reef. Colored depth marks indicate minimum insertion.
4. **Surface marker floats:** Floats show the collection line from the boat and carry a product identification code.
5. **Current flag:** A small fabric vane provides a visual indication of flow direction during deployment.
6. **Field log tags:** Reusable tags record the deployment date, basket number, and approximate collection location.

All submerged fabric components will be colored pale blue to avoid attracting fish. The main mesh is expected to lose structural strength after six months in seawater and can then be composted through ordinary municipal facilities. Frames, clips, and anchors are reusable for five years.

## 6. Operating requirements

### 6.1 Placement

The collector should be installed 2–5 m beyond the living reef edge, approximately perpendicular to the prevailing current. Panels may follow a curve, but adjacent panels must not differ in angle by more than 25 degrees. The bottom edge should remain 10–20 cm above live coral and no more than 15 cm above sand.

Sites qualify when average current speed is below 1.2 m/s and water depth remains within the product range at low tide. Crews may estimate current speed using the included float-and-rope method. If the float travels 10 m in under eight seconds, deployment should be cancelled.

### 6.2 Fragment handling

Baskets must be checked within 24 hours after a storm. Fragments with visible living tissue on at least one-third of their surface are considered recoverable. Each basket should be lifted slowly and placed in a seawater tub. Crews should keep branching, plate, and boulder-shaped fragments in separate tubs to prevent abrasion.

Fragments may remain inside the baskets for up to seven days because continuous flow provides oxygen and removes sediment. A basket that reaches the marked fill line must be emptied even if the scheduled collection date has not arrived.

### 6.3 Marine-life safety

Mesh openings are sized so that most reef fish can pass through. If a turtle, ray, or fish longer than 40 cm is observed inside the collection corridor, divers must open the nearest quick-release clip. Crews should stop using a site after two wildlife interactions in one month.

The collector should be inspected every 24 hours when practical. Broken mesh, loose anchors, and accumulated seaweed require immediate correction. During unattended operation, the flexible upper edge will collapse under contact from large animals and return to position afterward.

## 7. Deployment workflow

1. Review the two-day weather forecast and select the likely down-current reef edge.
2. Record a surface coordinate and photograph the proposed line.
3. Install the center anchor and confirm direction with the current flag.
4. Add panels outward from the center, connecting one recovery basket between each pair.
5. Pull each panel until wrinkles disappear, but do not fully tension the mesh.
6. Attach marker floats and record basket tag numbers.
7. Return after the storm, count captured fragments, and remove damaged or non-living material.
8. Rinse baskets in seawater, fold the panels, and inspect clips before storage.

Deployment should be cancelled when lightning is visible, waves are breaking over the work area, or divers cannot maintain visual contact.

## 8. Data recording

The field log records deployment duration, estimated current, storm category, number of fragments per basket, broad growth form, and the number accepted for restoration. An optional phone form can copy these entries into a spreadsheet. It does not require an account and stores records on the device until staff return to shore.

Performance is calculated as the number of usable fragments recovered per diver-hour. Sites reaching 12 fragments per diver-hour are considered successful. The product team will combine results from all deployments to recommend future panel spacing.

## 9. Verification plan

Prototype verification will use a 12 m outdoor flow channel containing molded coral pieces in three sizes. Twenty pieces will be released upstream at slow, medium, and fast pump settings. The product passes if at least 14 pieces enter baskets at every setting and no piece receives a visible chip.

A field pilot will deploy ten kits at three sheltered restoration sites for one storm season. At least 200 collected fragments must be followed for 30 days after transplantation. The pilot is successful if 60% remain alive, average deployment takes less than one hour, and there are no recorded injuries to users or large marine animals.

If the pilot succeeds, production can begin without changing the collector dimensions. The next version may add a low-cost current sensor and coded basket labels.

## 10. Packaging and service life

One kit packs into two 90 L crates and must weigh under 48 kg in total. Individual crates include lifting handles and should remain below 25 kg. Mesh panels are sold separately as replacement parts. Users should replace a panel when an opening stretches beyond 60 mm or when more than 10% of its edge stitching is missing.

Kits will be offered in 20 m and 40 m configurations. Both sizes use the same anchors and operating instructions.
