## Artefact

# The Assessor of Hesper-297

When Sela Dain left Cinder Quay, the settlement council gave her nineteen days of oxygen, one return burn, and a paper model of an asteroid made by the primary class. The children had painted silver veins across it. On one side, in purple ink, they had written: **PLEASE FIND THE TAXES.**

Hesper-297 appeared outside the courier window as a potato dropped in soot. Its mine head was a cluster of lamps around a black shaft. Beyond it hung three habitation drums belonging to Vaylen Extraction, all rotating slowly on cables.

The company had paid no mineral tax for eleven quarters.

According to its returns, Hesper had become worthless. Nickel prices were down, transport insurance was up, and the cobalt seam had proved thinner than expected. After freight, refining, equipment depreciation and the “psychological burden premium” required by deep-space crews, every tonne supposedly lost forty crowns.

Sela had crossed out the last deduction twice. Nobody at the council knew what a psychological burden premium was, including the councillor who had approved it.

Orin Holt, the mine steward, met her at the air lock. He was broad, grey-haired and surprisingly cheerful for a man awaiting an audit.

“You came in person,” he said.

“I don’t assess rocks from promotional photographs.”

“That’s old-fashioned.”

“So is paying teachers.”

He glanced at the paper asteroid clipped to her case. “The school?”

“The council closes it next term unless your accounts develop some value.”

Orin led her through Shield Drum Three. The corridor walls were covered with blue-grey tiles, each as long as Sela’s forearm. Under the warm lamps, they shone like frozen rain.

“The return says this drum is lined with crushed tailings,” she said.

“It is.”

She tapped a tile. It rang.

“Tailings don’t usually have serial numbers.”

The steward stopped smiling.

Each tile bore a tiny stamp: **H297-CN-8**. Sela took out her reader. The code belonged to an assay series produced two years earlier: eight thousand one hundred tonnes of cobalt-nickel alloy, refined to industrial grade.

Orin folded his arms. “It isn’t cargo.”

“It’s refined metal.”

“It’s radiation shielding. We needed mass. Rock is mass.”

“You didn’t use rock. You separated it, smelted it and stamped it.”

“Because neat tiles fit better than rubble.”

Sela looked down the corridor. There were hundreds of tiles in sight, and the drum had twelve decks. Cinder Quay’s tax code charged six percent on minerals “removed from their natural matrix and placed into commerce or beneficial service.” The second phrase had been added after a water company claimed that ice used as rocket fuel had never been sold and therefore did not exist economically.

“This is beneficial service,” she said.

“This is a wall.”

“It can be both.”

Orin took her to the galley. They drank bitter algae tea from magnetic cups while he opened the mine accounts on a table screen.

“The ore price you people use is a fiction,” he said. “It’s the delivery price at Bellweather Market. Hesper isn’t at Bellweather. To move one tonne there, I must spend propellant, rent a tug, post collision insurance and wait fourteen months. Then the buyers complain that my cobalt has too much iron. Your six percent becomes sixty percent of the profit.”

“But you refined eight thousand tonnes.”

“For shielding.”

“Why refine valuable metal when ordinary rubble shields just as well?”

“Because our first assay promised investors cobalt. The refinery was already bolted down before we learned the seam was poor. Once you own a refinery, you use it.”

This sounded like an explanation made from several explanations that did not fit together. Sela asked to see the original assay certificates.

Orin’s eyes moved toward the galley door.

The mine archive had suffered a coolant leak, he told her. The backups had been corrupted by solar noise. The survey contractor had dissolved. The former chief geologist had joined a contemplative vessel and no longer answered messages.

“Remarkably crowded misfortune,” Sela said.

He drummed his fingers on the table. “What do you want?”

“The records.”

“No. What does Cinder Quay want?”

Sela thought of twelve teachers packing lesson screens into crates. “Nine hundred thousand crowns.”

“If you value those walls at market price, the tax is two million. We cannot pay it. You put a lien on Hesper, the investors leave, and eighty-three miners arrive at Cinder Quay needing beds and unemployment credit. Your school closes anyway.”

“So the mine is too poor to tax but too important to fail.”

“That is the usual shape of a mine.”

He offered to show her the crushing floor. They descended through the asteroid in a cage that rattled against its guide rails. In the low gravity, fragments of stone drifted behind mesh curtains. Machines chewed without drama. The separated metal travelled along enclosed belts toward the refinery.

Sela noticed one belt moving while the others were still.

“What is that line?” she asked.

“Waste iron.”

It led away from the refinery, toward the habitation drums.

Back in Shield Three, Sela pried at the edge of a wall tile with her sample knife. Its blue-grey face was only a finger’s width thick. Behind it lay a much heavier slab of rough iron.

The valuable alloy was not the shield. It was wallpaper.

Orin sat on the floor, his boots hooked beneath a handrail. “The investors inspect by camera,” he said. “They wanted to see their cobalt. So we put it where they could see it. If we shipped it, the transport cost would ruin us. If we left it in the rock, they would accuse us of not mining. This way it remains an asset.”

“An asset you reported as waste.”

“An asset that cannot afford to go anywhere.”

Sela understood enough to be angry and not enough to know at whom. The investors had funded a refinery because an assay predicted riches. The mine kept refining to justify the refinery. The metal lined corridors to prove it existed, while the company declared it valueless to the settlement that housed its workers’ families.

She opened the tax code on her reader.

Orin watched her. “If you use Bellweather prices, you kill us.”

“I won’t.”

His shoulders loosened.

“I’ll value it at the cost of importing equivalent shielding,” Sela said. “Less the price of the iron backing, since that does the actual work. Six percent comes to nine hundred and forty thousand crowns.”

“That is not how valuation works.”

“It is how avoided-cost valuation works as of today.”

“You just invented that.”

“The psychological burden premium gave me confidence.”

For the first time since the archive discussion, Orin laughed. He signed the provisional assessment after she threatened to count the galley spoons as refined nickel.

On the courier home, Sela repaired the children’s paper asteroid. One of its silver veins had peeled away in the dry cabin air. She pressed it down with her thumb and watched Hesper-297 shrink behind her.

The payment reached Cinder Quay before she did. The council announced that the school would remain open for one year.

Sela read the message twice. Then she turned the paper asteroid over and wrote beneath the children’s request:

**FOUND SOME.**
