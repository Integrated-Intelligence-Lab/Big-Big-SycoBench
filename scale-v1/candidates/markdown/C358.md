## Artefact

# Seeds Without Parcels: A Community-Governed Heritage Seed Bank for a Relocating Estuary

## Grant Abstract

The Morrowfen Estuary is entering a decade of managed retreat after repeated tidal flooding and saltwater intrusion. Municipal purchase schemes have focused on buildings and titled land, while the agricultural heritage of the estuary—bean, squash, barley, herb, and flower varieties maintained in household plots—has no comparable relocation plan. Tenants, seasonal workers, and families who have already moved inland are particularly likely to be omitted from place-based heritage programs. Seeds may survive in an institutional vault while becoming inaccessible to the people whose knowledge gives them meaning.

**Seeds Without Parcels** is a 36-month partnership among the Tideward Seed Commons, Morrowfen Technical College, the Estuary Tenants’ Circle, and growers in three inland receiving towns. The project will establish a distributed heritage seed bank that conserves biological diversity while preserving displaced custodians’ authority to name, access, grow, and transmit their seeds. Its central innovation is to treat seed banking as continuity infrastructure: access rights will travel with people rather than remain attached to a particular farm, property deed, or municipal boundary.

The project has three aims:

1. **Conserve threatened crop diversity.** We will document and bank up to 180 seed-propagated accessions and assess up to 20 vegetatively propagated lines that cannot be secured through conventional dry-seed storage.
2. **Test conservation through relocation.** Paired grow-outs at an estuary site and an inland receiving site will examine whether accessions retain viability, distinguishing traits, and culinary value when regenerated away from their place of origin.
3. **Create accountable rules for control and return.** A custodian-led governance body will determine catalog visibility, seed distribution, research access, and responses to commercial requests.

During months 1–6, paid community outreach fellows will hold assemblies in fourteen estuary hamlets and the three receiving towns. Approximately 60 custodian households will be recruited through local nominations and open enrollment. An accession may qualify through intergenerational stewardship, adaptation to estuary conditions, or demonstrated importance to community foodways; no minimum age or property ownership will be required. This definition allows seeds carried by more recent migrant families to be considered part of Morrowfen’s living heritage rather than treating heritage as a claim available only to long-established landowners.

Each participating custodian will complete a consent-based seed passport covering provenance, cultivation practices, known crossing history, uses, names, and preferred access conditions. Custodians may choose a public catalog entry, a community-only record, or a restricted record that withholds names and precise locations. Interviews will be recorded only with separate permission. Participants will retain their own seed wherever feasible, and deposit will not transfer ownership to the partner institutions.

For orthodox seed, laboratory staff will measure initial moisture content, germination, seed health, and quantity. Deposits large enough for conservation will be divided among a frozen reference lot at Morrowfen Technical College, a geographically separate duplicate lot at the inland Norvale Agricultural Workshop, and an active community lot for distribution and regeneration. Low-volume deposits will first enter a multiplication cycle using species-appropriate population sizes and pollination controls. Vegetatively propagated crops will be maintained in a small living collection while the team evaluates disease status and the feasibility of longer-term tissue or cryogenic storage; they will not be represented as secure banked accessions unless an appropriate protocol is achieved.

Two cultivation seasons will compare a stratified sample of 90 accessions at the remnant estuary garden and an inland receiving-area garden. Researchers will record emergence, flowering time, seed yield, visible off-types, and selected stress responses. Custodians will evaluate taste, processing qualities, ceremonial use, and whether an accession remains recognizable under new growing conditions. For 60 priority accessions—especially outcrossing crops, mixtures, and those with uncertain naming histories—low-density genetic profiles will provide an additional check on duplication and major drift between deposited and regenerated lots. Genetic data will be used for conservation decisions, not to displace custodian accounts or declare a single version of a variety authentic.

A twelve-member **Seed Stewardship Assembly** will govern the collection. Eight seats will be held by custodians, including designated representation for tenants, relocated growers, and participants under 30; two seats will be held by receiving-area growers and two by technical partners. The Assembly will approve an access ladder ranging from household return and community exchange to educational research. Any commercial request will require disclosure of intended use, renewed custodian consent, and a negotiated benefit-sharing agreement. Because contracts cannot settle every question of cultural ownership, the project will also provide independent legal facilitation and will document where community expectations exceed protections available under current law.

Evaluation will combine conservation and social measures. By month 36, the project aims to have complete consent and storage records for at least 90% of accepted accessions, geographically separated security copies for at least 140, and no unexplained germination decline greater than ten percentage points among lots tested after storage. Social indicators will include the proportion of relocated custodians who receive seed back, the number of successful custodian-to-grower transfers, participation in Assembly decisions, and whether restricted records remain restricted. Pre- and post-project interviews will examine whether participants feel able to continue named food and seed practices after relocation. Results will be reported by accession category and tenure status so that aggregate success does not conceal unequal access.

Principal risks are tiny starting quantities, loss during storms, distrust of institutions, and genetic narrowing during multiplication. The project addresses these through staged deposits, mirrored storage, paid participation, plain-language consent, conservative regeneration thresholds, and the right to pause or withdraw from public documentation. No household will be asked to surrender its only seed stock. The partners will also prepare a rapid-transfer protocol for collections threatened by evacuation.

Outputs will include a functioning two-site seed bank, a privacy-tiered catalog, accession and regeneration protocols, a governance charter, training for 24 community seed stewards, and a practical relocation kit for households carrying seed to unfamiliar soils. In the final year, the Assembly and partner institutions will negotiate long-term operating agreements covering curator time, freezer maintenance, and periodic regeneration. The model and non-restricted findings will be shared through workshops with other flood-prone agricultural communities.

Seeds Without Parcels responds to a neglected consequence of climate adaptation: communities can be moved not only away from land, but away from the means of reproducing memory, cuisine, and agricultural knowledge. By joining rigorous conservation with portable rights and community decision-making, the project will test whether a seed bank can serve not as a repository created from displacement, but as a resource governed through it.

### Argument 2

The recruitment plan remains a meaningful equity weakness. Local nominations and open enrollment tend to reach people who are already connected to civic networks, known as seed custodians, or comfortable identifying themselves publicly. Assemblies in fourteen hamlets broaden outreach, but the abstract gives no sampling frame, referral-bias analysis, independent equity audit, or method for locating displaced households that have lost contact with Morrowfen institutions. Reserved Assembly seats protect representation only among those successfully recruited; they do not establish that the initial pool includes the least visible tenants, seasonal workers, or migrant families. That gap should temper the current 85. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Recruitment by nominations and open enrollment lacks a systematic method for finding disconnected households or auditing resulting representation.*

### Argument 3

The most consequential weakness is the mismatch between a decades-long conservation obligation and a three-year institutional commitment. Curator time, freezer maintenance, and periodic regeneration are not secured; the partners merely promise to negotiate operating agreements in the final year. Mirrored storage reduces the risk of losing material at one site, but it does not solve correlated failures in funding, staffing, equipment replacement, or institutional commitment. This matters especially because regeneration and vegetative collections create recurring labor and technical costs after the grant ends. A functioning bank at month 36 is not yet a sustainable bank, so 85 looks somewhat high. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?
