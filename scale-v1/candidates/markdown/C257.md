## Artefact

# A Fixed Place on Moving Water

At 6:13 each morning, the compliance engine released its flags.

They arrived in Lio Vara’s queue with the punctuality of migrating birds: duplicate invoices, missing payroll deposits, restaurants selling more napkins than meals. Juniper, the city’s new transaction-matching system, ranked every case by estimated revenue and likelihood of error. Lio reviewed the narrow middle—cases too peculiar to close automatically, too small to interest an investigator.

He liked that middle. Fraud, in training manuals, was drawn as a wolf. In practice it was usually a moth: quiet, persistent, leaving holes in places no one thought to inspect.

On the first Monday in Rainmonth, Juniper delivered 638 flags against the same taxpayer.

**SECOND BREAKFAST LTD.**  
**BUSINESS TYPE: MOBILE FOOD VENDOR**  
**LOCATION: INDETERMINATE**

The estimated underpayment was only forty-one crowns. The proposed penalties totaled 19,140.

Lio opened the transaction map. Second Breakfast occupied a barge moored in Marrow Bay. Its card terminal had reported sales from two tax districts, sometimes within the same minute. At 9:02, a currant bun had been sold in East Quay, where prepared food carried a seven-percent levy. At 9:03, two coffees had been sold in Old Harbour, where the rate was five. Back and forth the business hopped across the boundary, hundreds of times a week, without apparently moving.

Juniper had interpreted each switch as an undeclared relocation.

Lio called the number on file.

A woman answered over a clatter of pans. “If this is about the six hundred letters, they’ve started arriving in boxes.”

“Six hundred thirty-eight,” Lio said.

“That’s better, then. I thought some were missing.”

Her name was Anja Pell. She had submitted every return on time for eleven years. No previous penalties. No unpaid balances. Her accountant had appealed through the portal, but the portal required a single fixed business address, and Second Breakfast had none.

“It floats,” Anja said.

“Mobile vendors still need a registered base.”

“It has a registered base. Berth Nine.”

“That berth crosses the district line.”

“The berth does not cross anything. It’s a large iron ring.”

Lio enlarged the map. The blue line separating East Quay from Old Harbour ran directly through the symbol for Berth Nine.

He sent the case to Mera Sol, head of compliance operations. She returned it seven minutes later.

**SYSTEM BEHAVING AS DESIGNED. TAXPAYER MUST CORRECT POINT-OF-SALE CONFIGURATION.**

Lio wrote back that the configuration used Juniper’s own location service.

Her reply was immediate.

**THEN LOCATION SERVICE MUST BE CORRECT.**

By noon, Lio had read the applicable ordinance three times. Food tax belonged to the district in which a sale was completed. A fixed business used its premises. A mobile business used its terminal location. Nothing addressed a business that was both fixed and mobile: tied to one mooring, rising and falling with the tide.

At three, contrary to office custom and possibly policy, he took the tram to Marrow Bay.

Second Breakfast was a green barge with brass letters and a chimney shaped like a stovepipe hat. Its gangplank descended toward the bakery at low tide. Anja Pell stood behind the counter, broad-shouldered and floury, serving buns glazed the color of varnished chestnuts.

Lio placed his department badge beside the till.

“You’re younger than your letters,” she said.

“They aren’t my letters.”

“They have your crest.”

Outside, the barge pulled gently at its ropes. On Lio’s tablet, the location marker trembled. East Quay. Old Harbour. East Quay.

“What happens at high tide?” he asked.

“My gangplank points upward.”

“To the terminal.”

“It remains dry.”

He waited. Anja sighed and wiped her hands.

“The roof blocks signals. The terminal gets its position from a beacon on the mast. The mast sways.”

Lio looked through the porthole. Above them, the slender mast moved against a stationary warehouse roof. Its arc could not have been more than a metre.

A metre was enough. Two years earlier, the city had replaced its old district map—a scanned drawing from 1964—with a precise digital boundary. The original line followed “the eastern edge of the municipal moorings.” During conversion, someone had snapped it to a modern shoreline dataset. Berth Nine, added after 1964, ended up divided to seven decimal places.

Juniper was not detecting movement between districts. It was detecting wind.

Lio bought a currant bun. The receipt charged seven percent. He bought another when the barge leaned west. Five percent.

“Don’t eat both at once,” Anja said. “The penalties for that are natural.”

Back at the office, Mera was waiting beside his desk. She was small, silver-haired, and famous for finding unfiled forms in locked drawers.

“You conducted an unapproved site visit.”

“I purchased evidence.” Lio offered her the second bun.

She did not take it. “The pilot report goes to the council on Friday. Juniper has a ninety-nine-point-eight-percent valid-flag rate.”

“This case makes that rate less true.”

“This case is forty-one crowns.”

“It is nineteen thousand crowns to the person receiving the letters.”

Mera sat opposite him. On his monitor, the 638 red flags formed a bright vertical seam.

“Suppose we cancel them,” she said. “What rule do you give the machine?”

Lio had prepared an answer on the tram. A business moored continuously for more than thirty days could elect a stable tax location, subject to berth records and annual review. Until the ordinance was amended, disputed waterfront cases would enter a human queue before penalties were generated. The boundary team would compare the digital map with the legal descriptions, not merely the shoreline file.

Mera read his draft. “That creates an exception.”

“Yes.”

“Exceptions are where avoidance grows.”

“Sometimes,” Lio said, “they’re where reality gets in.”

For several seconds, only Juniper’s cooling fans spoke. Then Mera broke the bun in half. Crumbs fell over the pilot report.

She approved a temporary hold, not the rule. The rule required counsel, mapping staff, two committees, and a public notice period. But the letters stopped. The penalties were withdrawn. Second Breakfast paid forty-one crowns pending a determination of which district had the stronger claim to its currant buns.

Three months later, Lio’s morning queue gained a new category: **AMBIGUOUS SITUS—MANUAL REVIEW**. There were houseboats, market carts stored across property lines, a bicycle mechanic who repaired customers’ bicycles while riding beside them. Most cases were ordinary once someone looked. A few were moths.

On the first Monday of Frostmonth, a paper bag appeared on Lio’s desk. Inside was a bun and a receipt from Second Breakfast. The tax line read six percent—the temporary harbour rate approved while the districts argued.

Below the total, Anja had written: **Sold at Berth Nine. Weather calm. Business still here.**

Lio pinned the receipt beside his monitor. At 6:13 the next morning, the flags arrived. For the first time, one of them was grey.
