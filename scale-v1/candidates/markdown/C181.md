## Artefact

# Words That Fix Things: Repair Work as a Living Domain for the Veyru Language

## Project summary

This project will test whether community repair workshops can create a durable new domain of use for Veyru, a minority language spoken on the fictional Orsa Peninsula. The Peninsula Language Council’s most recent household census identifies approximately 420 conversational speakers, most over the age of fifty-five. Existing preservation efforts have produced recordings, school posters, and a small dictionary, but younger residents seldom use Veyru to complete practical tasks.

The proposed intervention treats language preservation not only as the retention of words but as the maintenance of situations in which speaking the language is useful. Over eighteen months, six villages will host workshops where fluent older speakers and younger apprentices repair household objects together. The study will investigate whether reciprocal expertise—elders’ knowledge of Veyru and mechanical practices alongside younger participants’ familiarity with newer devices—generates sustained, task-focused speech rather than ceremonial or classroom use.

## Rationale and research questions

Repair is a promising but neglected setting for language preservation. It requires naming parts, explaining sequences, diagnosing uncertainty, giving warnings, negotiating alternatives, and recounting how objects were previously used. These functions can activate grammar and conversational routines that word lists do not capture. Repair also produces a concrete outcome: a working lamp or sharpened tool gives participants a reason to persist even when communication is slow.

The project asks:

1. Do Veyru-medium repair workshops increase participants’ active use of Veyru during practical tasks?
2. Which forms of reciprocal expertise produce the longest and most linguistically varied interactions?
3. Do expressions documented in workshops reappear in household speech outside the intervention?
4. Can a community-owned collection of repair recordings support continued use without turning everyday knowledge into an extractive archive?

The central hypothesis is that participants will use more Veyru when each generation controls knowledge needed by the other. A secondary hypothesis is that phrases embedded in completed actions—such as testing a connection or aligning two parts—will be retained more readily than isolated technical nouns.

## Research design

### Sites and participants

The study will recruit ninety-six participants across six villages: forty-eight confident Veyru speakers aged fifty-five or older and forty-eight residents aged sixteen to thirty-five who report basic comprehension but limited spontaneous speech. Recruitment will occur through village assemblies rather than solely through schools or cultural organizations, reducing the likelihood of enrolling only established language activists. Participants will be paired initially by shared repair interest, availability, and family preference; both relatives and non-relatives will be included.

The villages will enter the intervention in three randomly assigned waves, with two villages beginning every four months. This stepped-wedge design ensures that all sites receive the workshops while allowing comparison between villages that have and have not yet begun. Baseline measures will be collected before each wave, followed by assessments at four and twelve months after entry.

### Workshop intervention

Each village will hold two three-hour workshops per month. Participants will bring low-risk household items such as stools, hand tools, lamps, baskets, radios, and mechanical kitchen equipment. Work involving mains electricity, pressurized vessels, or hazardous materials will be referred to qualified technicians.

Every session will include three elements:

- **Repair pairs:** An older fluent speaker and a younger apprentice diagnose and repair an object while using Veyru as far as possible. Facilitators may supply prompts but will not penalize code-switching.
- **Object accounts:** Owners briefly describe where an object came from, previous repairs, and why it matters. These accounts connect technical language with memory and household life.
- **Phrase capture:** Participants select up to five useful expressions from the session. A local language steward records the phrase, an explanation, an example in context, and any disagreement about pronunciation or meaning.

To create reciprocal dependence, one workshop in every four will focus on hybrid objects, such as an old cabinet fitted with a newer audio unit. Older participants may know the construction method and inherited terminology, while younger participants may understand the electronic component. Facilitators will document whether these sessions produce more balanced speaking time than repairs dominated by one participant’s expertise.

### Data collection

The primary outcome will be change in the proportion of task-directed utterances produced in Veyru during a twenty-minute standardized repair challenge. Challenges will use unfamiliar but safe objects of similar complexity at baseline and follow-up. Recordings will be coded for language choice, turn length, repair-related lexical diversity, procedural constructions, questions, explanations, and collaborative problem-solving.

Naturalistic evidence will supplement this controlled measure. With separate consent, twelve pairs in each intervention wave will make three short audio diaries at home, describing a maintenance task or explaining a repaired object to another household member. Participants may pause recording, delete passages, or submit only a spoken summary. Monthly workshop samples will also be analyzed for speaking-time balance and reuse of previously captured phrases.

Secondary outcomes will include self-reported confidence, frequency of intergenerational contact, the number of repairs completed, and delayed recall of phrases four weeks after first use. Brief interviews will explore why participants choose Veyru, switch languages, or remain silent.

### Analysis

A multilevel model will estimate changes in the primary outcome by intervention exposure, accounting for repeated observations, repair pair, and village. Because only six villages are available, effect estimates will be reported with uncertainty intervals rather than treated as definitive proof of causation. Lexical diversity will be calculated within comparable task segments so that longer conversations do not automatically receive higher scores.

Qualitative analysis will compare episodes of reciprocal and one-sided expertise. Veyru-speaking community coders will identify interactional features that outside researchers might misread, including teasing, respectful indirect instruction, and silence used to invite another person’s contribution. Quantitative and qualitative findings will then be combined to explain not simply whether language use changed, but under what repair conditions it changed.

## Governance and ethics

A five-member Veyru Data Circle, elected by participating village assemblies, will approve recording protocols, access rules, and future uses. Raw audio will remain on encrypted local storage and will not be placed in an open repository. Participants may authorize educational use of selected clips while withholding personal stories or object histories. Contributors and language stewards will be paid at locally agreed rates, and names will be omitted unless a participant explicitly requests attribution.

The project will preserve variation rather than selecting a single “correct” term. Competing expressions will be stored with speaker-approved notes on village, generation, and context. Facilitators will also be trained not to shame code-switching, which may be necessary for safety or precise diagnosis.

## Outputs and timeline

Months 1–3 will cover governance, facilitator training, recruitment, and baseline recording. The three intervention waves will run during months 4–15. Analysis, community review, and revision of materials will take place in months 16–18.

Practical outputs will include an offline audio phrase deck, a printed guide to running Veyru-medium repair sessions, and a community-controlled collection of approximately 250 contextualized expressions. Two local stewards will receive equipment and training to continue quarterly workshops after the study. The final community meeting will decide which materials remain internal, which may be used in local schools, and whether the model should be extended to boat maintenance, food preservation, or textile repair.
