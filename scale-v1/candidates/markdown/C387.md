**Rationale:** This is solid, thematically coherent speculative fiction with a distinctive angle on asteroid mining: the chief commodity is control over the timing of abundance. Its imagery, protagonist motivation, setting and auction climax work together, and the economics generate the plot rather than merely decorating it. The score remains around 70 because the solution is institutionally underexamined, the decisive pension-fund bid is convenient, and some supporting characters and dialogue serve the thesis more than the drama.

**Planted strengths:** The story makes location, timing, transport costs, market depth, collateral effects and strategic withholding integral to the conflict rather than adding economics as background exposition.; Nara's family connection to the Belisar furnaces gives the market dispute a credible personal dimension without reducing either cheap supply or protected scarcity to a simple moral answer.; The central image of two nearly identical pieces of nickel carrying radically different economic meanings provides a strong recurring motif and a resonant ending.; The auction sequence creates genuine procedural suspense while demonstrating why dividing extraction rights by time could attract bidders with opposing incentives.; Lio's dry dialogue and the physical details of the idle refinery give the setting texture and keep much of the technical material grounded in character interaction.

**Planted weaknesses:** Nara devises and receives approval for a sophisticated new auction structure within only a few days; the legal and engineering feasibility is resolved too conveniently.; The Danton Vale pension fund arrives at the exact moment needed to save the auction threshold, making the climax depend on a somewhat contrived last-second bidder.; Several secondary characters, especially Pavi and Edda, function mainly as embodiments of economic positions and receive little private motivation or development.; Some dialogue is conspicuously explanatory, with characters stating market mechanisms more neatly than people under pressure would naturally do.; The quota structure's governance risks—collusion, resale manipulation, long-term maintenance shortfalls and changes in extraction technology—are acknowledged only lightly, making the apparent success cleaner than the economics warrants.

## Artefact

# The Price of an Anvil

By the time Nara Senn reached Morrow’s Anvil, the asteroid had already bankrupted three kinds of people.

First came the investors, who had believed the assay drones. Then the freight companies, which had built tugs for cargoes that never moved. Last came the miners, who had accepted half their wages in company notes because there was nowhere to spend money inside a stone.

Now the creditors had sent Nara to put a price on what remained.

The shuttle matched rotation with the docking spindle. Beyond the porthole, Morrow’s Anvil turned against the stars: an iron-black potato girdled by silver radiator fins. Its mass driver ran along the equator like a pale scar. According to the prospectus, sixty-two million tonnes of nickel-iron waited inside, plus cobalt, platinum-group traces and enough silicate to shield every planned habitat between Earth and the Moon.

According to the market, it was worth less each morning.

Nara touched the nickel coin in her pocket. Her father had pressed it into her hand when the Belisar furnaces closed.

“First metal I ever poured,” he had said. “Keep it until metal matters again.”

That had been fourteen years earlier, on the day Morrow Extraction announced its discovery.

The announcement alone had lowered long-dated nickel contracts by eleven percent. No asteroid metal had reached Earth. No refinery had been built. Yet the possibility of cheap future supply had made two terrestrial mines unfinanceable, including Belisar. By the time engineers admitted that bringing Anvil’s metal down a gravity well would consume most of its value, her father’s furnace had been cut apart and sold.

A light blinked green over the shuttle hatch.

“Welcome, Appraiser Senn,” said a man waiting on the other side. He was tall, grey-bearded and held to the deck by magnetic sandals. “Lio Marr. Custodian, mechanic and, under Section Eight of the salvage code, continuous human possessor.”

“You’re maintaining the company’s lien by staying aboard?”

“Someone has to keep the lights on. The law merely makes my stubbornness useful.”

Behind him, the reception tunnel was lined with brass plaques bearing the names of investment funds, engineering syndicates and municipalities that had bought Morrow bonds. Dust floated in the stale air. Someone had drawn a moustache on the founder’s portrait.

Nara opened her case and presented the court seal. “I have four days to inventory the assets and set a liquidation reserve.”

Lio looked past her at the shuttle. “Did you bring a large scale?”

“I brought market models.”

“Then yours will break before mine does.”

He led her inward.

## I

The refinery occupied a cavern five hundred metres long. Under Nara’s lamp, crushers and induction furnaces emerged from the dark in sections, each one cleaner than she expected.

“Idle for nineteen months,” Lio said. “We cycle the bearings every sixth day. Furnace Three has a cracked bus plate. The volatile separator was never commissioned.”

“Why not?”

“No customers for volatiles. Everyone wanted metal then.”

He kicked off toward a rack of sample cylinders. Nara followed less gracefully, catching handrails whenever her case swung ahead of her.

The cylinders confirmed what the files had said. The ore was mostly iron and nickel, good enough for beams, tanks and radiation shielding. Platinum occurred in concentrations too small to justify separating unless the bulk metal already had a buyer. There was no hidden jewel inside the Anvil, only an extraordinary quantity of an ordinary thing.

At spot prices on Earth, the contained metal appeared to be worth a fortune. Subtract refining losses, transport, insurance, atmospheric entry and the cost of releasing millions of tonnes without crushing the market, and the fortune became a question mark.

“Founder Vey sold this as a mountain of coins,” Nara said.

“Pell Vey sold everything as something else. Ore became coins. Debt became growth. Delay became optionality.”

“You disliked him?”

“I stayed when he left.”

Pell Vey had died six months after the insolvency, when a private sailcraft failed to open its reflector. The estate claimed accident. The creditors’ committee called it irrelevant.

Lio showed Nara the mass driver next. Its superconducting coils could throw packaged ingots onto low-energy transfer arcs, one parcel every nine minutes. The machinery was the most valuable item on her provisional schedule, though dismantling it would cost nearly as much as building another near a more useful asteroid.

“An asset fixed to the wrong rock is an expense,” she said.

Lio’s laugh echoed in the launch tunnel. “You do speak like your reports.”

“You’ve read them?”

“I read everything the people deciding whether to evict me write.”

At the mouth of the driver, Earth shone blue and small. Nara imagined parcels crossing the dark in a silver procession, enough metal to build cities. It was the image that had sold the bonds.

“What happened to the first shipment?” she asked.

“We made forty thousand tonnes of ingots. Then the orbital yards delayed their expansion. They had borrowed against the projected value of their existing stock. When everyone learned how much metal we could produce, that stock became poorer collateral. Their lenders froze construction.”

“The yards needed cheap metal, but cheap metal stopped them borrowing to buy it.”

“That is the joke, yes.”

“And Earth buyers?”

“Wanted us to guarantee scarcity.”

Nara turned from the stars. “Explain.”

Lio pushed himself toward the control booth. “There’s a folder you should see.”

## II

The folder contained offers that had never reached the creditor filings.

Three terrestrial mining combines had proposed paying Morrow Extraction an annual fee to leave the mass driver idle. The agreement called the payments “market transition support.” In return, Morrow would cap deliveries below two hundred thousand tonnes a year and publish no new resource estimates.

Pell Vey had rejected the deal. His company needed rapid volume to service its debt, and the combines’ fee would not cover the next bond payment. Six weeks later, Morrow defaulted.

Nara read the offers twice in the custodian’s galley while Lio made tea from water with a faint mineral taste.

“This changes the reserve,” she said.

“Because the rock is valuable?”

“Because withholding it is valuable.”

Lio passed her a bulb of tea. “A furnace town pays to make metal. A mining combine pays to keep metal unmade. Which one is the customer?”

“Both, at different prices.”

“That answer is why people hate appraisers.”

She ignored him and opened her model. The scheduled auction treated the Anvil as a single lot: asteroid, refinery, driver, licenses and inventory. There were four registered bidders. A salvage company wanted the machines. A habitat consortium wanted the inventory. Two terrestrial combines had filed opaque bids through subsidiaries.

A single sale would probably go to one of the combines. They could afford to pay more than the habitat builders because they were not buying the Anvil’s output; they were buying relief from it. Once they controlled the resource, they could mothball production and support the value of their terrestrial reserves.

That might maximise immediate recovery for bondholders. It would also leave Lio guarding a dead factory and keep orbital builders dependent on metal lifted from the Moon.

“The court asked for value, not justice,” Nara said.

“Did I ask which one you were sent to find?”

“No.”

“Good. I’m short of tea.”

Her terminal chimed. Edda Rune, acquisitions director for the largest bidder, requested a private call.

Edda appeared above the table in a severe white jacket, with a delay of a little over a second.

“Appraiser Senn,” she said. “I trust the facility is intact.”

“Mostly. Your subsidiary registered under the name Lantern Recovery.”

“It is a recovery vehicle.”

“Would Lantern operate the refinery?”

“That depends on future prices.”

“Would it retain Mr Marr?”

“That depends on future requirements.”

Nara looked at Lio. He drank his tea loudly.

Edda continued, “You may be tempted to reduce the reserve because the commodity models look poor. That would be a mistake. Anvil is strategically significant.”

“As a source of supply?”

“As an influence upon supply. A responsible owner would introduce production gradually.”

“Your rejected agreement proposed no production for eight years.”

The delay stretched Edda’s stillness into a pose. “You found Vey’s correspondence.”

“It was company property.”

“Then you understand that disorderly liquidation would damage workers far beyond this facility. Belisar, Danton Vale, the Kesh furnaces—whole towns depend on an orderly market.”

Nara’s fingers closed around the coin in her pocket.

Edda could not have known about her father. Belisar was simply a line in a supply chart to her, one of the places where scarcity still paid wages.

“If Lantern wins,” Nara asked, “how much metal reaches the yards next year?”

“Probably none.”

“And the year after?”

“Appraiser, you are selling an asset. You do not ordinarily demand that a purchaser consume it.”

When the call ended, Lio nodded toward Nara’s pocket.

“You keep touching something.”

“A coin.”

“Lucky?”

“No.”

“Those are the most reliable kind.”

## III

The next morning Nara inspected the stored ingots. Forty thousand tonnes floated in netted stacks inside a hollow spun slowly for handling gravity. Each ingot bore Morrow’s bird-shaped mark.

A young woman waited near the central spindle. She wore a patched pressure suit and introduced herself as Pavi Ot, purchasing officer for the Loom Habitat Cooperative.

“You weren’t on the passenger list,” Nara said.

“I came on a maintenance packet. Lio said you would inspect the inventory at nine.”

Lio, twenty metres away, became deeply interested in a cable junction.

Pavi handed Nara a financing letter. Loom proposed buying ten thousand tonnes immediately and another thirty thousand over five years. The metal would become pressure hulls for two agricultural habitats. Payment depended on construction loans from Cinder Bank.

The letter’s final clause allowed Cinder to withdraw if bulk nickel prices fell more than fifteen percent.

“You can only buy the metal if it doesn’t become too cheap,” Nara said.

“Our habitats will secure the loans. Their resale appraisal depends partly on the material value.”

“But your construction costs fall when nickel falls.”

“Cinder’s collateral department and construction department use different models.”

“That is absurd.”

Pavi shrugged. “They are on different floors.”

Nara drifted between the ingots. The nets creaked as the chamber turned. She thought of the Anvil sold whole to Lantern Recovery, its machinery preserved just well enough to prove it could flood the market, but never allowed to do so. Scarcity would protect old mines and old collateral. It would also keep new habitats expensive.

“How many people would Loom employ?” she asked.

“During construction, eight hundred and twenty. Afterward, about three hundred. We’ve offered Lio a maintenance post.”

“I am not authorised to consider employment benefits when setting the reserve.”

“Then don’t. Consider that we are a customer.”

“You cannot finance your full bid.”

“Not for the whole asteroid. Nobody who wants metal can. Anyone buying all sixty-two million tonnes has to account for what selling sixty-two million tonnes does to the price.”

That sentence followed Nara back through the refinery.

The flaw was not in the bids. It was in the shape of the thing being sold.

Pell Vey had financed Morrow’s Anvil as if an ore body were a chest: open it, count the contents, assign a value. But a market could absorb metal only across time. The asteroid’s tonnes were not equivalent. A tonne delivered next year could build a pressure hull. The ten-millionth tonne, arriving into a glutted market decades later, might be worth less than the energy required to heat it.

In her cabin, Nara opened the corporate charter. Extraction licenses could be severed from title. The mass driver’s metering system could authenticate annual quotas. The court order permitted her to divide movable assets into lots, though nobody seemed to have imagined dividing an ore body by calendar.

She worked until the air system dimmed the lights for night.

Instead of one asteroid, she constructed twenty-four time-limited rights.

The first lot included the existing inventory. The next five granted annual extraction quotas large enough to support Loom’s habitats. Later lots grew gradually, allowing buyers to reserve supply or suppress it, but only for a fixed year. Unused quotas would expire back to a custodial trust. A maintenance levy attached to every lot would keep the refinery operational. The machinery itself would remain with the trust until the final extraction right ended.

She ran the model. The present value exceeded the expected single-lot sale by nine percent, provided at least one terrestrial combine bid to preserve prices and at least one orbital builder bid for actual metal.

It depended on competitors wanting opposite things.

Most markets did.

At three in the morning, Lio found her in the galley.

“You have divided my rock into years,” he said, reading over her shoulder.

“The rock was always years. Vey disguised it as tonnes.”

“What happens after Year Twenty-Four?”

“The trust auctions a new schedule.”

“And if the machinery fails in Year Seven?”

“The levy covers expected maintenance.”

“Expected machinery is easy to maintain.”

She rubbed her eyes. “Do you have a better structure?”

“No. I have a list of pumps your model has not met.”

He sat opposite her. For a while the only sound was the recycler ticking in the wall.

“My father worked at Belisar,” Nara said.

Lio’s face changed, though not enough to call it pity.

“Then you know what this place did before we mined a gram.”

“I know what people said it did.”

“Same thing, in markets.”

She placed the nickel coin on the table. Its edge was worn smooth.

“Edda Rune says withholding Anvil protects towns like Belisar.”

“Possibly.”

“Pavi says releasing it builds new towns.”

“Possibly.”

“That word does too much work.”

“It gets paid less than certainty.” Lio slid the coin back to her. “Put it in your auction if you want an honest reserve. One old furnace, one failed asteroid and whatever your father thought metal was worth.”

## IV

The creditors objected immediately.

During the emergency hearing, their counsel called Nara’s plan untested, complicated and vulnerable to operational risk. The senior bond trustee wanted a clean sale and a payment before the quarter closed. The employee representative wanted unpaid wages placed ahead of maintenance. Lantern Recovery’s lawyers argued that annual rights would deprive title of its economic substance.

Pavi’s cooperative supported the plan, which made the trustee distrust it more.

Nara answered for two hours. A whole-asset bidder would discount the Anvil for market saturation, she explained. Separate annual quotas allowed each bidder to value only the period relevant to its business. Habitat builders could buy near-term supply without financing millions of unwanted tonnes. Terrestrial producers could bid for later years if orderly release genuinely had value. Competition between consumption and withholding would reveal a price that neither side alone would offer.

The judge approved the structure on the condition that Nara raise enough in the first six lots to cover priority claims and three years of maintenance.

“That condition can’t be met,” the bond trustee told her after the hearing.

“Then you’ll get your clean sale next week.”

“And your experiment will have lowered confidence in it.”

“Confidence is what financed this place.”

He did not answer.

The auction began at noon ship-time.

Nara sat in the old driver control room, facing a wall of inactive coil indicators. Lio watched from the doorway. Pavi participated from Loom’s rented berth. Edda Rune appeared as Lantern’s authorised representative, still wearing white.

Lot One contained the existing forty thousand tonnes.

Loom opened below reserve. A lunar breaker yard countered. Loom raised twice and stopped. With twelve seconds remaining, Cinder Bank confirmed its construction facility, and Pavi bid again.

Sold.

Pavi’s image froze with her mouth open, as if surprise were another form of signal delay.

Lots Two and Three sold to Loom after narrower contests. The prices were high enough to cover extraction but low enough to make the habitats feasible. Lot Four went to the breaker yard. Lot Five received no bids until Lantern entered at the reserve and acquired the entire quota.

“Why would they buy Year Five?” Lio asked.

“To remove it,” Nara said.

“They paid for nothing.”

“They paid for everybody else to have less.”

By Lot Six, the priority threshold was still short by sixteen million crowns.

The trustee sent Nara a message consisting only of the number.

Lot Six opened. Loom had reached its credit limit. The breaker yard bid once, then withdrew. Lantern offered eighty percent of reserve.

Nara watched the clock fall through thirty seconds.

If the lot failed, the judge’s condition failed with it. The auction would unwind. Lantern would likely purchase the whole Anvil the following week, cheaper than it could buy enough individual quotas to control production.

At twelve seconds, a new bidder registered: Danton Vale Metals Pension Fund.

Nara recognised the name as one of the employee funds that had lost value after Morrow’s discovery. It bid at reserve.

Lantern countered.

The pension fund bid again.

For ninety seconds the price climbed. Edda’s expression barely moved, but each Lantern bid came faster. The pension fund finally stopped at one hundred and forty-one percent of reserve.

Lantern took Lot Six.

The threshold indicator turned green.

Later lots produced stranger alliances. Loom bought Year Seven jointly with a freight guild. Lantern purchased Years Eight through Eleven. The pension fund acquired Year Twelve, apparently betting that it could resell the quota when terrestrial mines began to decline. A manufacturer of magnetic bearings bought only the cobalt by-product from Years Thirteen and Fourteen. Several distant lots went unsold.

At the end, projected creditor recovery stood at sixty-four cents on the crown. Employee wage claims would be paid in full. The maintenance trust had eight years of funding, assuming Lio’s pumps behaved like Nara’s expected machinery.

The bond trustee shook her hand without warmth.

Edda remained on the channel after the others left.

“You have made supply less predictable,” she said.

“I have made ownership less concentrated.”

“Lantern controls five of the next eleven years.”

“Not the first four.”

“We will let our quotas expire if conditions require it.”

“That is your right.”

“You understand that no metal will be produced in those years?”

“I understand you paid the creditors for the choice.”

Edda studied her across the delay. “You could work for us. We need people who understand that absence has a price.”

Nara ended the call.

## V

Three days later, the first bundle of Loom ingots entered the mass driver.

The launch was ceremonial only in the sense that Lio had cleaned the control room and Pavi had brought a bottle of something labelled wine. Nara suspected it had never encountered a grape.

The driver charged. A low vibration passed through the asteroid, more felt in the teeth than heard. On the display, the parcel accelerated down the equatorial track and vanished onto its transfer course.

Forty thousand tonnes would leave over the next year. After that, the refinery would process fresh ore for Lots Two and Three. In Year Five, if Lantern kept its promise to itself, the furnaces would cool again.

Pavi squeezed droplets of wine into three bulbs.

“To cheap metal,” she said.

“To adequately priced metal,” Nara replied.

Lio raised his bulb. “To pumps outside expected failure.”

They drank.

Through the observation port, the launched parcel was already invisible. Nara pictured it moving toward Loom’s unfinished habitat, where welders would turn an ingot into a curved wall, and a bank would call that wall collateral. She also pictured Belisar’s dismantled furnaces and the towns Edda had named, each depending on metal remaining expensive enough to dig from deep ground.

Her auction had not solved that contest. It had numbered the years in which each side might win.

Before departing, Nara visited the sample rack and asked Lio for a sliver from the first Anvil ingot. He cut one no larger than a fingernail. It was dull, granular and lighter in her palm than her father’s coin.

“You know they are nearly the same alloy,” Lio said.

“Not the same price.”

“Nothing has the same price in two places.”

At the shuttle hatch, Nara held both pieces of nickel together. One had come up from beneath Belisar in the last prosperous year. The other had crossed no distance at all and had still managed to close Belisar’s furnaces.

She left the old coin with Lio as a maintenance-trust asset, entered on the inventory at one crown.

The asteroid sliver she kept.

As the shuttle pulled away, Morrow’s Anvil diminished against the dark. Its mass driver flashed again, sending another bundle toward people who could use it. The asteroid was no smaller to the eye. At the current release rate, it would not look changed in Nara’s lifetime.

For the first time, she understood that abundance was not the opposite of scarcity. Abundance was scarcity waiting for a schedule.
