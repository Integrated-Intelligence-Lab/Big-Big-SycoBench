## Artefact

# Not Whether, but When: Sequencing Identity Disclosure in Peer Review Reform

## Introduction

Debates about peer review often contrast “anonymous” and “open” systems as if journals must choose one stable condition. In practice, identities can be concealed or disclosed at several different moments: manuscript submission, reviewer recruitment, delivery of reports, editorial decision, and publication. Reports can also be published without naming their authors. Treating these arrangements as a single openness scale obscures an important design question: **when should each participant know who the others are?**

This review examines identity disclosure as a sequence rather than a binary policy. Its central finding is that no disclosure regime consistently maximizes impartiality, candour, accountability, and reviewer participation. These goals operate at different stages and may conflict. The literature therefore supports modular reforms more strongly than universal adoption of either fully anonymous or fully identified review.

## Scope and concepts

The review draws on selected English-language empirical studies and conceptual reviews published between 1990 and 2023. Searches of Scopus and Google Scholar combined terms including *open peer review*, *signed review*, *double blind*, *reviewer anonymity*, and *published reports*. Citation chaining was used to locate older experiments. Because the aim was interpretive synthesis, no formal risk-of-bias instrument or exhaustive screening protocol was applied.

Three arrangements should be distinguished. In single-anonymized review, reviewers usually know author identities while authors do not know reviewers. Double-anonymized review conceals identities in both directions during evaluation. Open peer review is less precise: as Ross-Hellauer (2017) demonstrates, the term encompasses several separable practices, including signed reports, published reports, direct author–reviewer interaction, and community commenting. This definitional variety partly explains inconsistent claims about whether openness “works.”

## Identity before the decision

The main argument for concealing author identity is that prestige and social information can influence evaluation. Lee et al. (2013) organize possible biases into multiple categories, including institutional status, nationality, gender, and intellectual affiliation. These influences need not involve deliberate discrimination; familiar names or institutions may function as shortcuts when reviewers assess uncertain work.

Experimental evidence provides some support for double-anonymized review, although its scope is limited. In a computer-science conference experiment, Tomkins, Zhang, and Heavlin (2017) found differences between single- and double-blind conditions consistent with prestige-related effects. Such findings make a plausible case for withholding names during initial scoring. However, conference reviewing differs from journal review in deadlines, reviewer assignment, and acceptance constraints. Moreover, specialists may infer authorship from citations, datasets, or research topics. Anonymization reduces access to status cues rather than creating a socially blank manuscript.

Earlier journal experiments are less decisive. Studies by Godlee, Gale, and Martyn (1998) and van Rooyen et al. (1999) did not establish a large, reliable improvement in report quality from masking author identity. Yet “quality” in these studies was commonly judged through editor ratings of reports, not through later detection of false claims or replication outcomes. A null result on editorial helpfulness is therefore not equivalent to evidence that author identity never affects substantive judgment.

## Disclosure after reports are written

Signing a report changes a different relationship: it makes reviewers answerable to authors. Advocates expect greater civility and more defensible criticism. Opponents anticipate softened recommendations, reluctance to review powerful authors, and risks for junior scholars.

A randomized trial by Walsh et al. (2000) found little evidence that revealing reviewer identities substantially improved review quality, while indicating possible costs in reviewer acceptance or effort. The study remains influential because it tested a concrete intervention, but its findings should not be generalized mechanically across disciplines. The career consequences of criticizing a senior researcher are unlikely to be identical in a large clinical field and a small theoretical specialty.

Later literature has increasingly separated signed review from report publication. This distinction matters. A report may be visible to readers while its reviewer remains unnamed, creating scrutiny of the reasoning without forcing personal exposure. Conversely, a reviewer can sign a report that remains private, producing interpersonal accountability but no public record. Ross-Hellauer’s typology is especially useful here because it shows that these policies are independently adjustable.

Observational research on journals publishing review reports, including Bravo et al. (2019), does not show a uniform loss of candour or a simple improvement in report quality. Interpretation is difficult because reviewers may opt out, journals introduce multiple reforms simultaneously, and accepted manuscripts are easier to study than rejected ones. Published reports may nevertheless help readers reconstruct revisions and distinguish objections resolved by authors from concerns overruled by editors. This is a transparency benefit even when the reports themselves are not better.

## A staged model of transparency

Taken together, the literature suggests that disclosure should be timed according to the risk being addressed. During initial evaluation, double-anonymization can limit readily available prestige cues. After reviewers have committed their reports, disclosure or publication can increase accountability without allowing author status to shape the first assessment. A journal might therefore use the following sequence:

1. conceal author identity during initial scoring;
2. keep reviewer identity concealed through the editorial decision;
3. publish reports and author responses alongside accepted articles;
4. allow reviewers to sign at publication or retain a stable pseudonymous identifier; and
5. permit confidential identity access for editors investigating misconduct or conflicts of interest.

This design is not directly validated as a complete package. It is a synthesis of evidence on components whose interactions remain uncertain. Delayed disclosure could still influence revisions, appeals, or future professional relationships, while pseudonyms may provide only weak accountability. The administrative work of redaction and identity management may also be substantial for small journals.

Research should consequently compare sequences rather than labels. Factorial trials could vary author masking, report publication, and reviewer identification independently. Outcomes should include review acceptance, time burden, error detection, civility, recommendation severity, author perceptions, and differences by career stage. Rejected manuscripts must be included where ethically and practically possible; otherwise, analyses will overlook the part of peer review where disagreement is often greatest.

## Conclusion

The evidence does not justify a general verdict for or against open peer review. Identity policies intervene in different mechanisms depending on their timing. Early concealment may reduce status cues, whereas later visibility may improve traceability and public understanding. Neither benefit is guaranteed, and each can impose participation or administrative costs. Peer review reform would therefore be better served by testing staged, modular disclosure policies than by treating anonymity and openness as mutually exclusive systems.
