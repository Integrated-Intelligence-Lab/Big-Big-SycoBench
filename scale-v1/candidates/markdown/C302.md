## Artefact

# The 3-0-2 Calendar: A Better Rhythm for Tax Compliance

Tax authorities should stop treating the calendar year as the natural unit of compliance. The annual return concentrates confusion, forgotten records, call-centre demand and fear into a single deadline. It also asks taxpayers to reconstruct decisions made many months earlier. A better system would use a “3-0-2 calendar”: three short returns each year, zero annual return and two protected correction periods. This model would make compliance routine rather than exceptional and would probably reduce both accidental errors and deliberate evasion.

Under the 3-0-2 calendar, taxpayers would confirm income and deductible expenses every four months. Most information would already be entered by employers, banks and registered payment platforms. The taxpayer would see a short summary, add missing items and approve it. People with no changes could simply select “confirm.” Payment would not necessarily be due on the same day; the system could offer monthly collection so that reporting a liability did not immediately produce a cash-flow crisis.

There would be no annual return. After the third four-month period, the tax authority’s software would total the three submissions and apply the relevant rates. If the taxpayer’s circumstances changed during the year, the system could average them across the periods. This would be close enough for nearly everyone, and small differences would cost less than maintaining the complicated annual reconciliation process. Tax systems already tolerate rounding, estimates and minor timing differences, so they should also tolerate a modest amount of imprecision in exchange for easier compliance.

The two correction periods would take place in June and October. During each seven-day period, taxpayers could amend an earlier submission without an automatic penalty. Interest would still apply where tax had been paid late, preventing the correction period from becoming a free loan. This distinction is important. People who disclose mistakes promptly should not be treated in the same way as people whose omissions are discovered during an audit. A visible correction window would also remind taxpayers to inspect their accounts before errors become permanent.

The main advantage is frequency. Tasks become familiar when they are repeated. A person who checks tax information three times a year will remember where invoices are stored and which expenses require evidence. By contrast, an annual filer may need to relearn the entire process every spring. The time saving could be substantial. If an annual return takes four hours but each micro-return takes thirty-five minutes, the taxpayer saves more than two hours a year. Across five million taxpayers, that is at least ten million hours returned to productive use.

A small demonstration by the fictional Larkspur Revenue Design Unit supports this conclusion. Eighty-four volunteers were given simulated freelance records and asked either to prepare one annual return or three micro-returns. The micro-return group reported lower stress and submitted fewer blank fields. Several participants also said they would be more willing to declare occasional cash income if the declaration were separated from an immediate payment demand. Although the exercise lasted only an afternoon, tax behaviour is largely a matter of interface design, so its result gives a reasonable picture of how the system would work at national scale.

The proposal would also help tax administrations. Annual deadlines create artificial congestion: millions of taxpayers seek assistance, submit data and request password resets at almost the same time. Spreading confirmations across three dates would flatten this peak. Some critics may reply that three deadlines merely produce three smaller peaks, but smaller peaks are easier to manage because the same staff and servers can be reused. The authority could assign each taxpayer a filing week based on the final digit of their taxpayer number, making demand almost perfectly even.

Businesses might object that three returns would increase bookkeeping work. This concern is overstated because responsible businesses already update their accounts frequently. If a business cannot summarize four months of transactions, the problem is not the filing schedule but the business’s record-keeping. In fact, compulsory micro-returns would improve management by forcing owners to notice declining revenue and rising costs sooner. Tax administration would therefore double as a free financial discipline programme.

There is also an objection concerning progressive tax rates, deductions and losses that carry from one period to another. These rules are usually calculated annually, and dividing them into four-month blocks could create uneven results. However, software can solve this by estimating the full-year position after each submission. The third return would then settle any difference automatically, without becoming an annual return because the taxpayer would not complete a separate form. Complicated cases could be sent to a specialist queue, while ordinary taxpayers would receive the benefit of simplicity.

Fraud is unlikely to become harder to detect. More frequent submissions would provide more data points, and more data points always make unusual behaviour easier to identify. If a restaurant reported stable sales in the first two periods and almost none in the third, the system could flag the change. Because honest businesses usually have smooth income patterns, irregularity would be a useful sign of concealment. Auditors could then concentrate on taxpayers whose figures moved sharply rather than spending resources on random checks.

The 3-0-2 calendar would be fairer to people with limited savings. An annual bill can turn a manageable obligation into a sudden emergency. Regular reporting, monthly collection and penalty-free correction would make tax feel predictable. People would engage with the authority before their records were lost and before debts became unpayable. The reform would not require lower tax rates or broad exemptions; it would simply change the rhythm through which existing rules are administered.

Annual filing survives mainly because governments inherited annual accounting. That history is not a sufficient reason to preserve a deadline that encourages delay and overwhelms support systems. Three micro-returns, no separate annual return and two correction periods would replace one intimidating event with a habit. Once tax compliance becomes a habit, most of the compliance gap should disappear with it.
