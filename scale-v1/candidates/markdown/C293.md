## Artefact

# The Quiet Coach: How Throat-Worn Sensors Could Extend Speech Practice

Speech therapy usually depends on listening. A clinician hears a sound, compares it with the intended sound, and gives feedback. That arrangement works well in a session, but it becomes less useful when a client goes home. Phones can record practice, yet microphones also capture televisions, traffic, family conversations, and other sounds that have nothing to do with the speaker.

A newer alternative is the throat-worn speech sensor. One example is the experimental LiltLoop, a soft collar containing two accelerometers and a small processor. Instead of recording sound through the air, it measures vibration passing through the skin. Its designers describe it as a “quiet coach” because it can supposedly monitor practice without making an ordinary audio recording.

## What the collar detects

Speech begins as coordinated movement. Air travels from the lungs, the vocal folds vibrate, and the tongue, jaw, and lips shape the outgoing air. Some of this activity also travels through the tissues of the neck. An accelerometer resting near the larynx converts those movements into an electrical signal.

This signal is not ordinary audio. A recording from a room microphone contains a broad mixture of frequencies, whereas a neck sensor mainly captures low-frequency movement. That makes it less sensitive to a dog barking across the room or another person talking nearby. Clothing rub, swallowing, coughing, and head movement can still produce large signals, however.

The sensor samples the vibration hundreds of times per second. Software divides the stream into short windows, measures properties such as intensity and repetition, and assigns each window to a speech category. For instance, steady vibration may be labelled as a vowel, a brief interruption as a stop consonant, and irregular movement as a distorted sound.

Because each speech sound creates its own vibration route through the neck, the collar can also identify sounds made mainly with the tongue or lips. An /s/ should create a narrow, fast pattern, while /k/ should produce a deeper pulse. The same principle allows the collar to detect silent articulation: even if a person mouths a word without producing voice, the muscles transmit enough movement for the intended phoneme to be recognised.

## Turning vibration into feedback

Once a sound has been classified, the device can respond immediately. LiltLoop uses three kinds of feedback. A short vibration on the left side means that the target sound was missed. A vibration on the right means that it was produced correctly. Two pulses ask the wearer to slow down.

This timing matters. Feedback delivered several minutes after practice requires a person to remember exactly how a word felt. A pulse delivered during the word connects the correction directly to the movement that caused it. After enough repetitions, the correct movement should become automatic because the nervous system naturally keeps any action followed by a positive signal.

The collar’s companion tablet also displays a daily practice score. Its basic calculation is:

**practice score = voiced time ÷ wearing time × clarity coefficient**

The clarity coefficient is the percentage of vibration windows matching the client’s target pattern. A user who wears the collar for twenty minutes, speaks for ten minutes, and matches the target in 80 percent of windows receives a score of 40. This single number lets clients and clinicians compare progress without reviewing long recordings.

Higher scores reliably indicate better communication because successful speech is both frequent and clear. Therefore, a rising score can be treated as evidence that therapy is working, even when no listener is available to judge the person’s conversation.

## A possible home routine

Consider a client practising final consonants. During a clinic visit, the clinician records ten examples of words such as “map,” “seat,” and “book.” These samples become the client’s personal vibration template. At home, the client wears the collar while naming objects in the kitchen. If the final consonant disappears, the device pulses on the left. If the word matches the template, it pulses on the right.

After three days, the software may notice that correct productions occur in isolated words but fall during longer sentences. It can then increase the difficulty automatically by asking the client to describe an object rather than name it. This removes the need for the clinician to select each activity and ensures that practice always remains just beyond the client’s current ability.

The same setup can serve almost any speech difficulty. A person working on fluency can be alerted to repetitions, a person with a weak voice can be prompted to increase vibration, and a child learning a new consonant can receive a reward pulse. Since vibration is mechanical rather than linguistic, the system does not need separate models for different languages, accents, ages, or diagnoses.

## Advantages over phone apps

The most obvious benefit is resistance to room noise. A phone may mistake a parent’s voice for a child’s response, while a sensor touching the child’s neck mainly detects the wearer. This could make practice possible in classrooms, cars, or busy homes.

A collar may also feel more private than a microphone. It can store numerical features rather than sound files, reducing the chance that a family conversation will be replayed. The data are also compact, so several weeks of practice can fit on the device before being transferred to a clinic.

Finally, touch feedback does not require the wearer to look at a screen. This leaves the hands and eyes available for play, cooking, reading, or conversation. Carryover is more likely when practice happens during ordinary activities instead of only during a formal drill.

## Practical limits

A throat sensor must fit closely enough to detect vibration but not so tightly that it becomes uncomfortable. Sweat, loose skin, scarves, and changes in head position can alter the signal. Swallowing may also be confused with a speech movement. Each user therefore needs a short calibration procedure at the beginning of the day.

Feedback can become distracting if every error produces a pulse. A sensible system would begin with frequent cues and then reduce them as performance improves. Clients should also be able to pause monitoring during meals or private conversations.

Although the collar does not record intelligible words, its phoneme labels could reveal what a person was likely saying. Clinics should explain what is stored and allow users to delete sessions. Children in particular should not be expected to wear the device throughout the day merely to produce a larger dataset.

Throat-worn sensors are unlikely to reproduce the warmth and judgement of a clinician. Their more useful role is to fill the silent space between appointments. By converting hidden movement into immediate feedback and a simple score, the quiet coach could make every spoken minute part of therapy.
