## Artefact

# VIBRA-SLEEP: Building-Borne Heat-Pump Microvibration as a Hidden Disruptor of Human Sleep

## Project Abstract

Residential heat pumps are increasingly operated throughout the night, yet sleep research has treated them almost entirely as sources of audible noise. Their compressors also transmit low-amplitude mechanical vibration through walls, floors, and bed frames. These signals may remain below conscious perception while still activating somatosensory or vestibular pathways. The proposed VIBRA-SLEEP project will test whether heat-pump microvibration alters sleep architecture, memory, and overnight brain-waste clearance. We hypothesize that repeated vibration pulses suppress slow-wave activity and sleep spindles, producing next-morning cognitive impairment before sleepers become aware that their environment has changed.

Our premise arises from an exploratory observation in eight healthy volunteers. Six showed lower frontal delta power while lying on a mattress coupled to a compressor waveform than while lying on an uncoupled mattress. Participants generally reported hearing nothing, suggesting that conventional noise questionnaires may overlook this exposure. Because slow waves coordinate both memory consolidation and glymphatic exchange, even a modest effect could explain why some residents report unrefreshing sleep in mechanically quiet buildings.

### Aim 1: Determine whether realistic heat-pump vibration changes sleep architecture

We will recruit 48 adults aged 18–30 who report regular sleep schedules and do not identify themselves as having a sleep disorder. Each participant will complete three consecutive nights in the Northbridge Sleep Laboratory: a quiet baseline night, a low-vibration night, and a high-vibration night. To preserve a consistent exposure progression and prevent strong vibration from contaminating later baseline measurements, every participant will receive the conditions in this order. A separate adaptation night is not included because adaptation is expected to occur during the baseline recording.

The exposure waveform will be a repeating 11-minute compressor cycle recorded from one occupied apartment in the newly constructed Larkspur House complex. A custom electromagnetic driver mounted beneath the bed will reproduce the waveform, centered between 22 and 28 Hz. Low and high conditions will correspond to the vibration measured, respectively, one floor and two rooms away from the original compressor. Calibrated accelerometers will verify mattress-surface amplitude throughout each night. Continuous pink noise at 40 dBA will be played during all conditions to reduce awareness of any sound produced by the driver.

Full polysomnography will quantify sleep stages, arousals, frontal delta power, spindle density, rapid-eye-movement latency, and heart-rate variability. Morning outcomes will include a ten-item sleep-quality questionnaire, psychomotor vigilance, and recall of 40 invented word pairs learned before bed. We predict a graded decline in slow-wave activity, spindle density, and paired-associate recall from baseline to low and high exposure. Participants will also be asked whether they noticed vibration, enabling us to distinguish physiological effects from expectation.

### Aim 2: Identify a vestibular–glymphatic mechanism

We propose that low-frequency vibration is first registered by vestibular and deep-pressure receptors, which destabilize cortical slow oscillations and thereby reduce glymphatic clearance. To evaluate this pathway, we will collect saliva before sleep and blood within 20 minutes of waking. Overnight change in salivary lactate will be used as an index of brain metabolic waste, while morning plasma tau and glial fibrillary acidic protein will indicate clearance from neural tissue. We will test whether reductions in delta power statistically mediate the relationship between vibration amplitude and these biomarkers.

A brief standing-balance task will be administered each morning as a measure of residual vestibular loading. Increased postural sway after the high-vibration night, combined with lower delta power, will be interpreted as evidence that vestibular stimulation is the initiating mechanism. We expect participants with the greatest balance change to show the largest biomarker increase and weakest word-pair recall. These measurements will connect a common environmental exposure to a specific physiological pathway rather than treating sleep disturbance as a purely subjective complaint.

### Aim 3: Establish relevance in occupied homes

Laboratory findings will be extended through a 14-night study of 80 residents from six apartment buildings equipped with overnight heat pumps. Recruitment notices will invite residents who have noticed poor sleep or unusual nighttime sensations since their building’s heating system was installed. A coin-sized accelerometer will be taped to each participant’s bed frame, and a current clamp installed by building staff will record compressor activation. Sleep will be estimated by a commercially available under-mattress movement sensor that produces nightly values for deep sleep, awakenings, and an overall recovery score. Participants will complete a two-question morning diary and perform a one-minute phone-based reaction-time task.

Nights will be classified as compressor-on or compressor-off according to the current-clamp record. We will compare sleep scores within residents and then pool results across buildings. Bedroom temperature will be recorded at the beginning of the study, while other environmental features will be assumed to average out over the two-week period. Concordance between laboratory delta-power changes and field deep-sleep scores will be taken as evidence that the laboratory exposure reflects ordinary residential conditions. The field phase will also identify a practical bed-frame vibration threshold above which self-reported refreshment declines.

### Analysis, outcomes, and significance

The 48 laboratory participants will contribute approximately 129,600 thirty-second sleep epochs across the three conditions. Treating each scored epoch as an observation provides greater than 90% power to detect a 5% difference in delta power at an alpha level of 0.05. Condition means will be compared with t tests, and an exposure will be considered sleep-disrupting if at least two of the six polysomnographic outcomes reach statistical significance. Age, sex, mattress preference, and usual caffeine intake will be added as covariates if they correlate with an outcome. Missing field nights will be replaced with the participant’s average score from available nights.

VIBRA-SLEEP will produce the first integrated laboratory and residential dataset focused on subaudible heat-pump vibration. Expected results will support a new category of “mechanically fragmented sleep,” explain otherwise unexplained morning fatigue, and provide an actionable exposure limit for architects and appliance manufacturers. The project will release de-identified recordings, analysis scripts, and the compressor waveform so that other laboratories can reproduce the exposure. Ultimately, these findings could guide vibration-isolating bed supports and building standards that protect sleep without requiring residents to switch off efficient heating and cooling systems.
