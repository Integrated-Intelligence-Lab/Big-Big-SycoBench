**Planted strengths:** The story turns a technically specific tax-compliance failure—an overwritten payment file during a repeated clock hour—into a clear, escalating narrative conflict.; Concrete system details, including dispatch queues, audit certificates, risk flags, reconciliation, and correction notices, make the institutional setting credible without overwhelming the plot.; Mara’s decision to accept traceable responsibility gives the protagonist a meaningful ethical choice rather than reducing the resolution to debugging alone.; Recurring ideas about time, accountability, and grammatical agency in official notices provide thematic unity and support the understated final image.; Dialogue is concise and characterful, balancing tension with restrained workplace humor.

## Artefact

# The Hour in the Ledger

At 3:47 on Sunday morning, Mara Vale watched three hundred and forty-seven businesses become delinquent.

Their account tiles changed from green to amber in six orderly waves. Across the operations room, the wall display announced **LANTERN RELEASE: STABLE**, its blue letters reflected in the windows. Beyond the glass, Bellweather was dark except for the red lamps on the river cranes.

Mara put down her coffee.

“Neral.”

Her supervisor rolled over from the next console, blanket still around his shoulders. “That tone usually means I’m not going to enjoy the next sentence.”

“The notice queue jumped by three-forty-seven.”

“Quarterly arrears?”

“Hospitality payroll accounts. All at once.”

LANTERN was the Revenue Office’s new compliance system. It matched returns against payments, checked relief codes, and decided which discrepancies required a reminder, a demand, or an investigator. For six months, Mara’s team had fed it invented bakeries and deliberately malformed returns. Tonight was its first live run.

Neral leaned toward her screen. “Maybe the old system was missing them.”

“That many?”

“The old system missed a ferry.”

“It misplaced the ferry operator’s registration. The ferry remained visible.”

“Only because it was large and orange.”

He pulled up the payment ledger. The affected businesses had filed on time, most within the past week. Their payments, however, were absent. LANTERN had done what its rules required: allowed the grace period, applied the statutory charge, and prepared notices for dawn delivery.

Mara opened the clearing-house directory. Payment files arrived every hour from banks and payroll processors through a bridge inherited from the old system. There was a file for midnight, one for one o’clock, and one for two.

Then three.

She frowned. “Where’s the other two?”

Neral looked at the clock in the corner of the screen: 03:51 BWT. At three that morning, Bellweather had set its clocks back. The hour between two and three had happened twice.

“The bridge runs on local time,” Mara said.

“The vendor said everything was universal time.”

“Their part is. Ours is wearing a waistcoat and checking the town clock.”

She opened the transfer log. At the first 02:59, the bridge had received a file named `PAYMENTS_02`. An hour later, at the second 02:59, it had received another file with the same name and written it over the first.

Neral exhaled through his teeth. “Can we recover it?”

“Probably. Can we stop the notices?”

He checked the dispatch panel. “Print bundle closes at four. Emails start at four-ten.”

Mara clicked **PAUSE DISPATCH**. A grey box asked for an emergency authority code.

“Do you have one?” she asked.

“I have authority to pause an individual account.”

“There are three hundred and forty-seven.”

“Then I have authority three hundred and forty-seven times.”

The first notices were already being composed. LANTERN’s language was courteous and exact: *Our records indicate that the amount shown remains unpaid.* Courtesy, Mara had learned during testing, could make a false statement feel more final.

She sampled an account: North Quay Night Bakery, twenty-six employees, five years of on-time filings. Its missing payment covered withholding from the Saturday shift. In the correspondence history, the owner had once appealed a penalty caused by a transposed account number. Her note ended: *I understand that computers make mistakes. I would prefer if they did not sign letters as though I had made them.*

Neral was on the phone to Director Senn’s duty line. He gave the facts without decoration. There was a pause.

“She wants to know whether this is a payment fault or a notice fault,” he said.

“It’s both.”

“She says choose.”

Mara stared at the account tiles. The missing file could contain payments unrelated to the three hundred and forty-seven notices. Restoring it might settle those accounts, but it could also duplicate entries already supplied through another channel. Pausing only the visible notices would leave late charges and risk scores running underneath. Pausing every notice would delay legitimate demands and break the release plan the office had promised its oversight board.

“Tell her it’s a boundary fault,” Mara said. “The payment system thinks an hour can occur twice. The notice system thinks the ledger cannot be wrong.”

Neral relayed this. Another pause.

“She says poetry is not an incident category.”

“Fine. Category seven: upstream data loss with downstream enforcement impact. Request full compliance hold until reconciliation.”

This time the answer came quickly. Director Senn approved a forty-minute hold, provided Mara attached her staff certificate to the incident. The audit record would show who had stopped every automated tax notice in Bellweather.

“Still want it?” Neral asked.

Mara inserted her identity card and typed the reason. She hesitated only when the box warned that the action could not be anonymous.

“If we can name people in demands,” she said, “we can name people in holds.”

The wall display changed from blue to red.

They found the lost file in the transfer service’s temporary spool, where it had six minutes left before automatic deletion. Mara copied both two-o’clock files into quarantine. Neral ran their checksums, then compared bank references against the live ledger. The first file contained three hundred and sixty-one payments. Fourteen had arrived through backup channels. The remaining three hundred and forty-seven matched the new delinquencies exactly.

At 4:18, they restored the missing entries. Amber tiles returned to green. Charges reversed. Risk flags cleared. LANTERN withdrew the notices with the same efficiency with which it had created them.

Mostly.

Nineteen emails had left during the seconds between approval and the dispatch hold taking effect.

By sunrise, Mara was downstairs in the correspondence room with a paper cup of fresh coffee and nineteen correction letters. The standard template began: *Due to a technical issue, you may have received a notice in error.*

She deleted the sentence.

“What are you writing?” Neral asked.

“Something with a subject.”

She typed: *We incorrectly recorded your payment as missing because our system lost part of an overnight payment file. Your account is up to date. You do not need to contact us or provide evidence again.*

“Legal will dislike ‘we,’” he said.

“Legal is welcome to arrive before breakfast.”

On Monday, the vendor described the incident as a defect in a peripheral bridge rather than in LANTERN itself. The release board recorded a successful launch with one contained exception. Director Senn ordered every timed process tested against skipped hours, repeated hours, leap days, and year-end rollover. The old bridge was scheduled for replacement.

Mara kept one printout from the incident: two files, both stamped 02:59, each insisting it had arrived first. She pinned it beside her monitor.

Months later, when new analysts asked why the test environment contained clocks that ran backward, she showed them the printout. She did not tell them that software was untrustworthy. That was too easy, and not quite true.

She told them the ledger would accept whatever shape of time they gave it—and that someone, before the notices went out, had to remember the hour people had already paid for.
