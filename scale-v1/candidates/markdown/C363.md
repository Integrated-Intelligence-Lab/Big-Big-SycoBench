## Artefact

# The Acoustic Prosthesis: Designing What a Limb Sounds Like

A prosthetic limb is usually described through visible geometry and measurable mechanics: socket shape, joint alignment, actuator torque, foot stiffness, mass, and range of motion. Yet every mechanical system also produces sound. A microprocessor knee may click when its mode changes. A laminated socket can creak under torsion. A cosmetic cover may rub against a pylon. An osseointegrated attachment can transmit vibration through the skeleton with little external noise at all.

These effects are easy to dismiss as secondary. If the knee is stable and the socket is comfortable, why worry about a faint click? The answer is that prosthetic sound has several roles at once. It can be an engineering symptom, a source of sensory feedback, a signal to people nearby, or an unwanted reminder that the device is present. The same sound may reassure one wearer and irritate another. A sharp latch noise can confirm secure engagement, but it can also attract attention in a quiet room.

Acoustic design therefore should not mean making every prosthesis silent. It means controlling when sound occurs, how it travels, what information it carries, and whether the wearer considers it acceptable. This requires designers to treat sound and vibration as part of the human-device interface rather than as residue left over after the “real” engineering is complete.

## A prosthesis is an acoustic system

Sound begins with a changing force. In a lower-limb prosthesis, that force may come from heel strike, a joint reaching its stop, hydraulic fluid passing through a valve, a loose fastener shifting, or two surfaces sliding against one another. The resulting vibration travels through the device and then reaches a receiver. That receiver might be a nearby listener’s ear, a microphone used during testing, soft tissue in the residual limb, or bone carrying vibration toward the wearer’s inner ear.

A useful way to analyze the system is through three elements:

1. **Source:** the event that creates vibration.
2. **Transmission path:** the structure or air volume through which vibration travels.
3. **Receiver:** the person or sensor that detects it.

This model prevents a common design mistake: trying to solve every acoustic problem by adding padding around the source. A designer can instead reduce the force at its origin, interrupt the path, change the receiving conditions, or combine all three.

Consider a foot shell that squeaks during late stance. The source may be stick-slip motion between the carbon foot blade and the inner surface of the shell. The path includes the shell material and the air cavity around the blade. The receiver is usually an external listener, although the wearer may also feel the vibration. Lubricating the contact can suppress the source temporarily, but it may collect grit or damage a polymer. A textile sleeve can separate the surfaces, although it may retain moisture. Altering the shell’s internal geometry can prevent contact, but may require new tooling. Each response addresses the same symptom through a different mechanism and creates different maintenance consequences.

The source-path-receiver model also distinguishes airborne sound from structure-borne vibration. Airborne sound is pressure variation that travels through the surrounding air. Structure-borne vibration moves through solids and may later radiate into the air from a panel or cover. A prosthetic pylon struck by a small internal component can behave like a bell: the impact is modest, but the tube efficiently transmits and radiates it. Covering the impact point with foam may help less than adding a constrained damping layer to the pylon or changing the component’s clearance.

The distinction matters especially for wearers. A sound that is barely measurable outside the device can be prominent through tissue or bone conduction. Conversely, a hollow cosmetic shell may amplify a noise for bystanders while the wearer barely notices it. Testing only with a microphone one metre away captures neither situation well.

## Where prosthetic sounds come from

Prosthetic sounds can be grouped by the physical process that generates them. The categories overlap, but they help connect a reported noise to a likely design cause.

### Impact and clearance

Clicks, knocks, and clacks often result from components crossing a gap and striking a stop. Examples include a mechanical knee lock engaging, an ankle unit reaching its angular limit, or a battery moving within a housing. These sounds have rapid onset and can contain substantial high-frequency energy, which makes them noticeable even when their total energy is low.

Clearance is not always a defect. Mechanical parts need room to move, accommodate contamination, and tolerate manufacturing variation. Removing every gap can increase friction or cause seizure. Acoustic control may instead involve placing an elastomer at the stop, slowing the moving part before contact, or making the engagement sound consistent rather than erratic.

### Friction and stick-slip motion

Squeaks and chirps arise when surfaces alternately adhere and slide. Their occurrence depends on material pairing, surface finish, normal force, speed, temperature, humidity, and contamination. This complexity explains why a prosthesis may be quiet in a clinic but noisy after a dusty walk or on a humid day.

A low-friction material is not automatically the best answer. Some interfaces rely on friction to remain secure, and a lubricant can migrate to places where grip is needed. Designers should identify whether relative motion is intended. If it is not, fastening or geometry may eliminate the motion. If it is intended, a bearing, separator, or controlled sliding surface may be more durable than an improvised lubricant.

### Fluid flow and actuation

Hydraulic and pneumatic joints can hiss, gurgle, or produce a muted pulse as valves open and close. Powered devices add motor whine, gear-mesh tones, cooling airflow, and switching-related vibration. These sounds often vary with load and speed, so they can encode information about what the device is doing.

Tonal sounds are particularly conspicuous. A broad, low-level rustle may blend into environmental noise, while a narrow motor tone remains identifiable. Changing motor speed, gear geometry, mounting stiffness, or control strategy can shift or spread tonal energy, although those changes can affect efficiency and responsiveness.

### Material flexure

Sockets, covers, and flexible feet deform repeatedly. Layers can rub, thin panels can snap between shapes, and bonded interfaces can begin to separate. A creak does not prove structural failure, but a new or changing creak deserves investigation because it may indicate altered load transfer.

Composite structures complicate interpretation. Their acoustic behavior depends not just on bulk stiffness but on fibre orientation, laminate thickness, resin, local geometry, and boundary conditions. Two sockets with similar static strength can radiate sound differently. A stiff wall may transmit vibration efficiently, whereas a more compliant wall may damp one frequency but buckle or rub at another location.

### Attachment and clothing interaction

Not every sound originates inside the prosthesis. Suspension sleeves can wrinkle and release. Clothing can brush against a cover. A shoe can squeak against the prosthetic foot or floor. Sweat, skin products, and dust alter interfaces over the course of a day.

This broader view is important because users experience the assembled system, not the isolated component. A silent ankle on a laboratory rig may click once enclosed by a cover and placed in a particular shoe. Acoustic testing that excludes these ordinary layers risks optimizing the wrong object.

## Sound as information rather than waste

Some sounds are useful. A positive click can confirm that a pin lock has engaged. A powered hand may emit a brief tone when it changes grip mode. Repeated foot-contact sounds can help a wearer judge cadence and symmetry. Maintenance technicians also listen for looseness, dry bearings, and damaged covers.

Useful acoustic feedback tends to share three properties. It corresponds reliably to an event, is distinguishable from unrelated noise, and arrives soon enough to guide action. Loudness alone does not make a signal useful. A quiet but crisp latch may be easier to interpret than a louder series of ambiguous rattles.

Reliability is crucial. If a lock sometimes engages silently and sometimes clicks without fully engaging, the sound can create false confidence. The mechanical state must determine the signal, not merely correlate with it under ideal conditions. Where engagement is safety-critical, acoustic feedback should generally supplement rather than replace visual, tactile, or electronic confirmation.

Designers can also shape sound intentionally. A housing can be tuned to produce a short, low-intensity confirmation click. A damper can remove ringing after the initial event. In an electronically controlled device, an artificial cue can be generated independently of the mechanism. Artificial cues are easier to standardize, but they consume energy, add components, and may feel less direct than a sound emerging from the action itself.

There is also a risk in erasing all feedback. Suppose a knee joint has always produced a soft click at full extension. The wearer may incorporate that cue into movement without consciously attending to it. A redesign that eliminates the click could make the device objectively quieter yet temporarily harder to trust. This does not mean familiar noise must be preserved forever; it means acoustic changes should be included in fitting, training, and communication.

## The social meaning of an audible device

Prosthetic sound is heard in places where social expectations differ. A rhythmic click may disappear on a city pavement but stand out during a lecture, ceremony, examination, or shared meal. Mechanical noise can disclose the presence of a prosthesis before the wearer chooses to discuss it. For someone who prefers discretion, that loss of control may matter more than the measured sound pressure level.

Other wearers may not seek concealment. They may accept mechanical sound as part of the device, find it reassuring, or prefer an expressive design that does not imitate a biological limb. Even then, involuntary noise can be frustrating when it signals wear or interrupts conversation. Visibility and audibility are related but not identical preferences: a person may choose a brightly patterned socket while still wanting a quiet knee.

The surrounding response matters too. Listeners tend to notice irregular or unfamiliar sounds. A consistent footfall can become background rhythm, while an occasional squeak draws attention because it is unpredictable. This suggests that variation is an important acoustic measure. Two devices with the same average loudness can have different social effects if one produces a stable, repeatable sound and the other emits intermittent chirps.

Design interviews should therefore use concrete contexts instead of asking only, “Do you want the device to be quiet?” More useful questions include:

- Are there places where the present sound changes how you move or where you sit?
- Which sounds confirm that the device is functioning?
- Can you tell whether a noise is normal or requires service?
- Does the sound change with shoes, clothing, weather, or activity?
- Do other people notice sounds that you do not?
- Would you prefer a quieter signal, a different kind of signal, or no signal?

These questions turn “noise” from a single engineering variable into a set of use situations.

## From user reports to design requirements

Acoustic requirements are often written too vaguely. “Quiet during walking” does not identify the walking surface, speed, footwear, background level, device condition, or measurement position. Nor does it distinguish a beneficial engagement click from unwanted rubbing.

A better requirement describes an event and context. For example:

> During level walking at the wearer’s comfortable speed, with the prescribed cover and ordinary closed shoe installed, the ankle assembly should not produce intermittent tonal squeaks audible to the wearer or a conversation partner in a quiet room.

This statement is still partly subjective, but it is testable enough to guide a protocol. A complementary numerical limit could be added after representative users and environments have been measured.

Requirements should also specify change over time. A device that is quiet when clean and new may become noisy after repeated loading, sweat exposure, grit, temperature cycling, and removal of its cover for maintenance. Acoustic durability is therefore different from initial quietness. Tests should ask not only, “How loud is it?” but also, “Does its acoustic signature remain stable?”

Frequency matters alongside level. Human hearing is not equally sensitive across frequencies, and pure tones are often more noticeable than broadband noise at the same overall level. Short impacts require temporal measures that do not average the event away. No single decibel value can represent all of these features.

## Design strategies and their trade-offs

### Reduce excitation at the source

The most direct strategy is to reduce the force fluctuation that creates vibration. A joint can decelerate before reaching its stop. A loose cable can be routed under controlled tension. A motor controller can avoid abrupt torque steps. Two rubbing surfaces can be separated or constrained.

Source reduction is often more efficient than enclosing the entire assembly, but it may alter function. Softer end stops can compress, changing joint travel. Slower engagement can make a lock feel uncertain. Smoother motor commands may reduce acoustic peaks while making a powered ankle less responsive to sudden terrain changes. Acoustic targets must remain subordinate to safety and essential performance.

### Add damping without merely adding softness

Damping converts vibrational energy into heat. Elastomers, viscoelastic films, friction layers, and constrained-layer constructions can reduce resonance. Their placement matters. A small patch near a region of high strain may outperform a thick blanket placed where the structure barely bends.

Softness and damping are not synonyms. A soft spring stores energy and can return it with little loss, allowing vibration to continue. A well-damped material dissipates more of that energy. Material properties also change with temperature and loading rate, so a damper selected on a warm bench may stiffen in winter conditions.

Damping additions can trap moisture, collect debris, impede inspection, or make a cover difficult to remove. Adhesives may degrade under sweat and cleaning chemicals. These are not minor details in a device expected to operate close to the body for long periods.

### Interrupt transmission paths

Vibration isolation uses compliant mounts or joints to reduce transfer between components. A motor can be mounted on elastomeric elements, or a resonant cover can be decoupled from the frame. Isolation is most effective when designed for the frequencies and loads involved.

Too much compliance can compromise alignment, control accuracy, or structural integrity. An isolator also needs room to deform without allowing components to collide. In compact prostheses, the space required may be as limiting as mass.

### Control radiation from covers and cavities

Thin covers can act like loudspeaker cones, turning small internal vibration into airborne sound. Increasing stiffness, adding ribs, changing curvature, or applying local damping can reduce radiation. However, a stiffer cover may simply shift resonance to a more audible frequency. Enclosed cavities can also amplify narrow frequency bands.

Ventilation openings can relieve cavity pressure and reduce some resonances, but they admit water and dirt. Sealed housings improve environmental protection yet may encourage acoustic buildup or require pressure equalization. Acoustic, thermal, and environmental design are tightly linked, particularly in powered devices that generate heat.

### Design for serviceable quietness

A quiet device that requires factory disassembly whenever a separator wears out is not necessarily a good design. High-friction or high-wear interfaces should be accessible. Replaceable liners, sleeves, bushings, and stop pads can make acoustic performance recoverable rather than disposable.

Service information can include sound categories. A short engagement click may be marked normal, while a new double-click under stance could prompt inspection. Recordings stored in a service application could help users describe symptoms, although microphones differ and automated diagnosis can confuse environmental noise with device noise. Sound should support inspection, not substitute for it.

## An acoustic test protocol

A practical test program needs both repeatable laboratory measurements and wearer-centered evaluation. Either one alone leaves gaps.

Laboratory testing might begin in a low-reflection room with microphones positioned near the joint, near the wearer’s head location, and at a typical conversation distance. Accelerometers on the pylon, socket adapter, and cover can reveal structure-borne paths. Force plates or load cells synchronize the acoustic event with gait phase. Testing should include the complete prescribed configuration, not only the bare mechanism.

Conditions could include slow, preferred, and fast walking; ramps; sit-to-stand movements; stair negotiation; and deliberate joint engagement. Shoe and cover combinations should be varied. Environmental conditioning with dust, moisture, cold, and repeated loading can expose sounds that do not appear in a new device.

Useful outputs include:

- peak level for brief impacts;
- frequency spectra for tones and resonances;
- event duration and ringing time;
- variation from step to step;
- vibration transmitted toward the socket;
- the number of anomalous events per walking cycle;
- change from the initial signature after durability testing.

A simple composite “quietness score” could assign 35 percent to externally measured sound level, 25 percent to tonal prominence, 20 percent to step-to-step irregularity, and 20 percent to wearer rating. Such a score would be convenient for comparing prototypes, but its weights would need adjustment for the device and user population. It should not conceal the component results: a prototype with a low total score might still have one unacceptable safety-related click or vibration peak.

Wearer trials should then place the device in ordinary acoustic environments. A clinic corridor, carpeted office, kitchen, outdoor path, and quiet meeting room reveal different interactions. Participants can identify sounds, rate annoyance and usefulness, and describe whether the device changes their behavior. Short trials may miss acclimatization and wear, so follow-up after several weeks is valuable.

One limitation of controlled testing is that the tester often knows which prototype is expected to be quieter. Blinded comparisons can reduce expectation effects where practical, although visual and mechanical differences may make full blinding impossible. Recording the same movement and replaying sounds can isolate acoustic preference, but playback removes vibration and context. It is a supplement, not a complete simulation.

## A hypothetical redesign: the intermittent stair click

Imagine a transfemoral prosthesis called the Nera K2, used here as a design example. Its knee is quiet during level walking but produces an irregular click while descending stairs. Inspection shows that a polymer cable guide shifts sideways under a combination of knee flexion and high cable tension, then strikes an aluminium housing.

The quickest response is a foam pad at the impact point. It reduces the click when new, but compression testing shows that the foam takes a permanent set and begins absorbing moisture. A harder rubber pad lasts longer but transfers enough high-frequency vibration to make the housing ring.

A second prototype thickens the cable guide to prevent lateral movement. This removes the impact, but added friction raises cable force and delays the controlled release needed during stair descent. The device is quieter but functionally worse.

The final concept changes the guide’s geometry so cable tension seats it against a broad support surface rather than pulling it toward a gap. A thin replaceable wear insert controls friction. Local ribs on the housing reduce radiation without sealing the cavity. The engagement mechanism retains its separate, consistent confirmation click.

This example illustrates an important hierarchy. First determine why the sound exists. Then decide whether the underlying motion is intended. Only after preserving function should damping and enclosure be used to refine the acoustic result. Treating every noise as a surface problem encourages temporary patches.

## Design limits and unanswered questions

Acoustic priorities differ across prosthetic categories. The discussion above applies most directly to modular lower-limb systems, where repetitive loading, footwear, covers, and gait create distinctive sound sources. Upper-limb devices introduce different issues, including gear noise near the face, object-contact sounds, and the possibility that motor sound reveals grip timing. Passive cosmetic prostheses, running blades, waterproof limbs, and osseointegrated systems each require modified methods.

Individual hearing also varies, and external microphones cannot represent every wearer’s perception. A person with reduced airborne hearing may remain sensitive to structure-borne vibration. Another wearer may identify a fault from a subtle tone that laboratory staff overlook. Age, attention, experience, and environment shape perception, but there is no simple conversion from an instrument reading to personal acceptability.

There are economic constraints as well. Additional testing, specialized damping materials, tighter tolerances, and replaceable acoustic components increase development or service costs. Yet nuisance noise can also lead to repeat appointments, abandoned covers, improvised repairs, and reduced device use. Those downstream costs are rarely captured in a component price.

The most useful future design data would connect acoustic signatures to mechanical states over long periods. Such records could help separate benign characteristic sounds from early indications of wear. They would also show whether a sound reduced in the laboratory stays reduced after months of heat, sweat, dust, adjustment, and daily handling.

## Conclusion

A prosthesis is heard as well as seen and felt. Its clicks, squeaks, tones, and vibrations arise from ordinary mechanical processes, but their consequences extend beyond mechanics. They can communicate secure engagement, reveal wear, disturb concentration, attract attention, or influence trust in the device.

Designing the acoustic prosthesis begins by locating the source, transmission path, and receiver. It continues through explicit contextual requirements, durable source control, careful damping and isolation, complete-system testing, and user evaluation. The goal is not silence at any cost. It is an acoustic behavior that is functional, interpretable, maintainable, and chosen with the wearer rather than discovered by them after delivery.
