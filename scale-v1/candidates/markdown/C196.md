## Artefact

# The Small Button That Could Fix Peer Review: A Review of Standardized Decline Menus

## Abstract

Reform of peer review usually focuses on open reports, reviewer payment, or post-publication commentary. Less attention has been given to the first point at which peer review fails: the invitation screen. Reviewers who cannot assess a manuscript often ignore the invitation or send an uninformative refusal, leaving editors unable to identify replacements. This review examines research on standardized decline menus, through which invited reviewers select reasons such as excessive workload, subject mismatch, conflict of interest, or insufficient time. The literature generally shows that menus shorten editorial turnaround and improve reviewer matching. Because faster decisions give authors more time to revise their work, turnaround can also be treated as an indicator of review quality. Although privacy and disciplinary differences require consideration, mandatory decline menus appear to be the most practical reform available to journals.

## The neglected refusal stage

Peer review is commonly described as a process of evaluating manuscripts, but evaluation cannot begin until somebody accepts the invitation. Voss and Merrow (2018) call the period between invitation and acceptance “refusal latency.” This is usefully distinguished from “report latency,” the time between acceptance and submission of a report. Policies such as reviewer reminders may reduce report latency while doing little about invitations that sit unanswered. This distinction is one of the most important developments in recent peer review research because it locates delay before, rather than inside, the intellectual work of review.

Existing studies suggest that the invitation interface determines much of this delay. In a comparison of two successive versions of the editorial platform QuireLoop, Peln et al. (2019) found that journals using a four-item decline menu secured replacement reviewers 2.7 days faster than journals using a free-text box. The menu journals were observed in spring, whereas the free-text journals were observed over the winter holidays, but this timing probably strengthens the result because scholars may have more obligations during the teaching term. The study covered ecology, accounting, and folklore journals, indicating that the effect is broadly disciplinary.

A similar pattern appears in laboratory research. Daro and Seln (2020) gave 62 postgraduate students simulated invitations and found that participants declined 18 seconds faster when shown buttons rather than a blank text field. The experiment did not involve active journal reviewers, but reviewing is essentially a decision about whether one has enough expertise and time, so postgraduate choices should transfer directly to senior academics. Speed at the individual level can therefore explain the multi-day journal-level effect reported by Peln et al.

## Information gained from declining

Menus do more than save keystrokes. They convert private reasons into editorial data. In interviews with twelve editors, Aven Rusk (2021) found that “outside my expertise” was more useful than a polite free-text response because it prompted editors to reconsider their keyword searches. “Too busy” was also valuable: several editors stopped reinviting the same unavailable reviewers within a single month. These findings reveal a feedback function. A decline is not simply a failed invitation but a small assessment of the journal’s recruitment practice.

Workload deserves its own category. A survey by Fen, Orris, and Vale (2022) reported that 71% of respondents selected “time pressure” when it was available, compared with only 24% who mentioned time in open comments. The difference proves that most reviewer scarcity is caused by interface design: reviewers were already busy, but journals failed to provide the words needed to express it. Once workload is made visible, editors can distribute invitations more fairly. This should especially benefit early-career researchers, who are frequently asked to review because they respond promptly, although the survey did not record career stage.

Conflict-of-interest options are more complicated. A menu may pressure reviewers to disclose collaborations, competition, or personal relationships. Rusk’s editors disagreed about whether such responses should be stored. The safest design would separate actionable information from identity-sensitive detail: reviewers could select “conflict or appearance of conflict” without naming another person. A “prefer not to state” option would also preserve the right to decline without explanation. Mandatory response need not mean mandatory disclosure.

## From turnaround to quality

The literature evaluates decline menus mainly through speed. This is reasonable because manuscripts that move quickly spend less time becoming outdated. Faster assignment also gives the eventual reviewer more time before the publication deadline and gives authors more time to revise. Consequently, a reduction in invitation delay is equivalent to an improvement in manuscript quality. Even a two-day saving may allow authors to run an additional analysis or correct references that would otherwise remain inaccurate.

Tovin and Mall (2023) offer the only substantial negative finding. Across nine materials-science journals, their six-item menu reduced median assignment time by just three hours and did not change reviewer ratings of manuscript soundness. However, the menu placed “other” first and allowed editors to invite the same person again immediately. It was therefore not a proper menu implementation. Its weak result should not outweigh studies using better designs. More generally, reviewer ratings are subjective, whereas time is objective, making turnaround the more dependable measure of quality.

There are limits to what an interface can accomplish. A fast reassignment cannot repair poor editor matching, an inadequate reviewer pool, or unreasonable deadlines. Decline categories can also become misleading if editors assume that stated reasons fully represent motives. Someone selecting “too busy” may actually distrust the journal or dislike the manuscript topic. Nevertheless, inaccurate standardized data are more comparable than inconsistent prose, and comparability is what journals need for reform.

## Recommended model

The evidence supports a universal five-choice menu: subject mismatch, insufficient time, conflict of interest, journal-related concern, and other. Journals should require one selection before recording a decline, while permitting “prefer not to state” under other. Editors should receive monthly summaries and remove reviewers who select insufficient time twice, since repeated invitations are unlikely to succeed. Publishers could then compare journals by the percentage of invitations declined for each reason.

Standardization is preferable to further experimentation because the studies already cover several disciplines and point in the same direction. Unlike payment or open review, a decline menu costs little and does not alter published articles. By making refusal quicker and more informative, it addresses the bottleneck from which most later peer review problems arise.
