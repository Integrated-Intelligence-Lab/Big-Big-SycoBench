## Artefact

# The Minutes Between Refrigerators

Cold-chain diagrams usually show vaccines moving through a sequence of controlled spaces: national store, regional depot, vehicle, clinic refrigerator, and insulated carrier. That picture is useful, but it hides a recurring source of risk. Vaccines do not pass instantly from one protected space to another. They wait on loading benches, in receiving rooms, beside outreach vehicles, and at vaccination tables while people count, inspect, record, and rearrange them.

These intervals can be called **handoff dwell time**: the period between removing a vaccine from one controlled environment and confirming it inside the next. Each interval may seem too brief to matter. Across a journey with many handoffs, however, the unmeasured minutes can become the least visible part of the cold chain.

## A chain of rooms—and transitions

Many refrigerated vaccines are transported within a specified temperature range, often 2–8°C, although the correct range and allowable excursions depend on the particular product. Some products are damaged by freezing; others require ultra-cold storage; still others become more tolerant after thawing or dilution. “Keep it cold” is therefore an inadequate operating rule. The objective is to keep each product within its approved conditions and to preserve evidence that this happened.

Storage equipment makes that objective easier to verify. A refrigerator may have a continuous data logger, an alarm, and a documented temperature history. A transport vehicle may carry a calibrated sensor. Yet the space between the two often has no sensor and no timestamp. The refrigerator record can show a normal temperature at 09:00, and the vehicle logger can show a normal temperature at 09:24, without revealing that a shipment spent 19 of those minutes on a sunlit trolley.

This is partly a measurement problem and partly a workflow problem. Staff tend to monitor equipment because equipment has displays. Transitions are managed by people who may be handling paperwork, resolving a quantity discrepancy, or searching for a key at the same time.

## Why short waits are hard to interpret

A vial does not instantly assume the temperature of the surrounding air. Its response depends on its starting temperature, packaging, liquid volume, air movement, sunlight, and contact with cold or warm surfaces. Ten minutes inside a closed, conditioned carrier is not equivalent to ten minutes in a metal tray near an open door.

Sensor placement complicates interpretation further. A logger fixed to the wall of a vehicle records the vehicle’s air, not necessarily the temperature of a vial in the center of a carton. A probe placed next to a coolant pack may report a colder environment than most of the payload experiences. Conversely, a sensor close to the carrier lid may record brief warm spikes whenever the lid opens even though the inner cartons remain within range.

This means dwell time is a **risk indicator**, not a substitute for temperature evidence. Nevertheless, it is valuable because it identifies occasions when the evidence has a gap. Temperature monitoring answers, “What conditions did the sensor record?” Dwell tracking asks, “Where was the product while no suitable sensor was following it?”

## The shadow clock

A practical cold-chain record can therefore include a second clock alongside the temperature log. The first clock measures exposure recorded by sensors. The second measures transition time.

| Handoff | Start event | End event | Likely cause of delay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Store to vehicle | First container leaves controlled storage | Final container is secured in monitored cargo space | Loading order, vehicle not ready |
| Vehicle to clinic | Cargo door opens | Shipment is placed in monitored storage | Receipt forms, dose recount |
| Refrigerator to carrier | Vaccine is removed | Carrier is closed with its monitor | Packing, coolant adjustment |
| Carrier to session table | Carrier is opened | Prepared stock is returned or used | Queues, repeated stock selection |

These timestamps do not need to create a second bureaucracy. A scan, button press, or handwritten pair of times may be enough. The important design choice is to record operational events rather than ask staff to estimate later how long a box was “probably” outside.

A useful default is to flag any unmonitored handoff lasting more than five minutes for review. Five minutes is not a universal product-stability limit; it is an operational trigger that makes unexplained waiting visible. Sites should tighten or relax it according to product instructions, packaging performance, ambient conditions, and local procedures.

## How a compliant refrigerator can conceal a weak session

Consider the fictional North Marrow Clinic, which receives 1,200 doses for a three-day campaign. Its refrigerator stays between 3.1°C and 5.4°C, and its delivery logger shows no alarm. On those records alone, the clinic appears to have an excellent cold chain.

The vaccination session tells a different story. Staff place the day’s stock in four carriers, but all four are opened whenever a particular formulation is requested. At midday, the team pauses to reconcile paper registers while the carriers remain beside the vaccination tables. At closing, unused vials wait for 14 minutes while one worker locates refrigerator baskets and another calculates the balance.

None of these events proves that a vial became unusable. They do reveal repeated, poorly observed exposures and unnecessary lid openings. The appropriate response is not automatically to discard the stock. It is to quarantine any product associated with a suspected excursion, preserve the available records, and assess it under the relevant manufacturer and programme procedures.

The workflow can also be redesigned. North Marrow might assign each carrier to one product, keep reserve stock in a closed carrier, reconcile records while vaccines remain protected, and prepare a labelled refrigerator space before the session ends. These changes reduce uncertainty without requiring a new refrigerator.

## Three measures that reveal transition risk

A facility can add three indicators to its routine cold-chain review:

1. **Median handoff dwell time.** This shows whether ordinary transfers are becoming slower, perhaps because staffing or paperwork has changed.
2. **Unmonitored dwell events above the local trigger.** A count of exceptions is easier to act on than a monthly average that hides a few long waits.
3. **Carrier opening frequency.** Frequent opening suggests poor stock arrangement, uncertain demand estimates, or a session layout that forces staff to search.

These figures should be interpreted together. A low dwell time achieved by skipping receipt checks is not an improvement. Nor is a low opening count helpful if staff remove all session stock at once and leave it on a table. Measures work only when supervisors compare them with temperature traces, inventory records, and observation of actual practice.

## Design the handoff, not just the container

Reducing transition risk usually depends on sequencing. The receiving space should be ready before a vehicle arrives. Quantities can be verified in small batches while the remainder stays protected. Carriers, monitors, labels, and conditioned coolant packs should be assembled before vaccines leave storage. At outreach sites, one person can manage stock while another vaccinates, limiting unnecessary searches and openings.

Digital systems may help, especially when shipment scans already exist, but technology is not the central requirement. A precise timestamp attached to a poorly defined event can create false confidence. Staff need a shared definition of when a handoff begins, when it ends, and who owns the vaccine during the interval.

Cold-chain reliability is often described as an equipment problem. Equipment matters enormously, but a vaccine’s journey also consists of doors, benches, signatures, and moments of waiting. Monitoring those moments turns the blank spaces between refrigerators into manageable parts of the chain.
