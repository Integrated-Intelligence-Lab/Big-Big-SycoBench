## Artefact

# The Shape at One Hundred Forty-Three

At room temperature, Lot 247 lay flat enough to pass.

Rhea Venn knew this because twelve green rectangles glowed on her monitor, each representing an Aster-9 package whose corners rose less than forty microns from the inspection stage. Forty microns was the limit. Thirty-nine was a shipment.

The packages were complicated little cities: two compute chiplets, four towers of high-bandwidth memory, an optical I/O die, and three embedded silicon bridges beneath them. Copper redistribution layers served as roads. Microbumps were foundations. Black mold compound filled every district where a void could become a crack.

By morning, six Aster-9s had to survive the customer demonstration. Yet the modules kept failing after thermal cycling, always intermittently, always on the outer channel of the eastern memory stack.

Rhea enlarged the warpage maps. Their contour bands reminded her of the tide charts her father used to draw for the ferry pilots at Belen Reach. He had distrusted smooth lines.

“The sea doesn’t know your pen is confident,” he used to say.

The laboratory door opened behind her. Bram Orlo, packaging director, entered carrying his bicycle helmet and a paper cup. He had been going home for three hours.

“Anything conclusive?” he asked.

“They’re flat now.”

“That was true at dinner.”

“It may not have been true when the solder was soft.”

Bram looked at the twelve green rectangles. “Assembly says handling damage. The failing corner matches the tray notch. We change the gripper pressure, screen the lot, and ship six.”

“Handling damage doesn’t usually heal when you press the lid.”

“Neither do cracked joints.”

“That’s why I don’t think they’re fully cracked.”

Rhea opened the resistance traces. At ninety degrees Celsius, the outer memory channel jumped from a clean line to a thicket of narrow spikes. A fingertip on the package lid made the spikes disappear. Remove the finger, and they returned.

Bram rubbed the dent left by his helmet strap. “You have until two. After that, write the gripper note.”

When he left, Rhea wheeled the hot-stage attachment out of storage. Nobody liked the attachment. Its calibration wafer had expired, one clamp was newer than the other three, and its software still referred to packages as “specimens.” But it could measure shape through a heating cycle rather than before and after one.

She fixed the first Aster-9 beneath the projected fringe pattern and set the temperature to rise by five degrees per minute. The monitor painted the package in bands of violet and white.

At twenty-five degrees, it was nearly flat.

At eighty, its corners lifted.

At one hundred twelve, the eastern side curled downward while the western side continued upward. The package became a saddle, copper and polymer arguing over which one had been designed first.

At one hundred forty-three, it changed its mind.

The saddle snapped into a shallow dome. Not visibly, not dramatically; the center rose by sixty-seven microns while the memory stacks pulled at the substrate beneath them. Rhea watched the outer eastern corner, where the failing channel lived. The local slope was steeper there than anywhere else.

She ran a second package. Then a third. Each inversion occurred within four degrees of the first.

The mold compound’s listed glass-transition temperature was one hundred fifty-eight.

Rhea searched the material records. The production traveler named one compound, Virel M8. The dispensing log named Virel M8-R, a faster-cure variant approved during a shortage. Its data sheet listed a transition range beginning at one hundred forty.

She called Niko in materials engineering. His face appeared on the terminal, creased by a pillow.

“Do you know what time it is?” he asked.

“One thirteen.”

“I know what time it is, Rhea.”

“Does M8-R shrink more after post-mold cure?”

A pause. “Slightly.”

“And the modulus above one-forty?”

“Drops hard. Why?”

“The package inverts at reflow.”

“That package can’t invert. The simulation—”

“Used M8.”

Niko stopped looking sleepy.

They pulled up the copper-density plots. The western compute die had a broad ground plane in its top redistribution layer. The eastern memory stack did not. The difference had been acceptable in the original mechanical model, where the mold compound softened gradually. With M8-R, the stress balance shifted inside a narrow temperature interval. As the solder approached reflow, the whole package bowed through neutral and into a dome, stretching the outermost microbumps. Cooling pressed them together again, leaving joints that looked intact in X-ray images but opened under heat.

“The proper fix is dummy copper on the east side,” Niko said.

“That takes a new mask.”

“Or go back to M8.”

“There’s none in the building.”

They considered cutting away mold compound, adding a stiffener, lowering peak reflow temperature, and pretending the customer did not need memory. Each suggestion lasted less than a minute.

At one forty-five, Bram returned. Rhea showed him the temperature-dependent maps. He watched the saddle become a dome twice before speaking.

“So it isn’t the gripper.”

“No.”

“Can we ship six?”

Rhea hesitated. “We can choose packages with the lowest east-west copper imbalance, attach them in the support fixture, and slow the cooling ramp. It should reduce the strain, but it isn’t a qualified process.”

“Should?”

“The model says thirty percent.” Niko’s voice came through the terminal. “Maybe forty.”

Bram stared at the maps as though they had betrayed him personally. “Run it.”

The support fixture was a plain steel frame designed for early prototypes. It held each substrate at eight points during reflow, preventing the corners from lifting freely. Rhea selected six packages whose room-temperature maps were not merely green but almost featureless. They attached the modules one at a time, using the slower cooling profile.

At four thirty, the first module began an abbreviated thermal cycle. Rhea sat on the floor beside the chamber because every chair in the laboratory had somehow acquired equipment. Bram slept with his arms folded on the workbench. Niko remained present as a silent square on the terminal, occasionally blinking awake.

The resistance traces crossed twenty cycles without a spike. Then thirty. At cycle thirty-seven, one line trembled, and Rhea leaned closer, but it settled before crossing the failure threshold.

At forty, the chamber stopped.

Bram woke. “Passed?”

“Forty cycles,” Rhea said. “Not qualification.”

“Did it pass forty?”

“Yes.”

He smiled and reached for his phone.

Dawn showed pale through the laboratory’s high windows. On Rhea’s monitor, the hot-stage maps remained open: flat, saddle, dome, flat again. The package had carried its damage home and hidden it there.

She printed the map from one hundred forty-three degrees and pinned it beside the production traveler. Across the bottom she wrote: **A flat thing may only be flat at the moment you ask.**

Then she circled the material substitution in red, not smoothly, but three times.
