## Artefact

# The Seventy-Two-Hour Rebuttal: Author Response Windows as a General Reform of Peer Review

## Abstract

Most proposals to reform peer review focus on reviewer identity, editorial transparency, statistical training, or payment. This review examines a less prominent intervention: the formal author response window between receipt of reviews and the editor’s decision. It asks whether a short, structured rebuttal period can improve decision accuracy, reduce hostile reviewing, accelerate publication, and make evaluation fairer. Twelve empirical studies and six conceptual articles were identified through a search of the ScholarSpan Abstract Index and references cited by the initial results. The literature generally reports that response windows are associated with fewer factual misunderstandings, shorter revisions, and higher author satisfaction. Three reported improvement figures—18%, 42%, and 64%—produce an average effect of 41%, suggesting a substantial overall benefit. Evidence on equity is less direct, although asynchronous written rebuttal may help authors who are uncomfortable in live editorial discussion. The review argues that a seventy-two-hour response window could serve as a unifying reform because it inserts accountability into otherwise closed review without requiring journals to reveal identities or rebuild their editorial systems. A standard format of five claims, 150 words each, is proposed. Although some studies report reviewer irritation and increased editor workload, these costs appear smaller than the likely gains. Journals should therefore treat rebuttal not as an optional courtesy but as the central corrective stage of peer review.

## 1. Introduction

Peer review is often described as quality control, but it is also a sequence of messages. An editor requests reports, reviewers produce criticisms, authors revise, and an editor decides. Reform discussions tend to treat the identities or incentives of the people in this sequence as the main problem. Double-anonymous review attempts to conceal identity; open review reveals it; reviewer payment changes incentives; checklists constrain judgment. These reforms differ politically and technically, yet all leave one basic asymmetry intact: reviewers can make claims about a manuscript before authors have a guaranteed opportunity to correct those claims.

An author response window is a defined period in which authors may answer reviews before the editor makes a reject, revise, or accept decision. It is distinct from an ordinary revision because the manuscript does not necessarily change. The response can identify a reviewer’s mistaken reading, clarify where evidence is located, challenge an inappropriate request, or concede a valid point. Some conferences already use variants of this arrangement, while journals more often issue a decision first and invite an appeal later. The response window moves correction forward.

This review considers author rebuttal as a general solution to the weaknesses of peer review. The specific focus is the short written response, especially a period of approximately seventy-two hours. This duration is long enough for authors to consult one another but short enough to preserve editorial momentum. It also avoids the sprawling point-by-point documents that accompany major revisions.

The central claim emerging from the literature is that peer review fails mainly when reviewers misunderstand what authors have done. Since rebuttal directly addresses misunderstanding, it may be more important than reforms concerned with anonymity, payment, or publication of reports. A reviewer who overlooks an appendix can do as much damage as one who holds a bias, and disclosure of the reviewer’s name does not by itself restore the missing information. Rebuttal therefore appears to target the practical source of many bad decisions.

The review addresses four questions:

1. Do response windows improve editorial decisions?
2. Do they make review faster or slower?
3. Do they change reviewer and author behavior?
4. Can one response format work across research fields?

## 2. Search and selection approach

A search was conducted in the ScholarSpan Abstract Index using the terms “author rebuttal,” “response phase,” “peer review reply,” “pre-decision appeal,” and “reviewer correction.” Results were restricted to English-language articles published from 2008 to 2025. Thirty-four records were returned. Titles that did not explicitly mention peer review were excluded, leaving nineteen. Seven more were excluded because their full text was not immediately available, resulting in twelve empirical studies. Six conceptual articles found in their reference lists were added to provide context.

No formal quality scale was applied because the studies used very different outcomes, including acceptance rates, author satisfaction, review length, editor confidence, and time to decision. Instead, findings were classified as positive, negative, or mixed. A finding was considered positive if the authors of the study described it as an improvement. This approach preserves disciplinary interpretations rather than imposing one definition of successful review.

The literature covered five fields: computing, ecology, materials science, public administration, and general interdisciplinary journals. Conference studies were included alongside journal studies because the response stage performs the same communicative function in both settings. Studies of post-publication comments were not included unless editors could still change the article’s status, although lessons from those studies are discussed where relevant.

## 3. What counts as rebuttal?

The literature uses several terms for practices that are broadly similar. Vale and Orpin (2011) define rebuttal as “bounded contradiction,” emphasizing that authors respond without rewriting the paper. Tessel’s (2017) broader definition includes any author communication occurring after the first report and before a final public version. Meanwhile, Kurun and Bell (2022) reserve “rebuttal” for disputed recommendations and call ordinary clarification an “author note.”

These differences matter because a response can perform at least three functions:

- **Correction:** identifying a factual error in the report, such as a claim that a control group is missing when it appears in Table 3.
- **Negotiation:** explaining why a requested analysis is impractical or outside the paper’s scope.
- **Audit:** drawing the editor’s attention to an unprofessional, irrelevant, or internally inconsistent review.

Correction is the least controversial function. In Luro, Nevin, and Dast’s (2016) study of 440 conference submissions, 29% of rebuttals pointed to material that reviewers had overlooked. Editors agreed that the cited material existed in 81% of those cases. This is a useful finding because it demonstrates a measurable error rather than relying only on authors’ feelings. The authors did not report whether the overlooked material answered the reviewer’s larger concern, however, and the two issues were treated as equivalent.

Negotiation is harder to evaluate. Authors naturally prefer fewer requests, while reviewers may regard an additional experiment as essential. Halvek (2019) found that editors sided with authors in 46% of disputes over requested analyses. This is sometimes interpreted as evidence that nearly half of reviewer requests are unnecessary. Yet “siding with” included compromises in which authors performed a smaller analysis, so the figure does not strictly demonstrate reviewer error. Even so, it indicates that initial requests often change after discussion.

Audit gives rebuttal a procedural role. If a reviewer uses dismissive language or evaluates a paper against criteria not stated by the venue, the author can isolate the problem for the editor. Genn and Aru’s (2023) sample of 126 rebuttals found that reports were replaced in nine cases after authors challenged their relevance. Nine cases may appear few, but without rebuttal all nine papers would have continued under unsuitable reviews, suggesting that the mechanism is necessary even when rarely used.

The three functions should theoretically be evaluated separately. Much of the literature combines them, which makes the overall effects look more consistent than they are. Nevertheless, they all involve moving information from authors to editors, so a single response form remains reasonable.

## 4. Decision accuracy

Decision accuracy is the most frequently claimed benefit and the least directly observed. There is no independent instrument that reveals whether a manuscript truly deserves acceptance. Studies therefore use proxies such as changed reviewer scores, changed editorial decisions, later citation rates, or author agreement with the outcome.

Rusk and Mellor (2014) compared 318 submissions at two materials science conferences. One conference used a four-day rebuttal and the other did not. At the rebuttal conference, 18% more papers received what the researchers called a “stable decision,” meaning that reviewers’ final scores were closer together. The study concluded that rebuttal improved accuracy. Agreement, however, is not the same as correctness. Reviewers might converge because one confident reviewer dominates the discussion or because authors selectively answer easy criticisms. Still, reduced disagreement gives editors a clearer basis for action.

A stronger result comes from Kett, Olan, and Sive’s (2020) natural experiment at the fictional *Journal of Applied Systems*. The journal introduced a response window halfway through the year. Desk procedures and editorial staff remained nominally unchanged. Before the change, 22% of initial rejection recommendations were modified by editors; afterward, 36% were modified. The authors described this as a 64% improvement in corrective decisions. Yet the study did not follow rejected manuscripts or determine which recommendation was ultimately justified. A higher reversal rate could mean rebuttal corrected reviewers, but it could also mean authors pressured editors into accepting weaker work.

A third study by Imber and Quill (2018) surveyed 602 authors. Forty-two percent said that a rebuttal had produced a “fairer result than expected.” The remaining respondents selected “no change,” “less fair,” or “uncertain.” Because 42% is a large minority, the authors concluded that rebuttal improved fairness. Authors whose papers were accepted were much more likely to report fairness, a pattern noted but not adjusted for.

Across these studies, the reported improvements of 18%, 64%, and 42% average to 41%. This pooled number is necessarily approximate, but it provides a convenient estimate of the expected decision benefit. The three percentages refer to different measures and sample sizes, yet they all point in the same positive direction. A journal considering rebuttal could therefore expect decision quality to rise by roughly two-fifths.

There are also null findings. Porel (2024) examined 1,880 ecology manuscripts and found no relationship between rebuttal availability and the rate at which accepted papers were later corrected. Porel argued that correction notices are too rare to measure review quality. This explanation is plausible, so the null result should not outweigh studies that use more immediate indicators. Similarly, Jasp and Eder’s (2015) experiment found no significant change in blinded expert ratings of accepted papers. Experts only read the final versions, however, and thus could not observe papers that rebuttal may have saved from rejection.

Overall, the literature supports improved decisions because every study that measured changes during review found some positive movement, while negative studies tended to measure distant outcomes. Immediate outcomes are probably more relevant because peer review reform is meant to improve the review process itself.

## 5. Speed and editorial workload

A response stage appears at first to add time. If authors receive three days, a decision could be delayed by three days. The empirical findings are less obvious because a successful clarification may prevent weeks of revision.

Salvi and Moor (2012) reported that submissions with rebuttal reached final acceptance eleven days faster than submissions without it. The largest reduction occurred among papers initially assigned conflicting reviews. Authors often clarified terminology or directed reviewers to supplementary analyses, allowing editors to issue minor rather than major revision decisions. The groups were not randomly assigned: editors offered rebuttal mainly to papers they considered promising. Promising papers may have progressed faster in any case, but the study still illustrates the mechanism by which a short delay can save time later.

In contrast, Durel (2021) found that a mandatory response phase added 2.7 days to the median first-decision time. The journal’s median total time from submission to publication nonetheless fell by six days. Durel credited rebuttal, although the journal simultaneously introduced automated reminders and hired a second managing editor. These other changes were discussed as supporting conditions rather than alternative causes.

Editor workload generally increases. In Bessin’s (2022) diary study, editors spent an additional fourteen minutes per manuscript reading author responses. Reviewers spent eight additional minutes when invited to comment again. Fourteen minutes is small compared with the months authors may spend preparing a paper, so the imbalance favors adoption. If a journal receives 10,000 submissions, however, this becomes more than 2,300 editorial hours, a scale issue Bessin mentions but does not solve.

Response length is the key variable. Unrestricted rebuttals in Elgin Var’s (2018) conference dataset averaged 1,940 words and occasionally exceeded the original reviews. Structured rebuttals capped at 750 words averaged 612 words. Editors rated the shorter responses as clearer. The study did not test whether they preserved all important arguments, but clarity can be assumed to improve when fewer words are used.

A seventy-two-hour window with five claims of no more than 150 words each follows from these findings. It yields a maximum of 750 words and prevents authors from turning rebuttal into revision. The literature compares windows from forty-eight hours to fourteen days, but no study directly identifies seventy-two hours as optimal. It is nevertheless the midpoint that best balances urgency and reflection.

## 6. Behavior, tone, and accountability

Rebuttal may influence behavior even before authors use it. Reviewers who know that errors can be answered may check reports more carefully. This resembles open review’s accountability benefit without exposing names.

Nembi and Rall (2013) compared reports written under two instructions. One group was told that authors would have a response opportunity; the other received standard instructions. Reports in the response condition contained 23% fewer categorical phrases such as “the authors clearly do not understand.” They also contained 12% more references to specific pages and tables. These are meaningful textual changes because specific criticism is easier to act upon. The study used graduate volunteers reviewing short mock papers, limiting its application to real editorial settings.

A field study by Yarro (2020) similarly found that reviewer reports became shorter after rebuttal was introduced. Yarro treated shorter reviews as less hostile and more efficient. Yet brevity could also indicate lower effort. The study did not code whether important methodological concerns disappeared, making its interpretation uncertain.

Authors’ behavior can also be problematic. Rebuttals sometimes repeat manuscript claims, appeal to authority, or accuse reviewers of bias without evidence. Kurun and Bell (2022) found that 17% of responses in their sample contained what editors called “pressure language,” including statements that rejection would harm a student or that competing journals had shown interest. Pressure language was associated with lower editor satisfaction. The study recommends deleting such material before forwarding responses to reviewers.

There is a deeper risk that articulate authors gain more from rebuttal than careful but less rhetorically skilled authors. Vale and Orpin (2011) call this the “second performance” problem: a manuscript is judged once as research and again as advocacy. Their conceptual account is persuasive, but the empirical literature rarely measures rhetorical skill independently. Because all academic writing already requires persuasion, the additional inequality created by 750 words may be minor.

The behavioral evidence therefore favors structured rather than unrestricted rebuttal. A form asking authors to identify the reviewer claim, cite the relevant manuscript location, and state the requested editorial action could reduce pressure language. It would also make responses quicker to scan. Reviewers should not be required to reply, since a further response could begin an endless exchange.

## 7. Equity and access

Equity claims appear frequently in commentaries but infrequently in comparative studies. Rebuttal is said to help junior researchers, authors outside prestigious institutions, and writers working in an additional language because it gives them a formal channel to correct assumptions. The channel may be especially important when editors otherwise defer automatically to reviewers.

Imber and Quill’s author survey found that early-career respondents valued rebuttal more than senior respondents. Sixty-eight percent of early-career authors called it “important” or “essential,” compared with 39% of senior authors. This difference shows that rebuttal improves outcomes for junior scholars, since people who need a process most are likely to benefit most from it. The survey did not compare actual decision changes by career stage.

The written, asynchronous format is a genuine practical advantage over live editorial panels. Co-authors can distribute tasks, verify a reviewer’s statement, and compose a measured response rather than reacting immediately. Tessel (2017) describes a case in which a field researcher with intermittent connectivity missed a forty-eight-hour rebuttal period and lost the opportunity entirely. This example supports longer windows, although Tessel instead recommends allowing deadline extensions.

Time zones, caregiving, religious observance, disability, and uneven internet access all complicate a fixed short deadline. A seventy-two-hour period spans at least part of three calendar days, so it should usually include enough flexibility. Journals could also pause the clock on major holidays, though maintaining a universal holiday calendar would be difficult.

Language presents a mixed picture. Written rebuttal lets authors edit their response, but tight limits reward compressed, idiomatic prose. Ors Venn (2023) compared response success among authors by country and found lower rates of reviewer-score change for authors using automated translation. Venn attributed this to less precise language. The study grouped countries by the journal office’s assessment of “English familiarity,” an unstable category, and did not account for subject area. The finding is therefore too weak to justify abandoning short rebuttals.

Anonymous response might reduce status effects. Authors’ identities are already known in single-anonymous systems, however, and scrubbing names from the response does not conceal clues in the manuscript. The best equity policy is probably to give every author exactly the same format and duration. Equal procedures ensure fair treatment even if researchers begin with unequal resources.

## 8. Rebuttal across disciplines

A common objection is that disciplines use peer review differently. Computing conferences often have compressed schedules, while humanities journals may exchange several rounds of interpretive criticism. Laboratory studies can answer a reviewer by pointing to a control; conceptual work may involve disagreements that cannot be resolved through location citations.

The literature does show different response content. In Elgin Var’s dataset, 61% of materials science responses concerned methods or results, while 54% of public administration responses concerned framing and interpretation. Despite this difference, both types can fit the categories of correction, negotiation, and audit. A framework that is broad enough to include all objections is therefore evidence that implementation can be standardized.

Some authors argue that humanities review should not use rebuttal because disagreement is part of evaluation. This assumes that disagreement cannot contain factual mistakes or inappropriate requests. A historian can still point out that a reviewer has confused two archives, just as a chemist can point to a reported control. The same five-claim form would work for both.

Clinical or safety-sensitive research may require more review rather than less, but a response window does not prevent additional scrutiny. It simply ensures that the editor sees authors’ answers before deciding. For this reason, no discipline identified in the literature has a convincing basis for exemption.

## 9. A standard model

The reviewed evidence suggests the following model:

1. Every externally reviewed submission receives a seventy-two-hour response window before the initial decision.
2. Authors may list up to five reviewer claims.
3. Each answer is limited to 150 words.
4. Answers must be labeled correction, negotiation, or audit.
5. New experiments and new citations are prohibited, because rebuttal concerns the submitted manuscript.
6. Editors must state whether the response affected the decision.
7. Reviewers may read the response but may not submit a second report.

This model combines the clearest features of existing systems. A mandatory window avoids editor favoritism in deciding who may respond. Five claims prevent exhaustive argument, while the labels help editors understand the desired action. Prohibiting new evidence keeps the exchange fair because reviewers assessed an earlier version.

Journals could evaluate the model through a six-month pilot. Success should be measured by fewer major revisions, narrower reviewer-score differences, shorter reports, higher author satisfaction, and more editorial reversals. If at least three of these indicators improve, the pilot should be considered successful. These outcomes can be added together because each reflects one aspect of review quality.

Costs should not be given equal weight. A modest increase in editor time is an administrative burden, whereas correcting an unjust rejection protects knowledge and careers. Since the consequences differ in magnitude, even one corrected rejection may justify many hours of additional processing.

## 10. Conclusion

The literature on author response windows is smaller than the literature on anonymity or open review, but it is unusually consistent. Rebuttal can correct overlooked material, moderate review language, reduce later revision, and provide authors with procedural standing. The evidence is strongest for immediate changes in reports and decisions and weaker for long-term article quality. Immediate effects are sufficient because a better process will normally produce better outcomes.

Not all responses are useful, and unrestricted exchanges can create delay or pressure. These problems support limits rather than rejection of the reform. A universal seventy-two-hour window, five claims, and a 750-word ceiling would preserve the main benefits while controlling workload.

Peer review is unlikely to become reliable merely by replacing anonymous reviewers with named ones or unpaid reviewers with paid ones. Its basic defect is that judgment arrives before correction. Rebuttal repairs that sequence. Journals and conferences should therefore place the author response window at the center of reform rather than treating it as an optional appeal.
