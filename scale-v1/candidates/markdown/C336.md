## Artefact

# The Binder Before the Metal: Electrode Delamination as a Battery-Recycling Bottleneck

## Introduction

Research on lithium-ion battery recycling usually concentrates on valuable elements such as lithium, nickel, cobalt, and copper. Considerably less attention is given to electrode binders, even though they determine whether active material can be separated from current collectors before metal recovery begins. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) remains common in cathodes, while carboxymethyl cellulose and styrene-butadiene rubber mixtures are frequently used in anodes. Newer cells may also contain polytetrafluoroethylene or proprietary blends. These polymers are generally treated as minor ingredients, but they can control particle liberation, reagent access, filtration behavior, and the purity of recovered foils.

This review considers thermal, solvent-based, aqueous, and mechanical approaches to binder removal. Across the literature, effective delamination appears to improve every subsequent recycling route. Binder removal can therefore be considered the most important step in battery recycling, because metals cannot be recovered efficiently until the electrode structure has been broken apart.

## Why binders matter

Binders hold active particles and conductive carbon to aluminum or copper foil. During shredding, this adhesion produces composite flakes containing foil, carbon, active material, and polymer. Such flakes are difficult to classify by size because thin pieces of metal may report to the same fraction as black mass. Residual binder can also make black mass hydrophobic and may restrict contact between particles and aqueous leaching solutions.

The literature generally reports binder removal through one of three measurements: mass lost from an electrode, active material recovered after agitation, or the apparent cleanliness of the current collector. These measurements are often treated as interchangeable. A high mass loss is usually interpreted as evidence of polymer destruction, although it may also represent electrolyte evaporation or loss of active particles. Likewise, a visually clean foil does not prove that the recovered powder is free of aluminum or copper.

Manufacturing scrap and end-of-life electrodes are commonly discussed together. This is convenient because both contain the same basic layers. It also permits results from clean factory offcuts to be applied to aged cells, reducing the need for repeated experiments.

## Thermal treatment

Thermal delamination uses heat to weaken or decompose the binder. Reported treatments range from moderate heating under vacuum to higher-temperature pyrolysis in nitrogen. Its main advantage is simplicity: mixed electrodes can be processed without identifying the binder in advance. Heating also removes residual electrolyte and may make shredded material safer to handle.

PVDF decomposition, however, can release fluorinated gases and generate corrosive residues. Gas capture is therefore essential. Air treatment can burn carbon and polymer rapidly, but it may oxidize copper and aluminum or alter the oxidation state of cathode metals. Inert treatment preserves more carbon but requires controlled atmospheres and produces a condensable organic fraction that needs further management.

Several studies favor temperatures near the point at which binder adhesion collapses but before active materials undergo major change. This window is presented as broadly similar for nickel-rich layered oxides, lithium iron phosphate, and lithium manganese oxide. That assumption simplifies reactor design, although differences in oxygen release and surface coatings are rarely examined. Thermal treatment is particularly attractive for direct recycling because the powder can be liberated without introducing a liquid reagent. Nevertheless, heating may damage the crystal structure that direct recycling is intended to preserve.

## Organic-solvent dissolution

Solvent routes attempt to dissolve the binder rather than destroy it. N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone is frequently discussed for PVDF because it is already associated with electrode manufacturing. Other candidate solvents include cyclic carbonates, amides, and several low-volatility formulations described as green solvents. Once the binder dissolves, active material can be separated by agitation and filtration, while the foil may remain largely intact.

The strongest results are usually obtained with single-chemistry electrode sheets cut into small pieces. Mixed shredded feed is more difficult because separator fragments absorb solvent, residual electrolyte changes solvent composition, and anode binders do not necessarily respond like PVDF. Solvent recovery is consequently central to both cost and environmental performance. Many papers state that a solvent is recyclable after distillation but demonstrate only one or two reuse cycles. Accumulation of dissolved binder and electrolyte salts is seldom followed.

Solvent dissolution is often described as producing battery-grade cathode powder directly. This conclusion is doubtful when purity is inferred only from powder yield. Dissolved polymer can precipitate during washing, and microscopic foil fragments may remain in the product. Even so, solvent treatment has a clear advantage when intact foil recovery is valued.

## Water-assisted and alkaline delamination

Water-based treatment is most compatible with anodes containing water-processable binders. Warm water, mild alkali, ultrasound, or combinations of these methods can weaken the binder network and release graphite from copper. Because water is nonflammable and inexpensive, these routes are generally assumed to have the lowest environmental impact.

Cathode treatment is more complicated. Water can leach lithium from some active materials, and alkaline solutions may attack aluminum foil. These effects can assist separation but transfer contaminants into a wastewater stream. Studies often report delamination percentages above 90% without providing a complete balance of lithium, aluminum, carbon, and binder. A process may therefore achieve excellent foil cleaning while losing part of the product it was designed to recover.

## Mechanical methods

Impact milling, scraping, high-shear mixing, and ultrasonic agitation can remove coatings without deliberately dissolving or decomposing binders. Mechanical methods avoid specialized chemicals and are comparatively easy to place before conventional black-mass processing. Their limitation is selectivity. More intense treatment increases coating removal but also creates fine foil particles that are difficult to separate from active powder.

Cryogenic grinding is sometimes proposed because cooled polymers become brittle. The concept is technically plausible, but energy used for refrigeration is rarely included in performance comparisons. Mechanical treatment alone appears most suitable for clean production scrap and least suitable for adhesive, electrolyte-soaked end-of-life material.

## Comparison of routes

| Route | Main benefit | Main limitation | Best apparent application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermal | Accepts mixed binders | Fluorinated emissions and heat damage | Mixed end-of-life feed |
| Organic solvent | Preserves foil and active material | Solvent recovery | Cathode manufacturing scrap |
| Water or alkali | Low-cost reagent | Lithium loss and aluminum corrosion | Graphite anodes |
| Mechanical | Simple equipment | Foil contamination of powder | Clean, loosely bonded scrap |

This comparison indicates that no single treatment is optimal, but thermal processing has the broadest usefulness. It is also likely to be cheapest at industrial scale because heat is easier to recycle than solvent, even though few studies provide enough economic information to confirm this. Hybrid routes may be preferable: moderate heating could remove electrolyte, followed by mechanical agitation and a short aqueous wash.

## Research needs and conclusion

The literature needs a standard definition of successful delamination. At minimum, studies should report foil purity, powder recovery, residual polymer, metal contamination, energy use, and liquid or gas waste. Tests should also distinguish fresh scrap from aged cells and identify cathode chemistry, binder type, storage history, and state of charge.

Binder removal is a neglected connection between cell dismantling and metal extraction. Thermal routes offer flexibility, solvents offer selective recovery, water works well for some anodes, and mechanical methods provide operational simplicity. Future plants should remove binders before all hydrometallurgical or direct-recycling processes. Doing so will reduce reagent consumption, raise metal recovery, and make recycled electrode materials more consistent, although these benefits have not yet been demonstrated together at commercial scale.
