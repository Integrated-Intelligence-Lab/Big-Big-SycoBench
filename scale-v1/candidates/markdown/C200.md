## Artefact

# Listening for Loose Lines: Acoustic Detection of Kelp-Farm Maintenance Events

## Question

Hydrophones near aquaculture structures often record low-frequency creaks and vibrations that analysts discard as deployment noise. This analysis asks whether that noise can instead indicate a maintenance problem: a slack or tangled kelp longline.

The Brindle Sound Longline Acoustic Record (BSLAR-42) covers 42 winter days at a three-line experimental kelp farm in Brindle Sound. Farmers reported that loose backbones sometimes struck droppers or oscillated against their floats, but inspections were too infrequent to establish when this began. We tested whether a repeatable acoustic signature distinguished normal lines from those requiring adjustment.

## Data and preparation

One hydrophone was clamped 0.5 m below the backbone of each farm line. A fourth, mounted on an independent bottom lander 620 m away, served as a reference. All four recorded for 10 minutes every half-hour at 48 kHz. A current meter at the centre of the farm logged speed every five minutes.

Thirteen inspection rounds produced 39 line-level observations. Inspectors classified each line as **normal**, **slack**, or **tangled/fouled** and photographed the attachment points. Slack and tangled/fouled states were combined as **maintenance required** because only ten abnormal observations were available, representing seven distinct maintenance episodes. Six episodes were corrected during the inspection; the seventh remained unresolved when recording ended.

Analysis was restricted to the 24 hours before each inspection and, where a repair occurred, the 24 hours after it. This produced 612 line-hours after removing 7% of files for clipping, incomplete recording, or obvious vessel passages. Vessel passages were screened using elevated broadband energy between 150 and 800 Hz. The screening rules were fixed before fitting the maintenance model.

For each minute, power spectra were estimated from four-second Hann-windowed segments with 50% overlap. Listening to a stratified sample suggested that loose lines generated intermittent, narrow peaks rather than a continuous broadband rise. Two features were therefore calculated:

- **Strum index:** energy from 24–58 Hz relative to the mean energy in flanking bands at 12–20 Hz and 65–90 Hz.
- **Peak persistence:** the proportion of five-minute frames in which the strongest 24–58 Hz peak stayed within a 3 Hz range.

The hourly value was the 90th percentile of the minute-level strum index, preserving bursts that a median would suppress. Absolute levels below 100 Hz were not used because the hydrophones had only been bench-calibrated from 100 Hz upward.

A mixed-effects logistic regression predicted maintenance state from strum index, peak persistence, current speed, and the interaction between strum index and current. Line identity was included as a random intercept. Performance was evaluated by leaving out one inspection round at a time, so hours around the same inspection did not occur in both training and test folds. Confidence intervals were estimated with 2,000 bootstrap resamples grouped by maintenance episode.

## Results

Abnormal lines had a median hourly strum index of 10.6 dB, compared with 2.1 dB for normal lines. During the same tidal periods, the reference hydrophone had a median of 1.7 dB. Peak persistence also increased, from 0.19 on normal lines to 0.51 on abnormal lines.

| Acoustic features plus current | 0.87 | 0.80 | 0.84 |

Sensitivity and specificity in the table use a predicted-probability threshold of 0.40, selected to give approximately equal costs to missed events and false alerts. The episode-bootstrap interval for the combined model’s AUC was 0.75–0.94. Each 3 dB increase in strum index was associated with an estimated 2.3-fold increase in the odds of a maintenance state after accounting for current speed, although the interval was broad (1.4–4.8).

Current strongly affected discrimination. Below 0.20 m/s, both normal and abnormal lines were usually quiet. Between 0.20 and 0.55 m/s, abnormal lines had a median strum index 8.2 dB above normal lines. Above 0.55 m/s, normal lines also began to strum, reducing the difference to 3.0 dB. Four of the six false alerts occurred during these high-current periods. Thus, a fixed sound-level alarm would likely perform worse than a current-conditioned model.

The repair comparisons supported, but did not prove, a mechanical explanation. Across the six corrected episodes, the strum index fell by a median 6.8 dB in the first post-repair tidal cycle. The corresponding change at the reference hydrophone was 0.5 dB. Five episodes showed a decline greater than 4 dB; the remaining episode involved removing fouling without retensioning the backbone and changed by only 1.6 dB.

Applying the combined model to the full pre-inspection windows produced an alert at least 12 hours before inspection in five of the seven episodes. This should not be interpreted as a measured 12-hour warning time, because the true onset of each fault was unknown. It only shows that the signature was already present before staff arrived.

## Interpretation

The results indicate that low-frequency deployment noise can carry useful information about the condition of a kelp longline. The localised increase, current-dependent pattern, and decline after repairs are more consistent with line motion than with a sound source affecting the whole inlet. Peak persistence adds information because sustained tonal motion is less typical of passing vessels or short impacts.

Several constraints limit operational use. The hydrophones were mechanically coupled to the farm, so the signal may have travelled through rope as well as through water. A bottom-mounted hydrophone at the same distance might not detect it. Maintenance labels were sparse, and assigning the preceding 24 hours the inspection state could mislabel periods before a fault developed. The shared current meter also cannot represent flow differences among all three lines, and no wave-height measurements were available. Finally, seven episodes are too few to establish whether the model transfers to other rope materials, farm layouts, or seasons.

A practical next trial should instrument more lines with both coupled and free-field hydrophones, add tension sensors, and schedule inspections immediately after acoustic alerts. That design would establish onset times and separate waterborne sound from structural vibration. On the present evidence, the acoustic index is best treated as a maintenance-screening signal rather than an autonomous fault detector.
