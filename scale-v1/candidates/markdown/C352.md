## Artefact

# Wastewater Epidemic Models Need a Moving Denominator

Wastewater surveillance is often praised for observing infections that clinical testing misses. Yet the models built on it commonly preserve a quieter source of error: they treat the population contributing to a sewer catchment as fixed. A district may be assigned its census population, university enrolment, or number of registered utility accounts, and that denominator may remain unchanged for months. Real sewer populations are not so obedient. Commuters arrive each morning, tourists occupy seasonal rentals, students leave during holidays, and industrial sites draw workers from outside the catchment. Wastewater epidemiological models should therefore treat the contributing population as a time-varying latent quantity rather than a constant. This change would not solve every difficulty in wastewater inference, but it would make estimated infection rates and comparisons between catchments more defensible.

## The denominator is part of the observation process

A wastewater sample does not directly reveal prevalence. After adjustment for flow, an observed pathogen signal can be represented approximately as

\[
Y_t = \kappa \frac{\sum_{a=0}^{A} I_{t-a}s_a}{N_t} + \epsilon_t,
\]

where \(I_{t-a}\) is the number of infected contributors at earlier times, \(s_a\) describes shedding by time since infection, \(N_t\) is the total contributing population, and \(\epsilon_t\) collects sampling and laboratory error. The constant \(\kappa\) covers assay and dilution factors not modelled separately. This expression is simplified, but it exposes an important fact: uncertainty about \(N_t\) can be mistaken for change in infection burden.

Consider the hypothetical Harbor Ward in Ravelin Bay. It has 80,000 registered residents but receives roughly 40,000 weekday commuters. If a model interprets a given viral load using the residential denominator alone, it may infer a prevalence of 3 percent where a denominator of 120,000 would imply 2 percent under otherwise identical assumptions. On a holiday, the direction of error could reverse. The illustration ignores differences in infection and shedding between residents and commuters, but that limitation strengthens the central point: the people represented in the numerator and denominator must be modelled as populations in motion.

This matters even when analysts focus on trends rather than absolute prevalence. Suppose the pathogen signal falls every weekend. A fixed-population model may interpret the recurring dip as reduced transmission or a laboratory artefact. In a business district, however, it may largely reflect the disappearance of commuters from the sewer system. Smoothing the series can conceal the pattern without explaining it. Worse, a transmission model fitted to the smoothed observations may distort its estimates of the reproduction number to accommodate a demographic rhythm that was never caused by transmission.

## A practical latent-population model

The alternative need not be a complete simulation of every toilet visit. A workable model could separate two linked processes. The first would describe infections and shedding. The second would estimate the daily contributing population around a slowly changing baseline, with terms for weekdays, holidays, academic calendars, large events, and exceptional disruptions. The population state could be constrained by several imperfect signals: wastewater flow, a human fecal biomarker, water consumption, transit entries, and anonymized counts of occupied buildings.

No single proxy should determine the estimate. Rain increases flow without adding people; diet can alter biomarker excretion; remote work can weaken the connection between transit use and sewer contribution. A Bayesian state-space model could combine these channels while assigning each its own error process. Catchments with sparse information could borrow broad patterns from similar catchments through partial pooling, while still allowing local deviations. The result would be a distribution for \(N_t\), not a falsely exact head count.

The epidemiological model should propagate that distribution into its infection estimates. If uncertainty about the contributing population is high during a festival week, uncertainty about prevalence should widen too. This is preferable to reporting a precise surge that may merely reflect an influx of visitors. Dashboards could show both total estimated infections contributing to the sample and infections per 100,000 contributors. The first may be most relevant for anticipating total clinical demand; the second is more useful for comparing risk through time and across places.

## Identifiability is an objection, not a veto

The strongest objection is that pathogen concentration alone cannot distinguish a larger infected population from more shedding per infected person, a smaller total population, or changes in dilution. Adding a latent denominator may appear to create another parameter that the data cannot identify. That concern is valid. A model with flexible infection, shedding, flow, and population processes could explain almost any observation after the fact.

The response should be constraint rather than retreat. Population changes can be given regularized dynamics and informed by external measurements collected for independent purposes. Calendar effects should be estimated across repeated weeks instead of freely fitted each day. Analysts should also compare the dynamic model with a fixed-denominator version and report when the available evidence cannot meaningfully separate them. In a residential catchment with stable occupancy, the more elaborate model may correctly collapse toward a nearly constant population. In a transport hub or resort district, assuming stability should require evidence rather than serve as the automatic default.

There is also a risk of using mobility data twice: once to estimate how many people contribute and again to explain transmission. A crowded festival may both increase the sewer population and create new infections, although those infections may not begin shedding immediately. Models therefore need explicit timing assumptions and should avoid treating contemporaneous mobility as a generic correction factor. Even so, a rough dynamic denominator with transparent uncertainty is likely to be less misleading than a census count treated as biological truth.

## A better default, with limits

Moving denominators will not fix irregular shedding, sewer travel time, assay failures, or the absence of sewers in some communities. Nor will the approach be equally feasible everywhere. Transit and occupancy data may be costly, inconsistently defined, or sensitive from a privacy perspective. The case for adoption is consequently strongest in catchments known to experience large, repeated population changes.

Nevertheless, dynamic contributing populations should become the default design for urban wastewater models, even if an initial implementation uses only calendar effects and a biomarker. The fixed denominator is not a neutral simplification: it assigns all unexplained variation to other parts of the epidemic system. By modelling who was plausibly present, analysts can better distinguish demographic movement from epidemiological change and communicate the uncertainty that remains. Wastewater is valuable because it observes populations collectively; its models should acknowledge that the collective itself does not stand still.
