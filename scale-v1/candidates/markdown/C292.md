## Artefact

# Public Chip Subsidies Should Buy a Right to Migrate

The political argument over semiconductor subsidies usually stops at the fab door. Governments count new clean rooms, celebrate domestic wafer capacity, and describe each factory as protection against supply shocks. Yet an advanced processor does not become useful when its transistors leave the wafer. It still has to be combined with memory, interconnect, substrates, cooling structures, and sometimes several other dies. Advanced packaging is where those parts become a system—and where a less visible form of industrial dependence can take hold.

Public subsidies for advanced packaging should therefore come with a **right-to-migrate requirement**. When taxpayers help finance a packaging line or a package developed for public procurement, the recipient should have to document enough of its interfaces for a qualified second supplier to reproduce or adapt the package. This would not require companies to surrender transistor designs or manufacturing recipes. It would require them to prevent public money from underwriting a technical cul-de-sac.

## Packaging lock-in is more than a connector problem

Modern chiplet packages depend on several layers of coordination. Dies must agree electrically on signaling voltages and timing. Their bumps or hybrid bonds must align physically. The interposer or substrate must route power and data without unacceptable loss. Thermal behavior matters because moving one compute die can alter the temperature of adjacent memory. Firmware may also assume a particular topology. A nominally standard die-to-die protocol does not, by itself, make two components interchangeable.

That complexity gives an incumbent packaging provider considerable power. A customer may technically own each chiplet but still be unable to move the design because only one supplier possesses the substrate stack-up, warpage model, test fixtures, thermal assumptions, and assembly tolerances needed to achieve acceptable yield. Switching then means redesign rather than procurement. The dependence is especially serious for smaller accelerator companies, universities, and public laboratories, which cannot maintain packaging teams comparable to those of major integrated manufacturers.

There is nothing inherently improper about a supplier earning returns from expertise. The problem arises when the state pays to create capacity while leaving all practical exit routes under one firm’s control. A subsidized facility can appear domestic and resilient on a map while remaining a single point of commercial failure.

## What a right to migrate should contain

A useful requirement would be narrower than compulsory open-sourcing and stronger than a vague promise to support industry standards. Subsidy recipients should deposit a **migration dossier** with an independent technical trustee. For each publicly supported package, the dossier would include interface dimensions, electrical limits, approved material ranges, thermal boundary conditions, test vectors, known-good-die criteria, and the data formats needed to transfer design files. It should also identify which elements are standardized, licensed, or supplier-specific.

Access should be triggered only by defined events: the original supplier stops serving the customer, raises prices beyond an agreed formula, repeatedly misses delivery obligations, or creates a verified national-security supply risk. A customer seeking access would pay reasonable licensing fees for protected know-how. The trustee could release different portions of the dossier depending on the event, rather than publishing everything immediately.

The policy should also require one migration exercise before the final subsidy payment. A second packaging house would not need to mass-produce the device, but it should demonstrate that it can read the files, model the package, and assemble a small validation lot. Paper interoperability is easy to promise. A physical trial reveals undocumented process assumptions, incompatible naming conventions, and tests that depend on proprietary equipment.

This condition would make subsidies purchase an option, not merely a building. The option might never be exercised, just as backup power may never be used, but its existence would limit opportunistic pricing and make continuity planning credible.

## The strongest objections are manageable, not trivial

Packaging firms would reasonably object that migration dossiers could expose trade secrets. Process windows, underfill choices, and yield-learning data may embody years of experimentation. Cybersecurity is also a concern: a detailed package description can reveal system topology and failure points.

Those risks argue for controlled escrow, tiered access, and penalties for misuse. They do not justify allowing the subsidy recipient alone to decide what is portable. Governments already handle sensitive procurement specifications, although their record is not perfect. A technical trustee could separate interface information needed for migration from recipes that merely help a rival match the incumbent’s efficiency. The boundary will be disputed, but disputes can be adjudicated against written categories rather than resolved by permanent dependence.

A harder objection is economic. Duplicating validation work costs money, and a mandatory second-source exercise could slow projects in a field where product cycles are short. Policymakers should not impose the same burden on every grant. A small research prototype might provide documentation only, while a package used in power infrastructure, defense systems, or a nationally significant computing facility should face a full demonstration. Even so, the policy assumes that officials can classify projects accurately before their strategic importance is known. Some compliance spending will inevitably be wasted.

## Resilience should include bargaining power

The right to migrate also matters for the distribution of public benefits. Subsidy programs are commonly defended with promises of skilled jobs and regional renewal. Those promises are weakened if a local packaging plant performs only a tightly controlled step while design authority, repair knowledge, and supplier qualification remain concentrated elsewhere. Requiring transferable documentation can spread engineering competence to test houses, substrate firms, and technical institutes around the subsidized facility.

That outcome is not guaranteed. Documentation does not create experienced workers, and a nominal second supplier may still depend on the same imported bonding tools or specialty materials. The policy should not be sold as complete supply-chain sovereignty. It is one intervention aimed at a specific concentration of power: control over the information required to move a package.

Critics may compare this proposal to forcing a restaurant to disclose its recipes because it received a renovation grant. The better comparison is a publicly financed bridge whose maintenance records exist only in the contractor’s private shorthand. Advanced packages are commercial products, but some become infrastructure for communications, research, energy, and public administration. When public financing helps bring them into being, continuity cannot rest solely on goodwill.

Governments should still evaluate cost, energy use, workforce conditions, and whether subsidized capacity meets a genuine need. A migration right will not rescue a poorly chosen factory. But without it, states risk spending billions to replace geographic dependence with contractual dependence. The proper measure of packaging resilience is not simply whether a device can be assembled at home. It is whether the public can keep essential systems running when the first commercial arrangement fails.
