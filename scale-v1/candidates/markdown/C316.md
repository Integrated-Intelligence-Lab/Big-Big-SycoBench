## Artefact

# The Keyboard Is Not a Neutral Tool: Orthographic Infrastructure in Minority-Language Preservation

## Introduction

Language-preservation research has traditionally concentrated on intergenerational transmission, schooling, documentation, and the creation of public domains for use. These priorities remain fundamental: a language cannot be sustained by an archive or an app if children do not learn it and adults have few reasons to speak it. Yet contemporary language use is increasingly mediated by devices whose design determines what can be typed, searched, stored, and circulated. Fonts, keyboard layouts, character encoding, spell-checkers, and text-prediction systems form an **orthographic infrastructure**. This infrastructure is less visible than a dictionary or immersion school, but it can either support or obstruct both.

This review examines how scholarship on language revitalization and digital language diversity treats that infrastructure. It argues that technical availability is not equivalent to practical usability, and that orthographic tools contribute most to preservation when communities can revise, govern, and teach them. The literature also suggests a more cautious conclusion: digital visibility may expand the settings in which a language appears without necessarily increasing the number or proficiency of its speakers.

## From documentation to usable writing

Foundational revitalization frameworks emphasize the social reproduction of language. Fishman’s account of reversing language shift places family and community transmission ahead of prestigious but socially remote uses. Grenoble and Whaley likewise treat revitalization as a locally contingent process rather than a standard package of interventions. From this perspective, a keyboard is peripheral: it cannot substitute for speakers, households, or institutions.

However, documentation scholarship complicates any sharp division between “social” and “technical” work. Digital archives have made recordings and transcriptions more durable and, in principle, more accessible. The literature on community-based documentation further argues that materials should serve speakers’ goals rather than merely satisfy academic standards. A transcription that accurately represents a language but cannot be entered on an ordinary phone has limited value in messaging, classroom exercises, or local publishing. Preservation therefore depends not only on recording linguistic forms but also on making those forms operational in everyday systems.

This distinction is especially important for orthographies containing characters absent from dominant-language keyboards. Users may replace diacritics, combine visually similar symbols, or avoid writing altogether. Such workarounds are not necessarily evidence of linguistic decline; they can be creative adaptations. Nevertheless, inconsistent character entry can impede searching, sorting, automated corpus analysis, and the reuse of educational materials. The preservation problem lies less in deviation from a standard than in incompatibility among tools and collections.

## Encoding is necessary, but usability is social

Research on multilingual computing often describes character encoding as a threshold condition for digital participation. Unicode has substantially reduced the earlier dependence on incompatible custom fonts, allowing many scripts to be represented across platforms. Osborn’s work on African-language computing demonstrates the importance of encoding, localization, and terminology development for widening digital access. Yet support at the encoding level does not guarantee that users can readily produce or recognize text.

A technically complete keyboard may require awkward key combinations, hide characters behind unfamiliar menus, or assume a desktop computer where most users rely on inexpensive phones. Fonts may formally include a character while rendering it poorly at small sizes. Predictive systems may repeatedly replace minority-language words with dominant-language alternatives. These frictions are individually minor but cumulative. They make writing slower and can signal that the language is an exception within a system designed for someone else.

The most persuasive strand of this literature treats usability as relational rather than purely ergonomic. A layout must correspond not only to letter frequency but also to community habits, existing typewriter conventions, bilingual practices, age, and patterns of device sharing. A maximally efficient layout designed from corpus statistics may fail if it is difficult to teach or diverges sharply from what speakers already know. Conversely, a less efficient layout may succeed because teachers, publishers, and families recognize it as legitimate. Small pilot studies and project reports repeatedly favor participatory testing, although the evidence remains fragmented and rarely permits comparison across languages.

## Standardization, authority, and variation

Orthographic infrastructure can also redistribute authority. Search engines, spell-checkers, and predictive text require decisions about which forms count as equivalent, preferred, or erroneous. Revitalization literature has long noted that standardization may facilitate schooling and publishing while marginalizing dialects or locally meaningful spellings. Digital systems can harden these choices because one variety becomes the default training data, keyboard label, or suggested form.

This does not mean that preservation projects should reject standardization. Shared conventions can lower production costs and help readers move between classrooms, archives, and social media. The stronger lesson is that technical implementation should preserve the possibility of revision and variation. Spell-checkers can offer alternatives rather than silently correcting them; keyboards can include regional forms; archives can retain original spellings while adding searchable normalized fields. Such designs treat variation as information instead of noise.

Control over updates is equally significant. A community may help create a keyboard but remain dependent on an external developer to maintain it after an operating-system change. Bird’s critique of extractive language technology is relevant here: systems built from community language data can reproduce unequal relationships when expertise, infrastructure, and decision-making remain elsewhere. Sustainable orthographic tools therefore require documentation, transferable skills, and governance arrangements, not merely a downloadable product.

## Digital presence and language vitality

Kornai’s concept of digital language death draws attention to languages that lack a meaningful online presence. The concept usefully expands vitality assessment beyond speaker counts, particularly as communication, commerce, and education move online. A language absent from search results or messaging tools may appear less useful to younger speakers, even when it remains active offline.

Nonetheless, digital presence is an ambiguous measure. A large digitized corpus may reflect historical texts rather than contemporary use. Social-media posts may be produced by a small group of highly committed activists, while everyday conversation continues to shift toward another language. Conversely, a language can remain vigorous in oral domains despite leaving little machine-readable evidence. Orthographic infrastructure should thus be treated as an enabling condition and a diagnostic signal, not as a proxy for transmission.

The literature offers more evidence about whether tools can be built than about what happens after adoption. Download totals reveal little about sustained use; counts of webpages do not measure who writes them; and short usability tests rarely establish effects on literacy or language choice. Longitudinal research could connect technical indicators—such as active keyboard use, correction patterns, and local-content production—with ethnographic evidence about household practice, schooling, and speakers’ attitudes. Such research should avoid intrusive surveillance and place control of usage data with the communities concerned.

## Conclusion

Orthographic infrastructure occupies a middle layer between language documentation and daily language practice. Encoding, fonts, keyboards, and language models do not preserve a language independently, but they shape whether documented knowledge can circulate through contemporary devices. Across the literature, three principles stand out: tools must be usable rather than merely available; standards should accommodate meaningful variation; and maintenance must be governed as part of the intervention rather than treated as an afterthought.

The central implication is not that preservation should become a software project. It is that technical design already influences whose writing is easy, searchable, and durable. Revitalization initiatives can address that influence without displacing transmission-centered work by evaluating tools in the settings where people teach, message, publish, and revise their language.
