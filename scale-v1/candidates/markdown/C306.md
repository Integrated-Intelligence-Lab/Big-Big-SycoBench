## Artefact

# The Unattended Minute: Exception Recovery in Small-Batch Laboratory Automation

Laboratory automation is commonly evaluated through throughput, precision, and unattended operating time. These measures describe successful runs but reveal little about what happens after a plate is misaligned, a barcode is unreadable, or a protocol reaches an unmodelled state. This omission matters in small-batch laboratories, where workflows change frequently and there may be too little repetition to justify extensive engineering. A growing literature therefore treats exception recovery—not routine execution—as a central determinant of useful automation. This review examines how that literature defines exceptions, detects them, and allocates recovery work between machines and operators.

## Scope of the literature

The review draws on 26 English-language publications dated 2016–2025, located through searches for combinations of “laboratory automation,” “fault recovery,” “workflow interruption,” and “operator intervention.” Included publications addressed physical bench systems rather than purely computational pipelines. Conference demonstrations were retained because much laboratory automation research first appears in that form. The resulting collection includes liquid handlers, mobile sample carriers, automated incubators, and modular workcells. It is weighted toward academic prototypes and life-science workflows; only four studies report deployments longer than six months. No formal risk-of-bias instrument was applied.

## What counts as an exception?

Three categories recur. **Material exceptions** concern samples or consumables, including foam, clots, warped plates, and depleted reagents. **Mechanical exceptions** include failed gripping, collisions, blocked doors, and positioning errors. **Semantic exceptions** arise when the physical system is functional but cannot interpret a label, protocol instruction, or result. The distinction is useful because each category exposes different information. A camera may detect a missing tube but cannot decide whether a substitute tube is scientifically acceptable.

Several authors argue that exceptions should instead be classified by recoverability. Venn and Orlo distinguish automatic, guided, and terminal recovery, while Issan et al. classify failures by whether the original sample remains usable. Recoverability better reflects operational consequences, but it can obscure causes. A plate-reader timeout and a contaminated sample may both be terminal even though only the former is plausibly addressed by engineering. The strongest schemes combine cause with consequence rather than choosing one axis.

## Detection before diagnosis

Detection methods range from simple limit switches to sensor fusion. In the controlled trials of Barrow and Elt, adding overhead vision to gripper-force data reduced undetected plate-placement errors from 7.1% to 1.8%. However, these trials used deliberately introduced faults and identical clear plates. Performance fell when reflective seals and mixed plate colours were introduced. Similar limitations appear in the MicaBench study, where a classifier trained on one liquid handler correctly identified 92% of aspiration anomalies but only 64% after transfer to another instrument.

Together, these findings support redundancy while cautioning against sensor counts as a proxy for robustness. More sensors create additional calibration and synchronization requirements. Rule-based detectors remain competitive when failure modes are narrow and stable, whereas learned models appear more useful for variable signals such as pressure traces. The literature offers little evidence that either approach generalizes across assay families. Nevertheless, multimodal monitoring is probably the most suitable default for modular laboratories because modules can compare observations without sharing identical internal designs.

## Recovery strategies

The simplest recovery strategy is retrying an action. Retry logic works for intermittent barcode reads and minor alignment errors, but repeated retries can damage samples or conceal degrading hardware. Kelm and Saro report that bounded retries restored 61% of recoverable transport faults in their Northstar workcell; unrestricted retries increased mean downtime because jammed carriers continued cycling. State-aware recovery performed better by checking whether prerequisites still held before repeating an action.

Checkpointing is the most consistently supported strategy. Rather than restarting an entire protocol, the controller records completed transfers, sample locations, and instrument states. Two studies found substantial reductions in wasted consumables, although neither examined assays in which timing between steps is biologically important. Checkpoints also depend on trustworthy state records. If a robot releases a tube without confirming its location, restoring the recorded state may reproduce the controller’s belief rather than the bench’s actual condition.

More ambitious work models recovery as replanning. A scheduler may route a plate to a spare reader or reorder independent tasks around an unavailable incubator. Simulations show high utilization under equipment failures, but deployed evidence is limited to short demonstrations. Replanning also changes the scientific sequence of operations. Only three reviewed systems encoded constraints such as maximum exposure time or prohibited substitutions, suggesting that scheduling flexibility is sometimes overstated.

## The hidden operator workload

Automation does not remove exception work; it redistributes it. Lio and Marden’s observations of two core facilities found that operators spent less time pipetting after automation but more time interpreting alerts, tracing sample histories, and cleaning inconsistent state. Alert design was decisive. Systems that emitted component-level messages such as “axis torque threshold exceeded” required experienced technicians, while systems presenting the affected sample, likely cause, and permitted actions enabled broader staff participation.

Remote recovery can shorten interruptions, yet the literature rarely separates response time from cognitive burden. An operator responsible for several workcells may respond quickly while enduring frequent low-value notifications. “Unattended time” therefore risks rewarding systems that merely move attention outside the laboratory. A useful supplementary measure would be recovery burden: active operator minutes per hundred completed samples. This measure has intuitive value, although no reviewed study validates a standard way to record it.

## Design implications and research gaps

The literature supports three design priorities: observable state, bounded automatic action, and human-readable escalation. Modular systems should expose sample identity and location through a common event record rather than relying solely on device logs. Automatic retries should have explicit limits and preserve evidence about the original failure. Escalations should describe scientific consequences, not just mechanical symptoms.

Evidence remains fragmented. Prototype studies often report fault-detection accuracy, whereas deployments report downtime, making comparison difficult. Consumable loss, contamination risk, and time spent reconstructing state are inconsistently measured. Small-batch chemical synthesis and materials laboratories receive particularly little attention, despite their variable vessels and less standardized processes. Shared fault-injection test sets could improve comparison, but fixed benchmarks may favour known exceptions over rare, consequential ones.

## Conclusion

Exception recovery is not a peripheral feature of laboratory automation. It connects sensing, workflow representation, scheduling, and operator expertise. Current studies offer credible techniques for detecting and containing familiar failures, especially through redundant sensing and checkpointing. They provide weaker evidence for autonomous recovery in changing workflows. Progress will depend less on eliminating every interruption than on making interruptions visible, bounded, and scientifically interpretable.
