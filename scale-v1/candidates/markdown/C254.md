## Artefact

# Policy Memo: Add a Decomposer Test to Rain-Garden Acceptance

**To:** Commissioner, Gannet Vale Department of Public Works  
**From:** Mara Elling, Green Infrastructure Policy Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Pilot a soil-biological performance test for newly constructed rain gardens

## Decision requested

Approve a one-year pilot requiring a simple buried-wood decomposition test at 24 new municipal rain gardens. The test would supplement—not replace—the department’s current checks for soil depth, infiltration, grading, and plant survival. Staff would use the results to determine whether biological soil repair should be added to project closeout requirements in 2028.

The pilot would cost an estimated **$37,300**. No citywide mandate is recommended until the department has compared decomposition results with infiltration, vegetation survival, and maintenance costs.

## Problem

Gannet Vale accepts new rain gardens primarily on their physical dimensions and short-term hydraulic performance. Inspectors confirm that water enters and drains, specified soil has been installed, and required plants are present. These checks are necessary, but they can miss biologically depleted soil.

Construction can compact soil, separate it from organic surface inputs, and leave a planting medium with few active decomposers. Bacteria, fungi, springtails, mites, and other soil organisms help fragment litter, release nutrients, and create aggregates that maintain pore space. A rain garden may therefore pass its initial infiltration test while remaining dependent on fertilizer, mulch replacement, and repeated replanting.

The department currently has no practical measure of this biological function. Laboratory microbial analyses are expensive and difficult to interpret consistently. Counts of earthworms or other visible fauna are also unsuitable because many healthy rain-garden soils do not support the same organisms, especially during dry periods.

## Proposed test

The department should trial a standardized **decomposer tag**: an untreated, kiln-dried poplar strip buried at a depth of eight centimetres for eight weeks. Each strip would be weighed dry before burial, recovered, gently cleaned, dried again, and reweighed. Percentage mass loss would serve as an indicator of decomposer activity.

Each participating rain garden would receive four tags: two near the inlet and two in the vegetated interior. A sealed reference strip from each manufacturing batch would be retained to detect changes caused by storage or handling. Tags would be installed once between April and June and again between August and October.

For the pilot, staff should classify results as follows:

- **Responsive soil:** average mass loss of 8% or more over eight weeks;
- **Intermediate soil:** average mass loss of 4% to 7.9%;
- **Low-response soil:** average mass loss below 4%, or no recoverable strip showing more than 5% loss.

These thresholds are provisional. They are intended to create consistent pilot categories rather than define healthy soil for every site. Eight percent is a workable initial benchmark based on the expectation that a visibly active soil should alter a thin wood strip within two months; it has not yet been calibrated to Gannet Vale’s soil types.

## Pilot design

The 24 projects should be paired by drainage area, installation season, and planting design. Within each pair, one site would follow current practice and one would receive an “active-soil closeout” package consisting of:

1. loosening the upper 15 centimetres of planting soil without disturbing drainage layers;
2. applying two centimetres of screened, mature compost;
3. retaining a thin layer of leaves after the first autumn maintenance visit; and
4. adding five litres of locally collected leaf mould from an established municipal rain garden.

Leaf mould should be moved only between sites in the same drainage district. Donor material must come from locations without known invasive plants, recent pesticide application, or visible disease. Contractors should record the donor site and transport date.

At installation and after 12 months, inspectors would also measure infiltration, surface crusting, plant survival, and exposed-soil cover. Maintenance crews would record weeding, replanting, and mulch hours. This paired design should help distinguish the effect of the active-soil package from ordinary differences among construction seasons, although variation in shade, sediment loading, and rainfall will remain.

The decomposer score should not be used to reject a project during the pilot. However, contractors would be required to permit tag installation before final payment and correct obvious departures from the assigned treatment, such as omitted compost or heavily compacted surfaces.

## Evaluation and possible adoption

At the end of the pilot, staff should recommend citywide adoption only if treated sites meet all three of the following conditions:

- median tag mass loss is at least 25% higher than at paired control sites;
- plant survival or measured infiltration is better, with neither outcome showing a material decline; and
- added installation and first-year maintenance costs remain below $1,800 per site.

The first condition tests whether the closeout package changes biological activity. The second guards against adopting a biologically active treatment that interferes with the rain garden’s primary drainage purpose. The third limits the policy to a cost comparable with replacing a modest number of failed plants.

The analysis should also report the proportion of missing or damaged tags. If more than one-quarter cannot be recovered, the method is probably too fragile for routine contract administration.

## Cost and staffing

Estimated pilot expenses are:

- tag materials and field supplies: **$864**;
- 192 staff hours for installation, retrieval, and records: **$8,448**;
- drying, weighing, and quality-control support: **$4,000**;
- active-soil treatment allowance: **$18,000**; and
- training and data administration: **$6,000**.

Total estimated cost is **$37,312**, rounded to $37,300. The estimate assumes that field visits can be combined with existing inspections. It does not include costs caused by construction delays or additional screening of donor leaf mould.

## Risks and safeguards

Wood loss is an imperfect proxy. Moisture and temperature strongly affect decomposition, so a wet site may score highly even if its soil community is not diverse. Conversely, a shaded or recently installed garden may score poorly despite developing normally. Pairing sites and repeating the test in two seasons will reduce, but not eliminate, these effects.

Rapid decomposition is also not automatically beneficial. It can indicate fast carbon loss rather than stable aggregation. For this reason, the department should not use tag results alone as evidence of soil health or award contractors for maximizing mass loss.

Moving leaf mould may introduce weed seeds, pathogens, or organisms poorly suited to the receiving site. The drainage-district restriction and donor checklist reduce this risk but do not provide laboratory-level biosecurity. If any donor site later develops a serious soil-borne problem, its material should be excluded and recipient sites flagged for inspection.

Finally, the pilot adds a metric that contractors may treat as another box to check. Training should emphasize that the purpose is to test a low-cost indicator, not to substitute one number for field judgment.

## Recommendation

Approve the $37,300 pilot for the 2027 construction season and direct the Green Infrastructure Policy Unit to return with paired-site results by March 2028. Until those results are available, retain existing acceptance standards and use decomposer scores only for evaluation.
