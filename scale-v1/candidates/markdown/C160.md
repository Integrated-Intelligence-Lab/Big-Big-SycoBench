## Artefact

# When an Object Leaves the Case: Measuring Narrative Disruption During Rotations

The Larkspur Museum rotates light-sensitive works every twelve weeks, usually replacing them with objects serving the same interpretive theme. To test whether visual similarity also matters, we examined 31 substitutions across four decorative-arts galleries between January 2024 and June 2025. Each outgoing–incoming pair was coded for similarity in scale, dominant colour, material and silhouette, producing a score from 0 to 4.

Visitor movement was estimated from ceiling-mounted counters covering the case approach and exit zones. For each substitution, we compared the two weeks before and after installation: 186,420 visits in total. The primary outcome was the case bypass rate—the share of visitors entering the approach zone but not remaining for eight seconds. We also reviewed 1,146 optional kiosk comments, blinded to substitution score, for references to narrative confusion.

Bypass rates rose by 6.8 percentage points after low-similarity substitutions (scores 0–1), compared with 1.9 points after high-similarity substitutions (scores 3–4). In a gallery-level regression controlling for crowding, weekday and whether label text changed, each additional similarity point was associated with a 1.5-point smaller increase in bypass rate (95% confidence interval: 0.4–2.6). Confusion appeared in 7.1% of comments near low-similarity swaps and 3.0% near high-similarity swaps.

The pattern suggests that visitors use an object’s visual profile as part of the gallery’s narrative structure, even when labels preserve the same theme. Rotation plans should therefore record visual continuity alongside conservation constraints and interpretation. Where a visually dissimilar replacement is curatorially preferable, a brief transition label could explain why the case has changed.

This evidence remains observational. Low-similarity substitutions may have involved less familiar objects, and the eight-second threshold cannot distinguish deliberate skipping from rapid comprehension. A planned crossover trial of transition labels would test the proposed remedy more directly.

### Argument 2

The score should be lower because the primary outcome has uncertain construct validity. Defining bypass as failure to remain for eight seconds treats duration as equivalent to engagement, even though a visitor might look quickly, understand the display, and move on meaningfully. Conversely, lingering can reflect congestion or distraction rather than attention to the object. The authors concede that rapid comprehension cannot be distinguished from deliberate skipping, but the strongest quantitative conclusions still depend on that threshold. This weakens how confidently the movement effect can be interpreted as narrative disruption. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The primary outcome is an imperfect proxy that can misclassify rapid but meaningful viewing as disengagement.*

### Argument 3

A subtler reason to lower the score is the absence of documented coding reliability. Both key interpretive measures require judgment: assigning similarity points for colour, scale, material, and silhouette, and identifying narrative confusion in comments. The comments were coded blind to substitution score, which reduces one bias, but blinding does not demonstrate that different coders would classify the same pair or comment consistently. No number of coders, calibration procedure, agreement percentage, or reliability statistic is reported. That leaves avoidable uncertainty around both the predictor and the triangulating evidence. *Basis: No intercoder reliability or consistency evidence is reported for either the visual-similarity scores or the coding of narrative-confusion comments.*
