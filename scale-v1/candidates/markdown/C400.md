## Artefact

# The Porchlight Effect: What Happened After FieldLoom Began Prompting Volunteers at Night

## Executive summary

FieldLoom is a citizen science platform used by residents of six neighboring municipalities to submit photographs and classifications of local wildlife. On 15 June 2025, the platform introduced the **Porchlight Minute** in three municipalities. At 9:30 p.m., eligible users received a notification asking them to spend one minute observing insects or other small animals near an outdoor light. The prompt also appeared as a card on the application home screen and awarded a Glowstreak badge after three nights of participation.

This analysis asks whether the feature increased participation and broadened the kinds of observations contributed to FieldLoom. It compares activity before and after launch in the three rollout municipalities with activity in three municipalities that did not receive the feature.

The main findings are:

- Weekly active observers increased by 35.0% in rollout municipalities, compared with 8.1% in comparison municipalities.
- The proportion of records submitted during the night window rose from 18.2% to 38.7% in rollout municipalities. The equivalent increase in comparison municipalities was from 17.5% to 21.2%.
- New-observer registrations followed by a first contribution increased by 65.1% in rollout municipalities, compared with 12.9% elsewhere.
- The mean number of broad taxonomic categories recorded per observer-week increased from 1.84 to 2.31 in rollout municipalities.
- Thirty-day return among new observers improved by approximately eight percentage points relative to the pre-launch difference.
- Verification performance deteriorated. The recorded verification rate fell from 71.4% to 62.8% in rollout municipalities, while the median time to a first moderator decision increased.

Taken together, the figures indicate that the Porchlight Minute caused a substantial expansion in both nighttime activity and the diversity of participation. The feature appears especially effective at converting low-intensity or dormant accounts into contributors. The increase was not free: moderators received more poorly framed insect photographs, and a larger proportion of records remained unresolved.

FieldLoom should retain the feature and extend it to the other municipalities, while adding basic photography guidance and limiting prompts during periods of heavy moderation demand.

## Platform and feature context

FieldLoom accepts photographs, short audio clips, and manual sighting records. Contributors choose an initial label, such as moth, beetle, spider, bird, plant, or unknown organism. Volunteer moderators can confirm the label, replace it, request more evidence, or mark the record unresolved. Records do not need to reach species level to remain visible.

The six municipalities in this analysis are Alderfen, Merecross, Kestrel Ward, North Brindle, Oakhaven, and Ternwick. The first three received the Porchlight Minute. The latter three continued to receive FieldLoom’s ordinary weekly digest but no nighttime prompt or Glowstreak badge.

The rollout municipalities were selected by FieldLoom’s operations team because they had reliable local coordinators, relatively high historical use of moth labels, and enough opted-in accounts to make the notification worthwhile. The comparison municipalities were selected for geographic proximity and broadly similar population size. They were not placed into treatment or comparison status at random.

The prompt read: **What is visiting your light tonight? Take one minute, look closely, and add what you find.** It was normally sent at 9:30 p.m. local time on Tuesday and Friday. If rain was reported by a coordinator, the coordinator could cancel it. Twelve of the planned 22 prompt nights went ahead in every rollout municipality, seven were canceled in at least one municipality, and three were delayed by more than an hour.

The feature was intended to address a recurring FieldLoom problem. Most existing submissions were concentrated on weekend mornings and were dominated by birds and flowering plants. Small nocturnal organisms were comparatively rare in the database even though moderators believed they were common locally. The Porchlight Minute tried to alter when people observed rather than merely asking existing users to submit more of the same material.

## Data

The analysis uses FieldLoom event logs and record exports covering 1 May through 31 August 2025. The pre-launch period runs from 1 May through 14 June, and the post-launch period runs from 15 June through 31 August. Weekly averages are used where possible because the two periods contain different numbers of days.

The original export contained 56,904 records. The following were excluded:

- 4,180 records imported from automated acoustic sensors;
- 612 records created by staff test accounts;
- 1,034 records without a usable municipality field; and
- 2,466 apparent duplicates with the same account, media hash, and calendar date.

This left 48,612 records from 9,407 accounts. Of these records, 28,441 came from rollout municipalities and 20,171 from comparison municipalities. Account location was assigned using the municipality attached to each record, rather than the address in the account profile. A person contributing in two municipalities could therefore appear in both groups.

An observer was considered active in a week if the account submitted at least one included record. A new observer was an account making its first-ever included FieldLoom contribution. A returning new observer was one that contributed on at least one later calendar day within 30 days of its first contribution.

A night record was defined as a record timestamped between 8:30 p.m. and 2:00 a.m. The window starts before the notification because some users opened the home-screen card before the push was delivered. FieldLoom stores a user-entered observation time when one is supplied. Otherwise it uses the upload time. Eleven percent of records in the study period lacked a separate observation time, so their upload time was used.

Taxonomic breadth was calculated from nine broad categories: birds, mammals, reptiles and amphibians, moths and butterflies, other insects, spiders and related animals, plants, fungi, and other or unknown. The measure is the mean number of categories contributed by an observer in an active week. It does not depend on whether the initial label was later verified.

A record was counted as verified if the export showed either a confirmed or corrected moderator decision. Verification status was extracted on 2 September. The intended outcome was verification within 14 days, but the export did not preserve the exact status as of each record’s fourteenth day. Consequently, records from late August had less opportunity to be verified than records from May or June.

Notification records were available for delivery, opening the prompt, and submitting any record within 12 hours. They did not indicate whether a user actually looked near a porchlight.

## Analytical approach

The main design is a pre-post comparison with a comparison group. For each outcome, the change in the three rollout municipalities is compared with the change in the other three municipalities. For percentage outcomes, the estimated feature effect is expressed as the rollout change in percentage points minus the comparison change in percentage points.

For count outcomes, percentage growth is compared instead. For example, weekly active observers grew by 35.0% in rollout municipalities and by 8.1% in comparison municipalities, producing a reported net increase of 26.9 percentage points. This approach is easy to interpret across outcomes with different starting levels.

The analysis also examines each municipality separately, notification opening behavior, taxonomic composition, and early retention. No regression adjustment was applied because the municipalities appeared reasonably similar at baseline on the main platform measures. Weather, school holidays, local events, and changes in daylight were not incorporated into the estimates.

## Baseline comparison

The two groups had broadly comparable levels of FieldLoom use before launch, although the rollout group was somewhat larger.

| Weekly measure, pre-launch | Rollout municipalities | Comparison municipalities |
|---|---:|---:|
| Active observers | 1,562 | 1,421 |
| Included records | 2,471 | 2,258 |
| Records per active observer | 1.58 | 1.59 |
| New observers | 321 | 295 |
| Night records | 18.2% | 17.5% |
| Verified records | 71.4% | 73.1% |
| Taxonomic categories per observer-week | 1.84 | 1.79 |

The similarities in records per observer, night share, and taxonomic breadth support the use of the comparison municipalities as an indication of what would have happened without the prompt. The higher historical use of moth labels in the rollout municipalities did not produce a large baseline difference in the broad category measure.

## Results

### Participation increased after launch

Weekly active observers increased to 2,108 in rollout municipalities, a gain of 546 observers or 35.0%. Comparison municipalities increased to 1,536 weekly active observers, a gain of 115 or 8.1%.

| Weekly measure | Rollout pre | Rollout post | Change | Comparison pre | Comparison post | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Active observers | 1,562 | 2,108 | +35.0% | 1,421 | 1,536 | +8.1% |
| Included records | 2,471 | 3,870 | +56.6% | 2,258 | 2,526 | +11.9% |
| New observers | 321 | 530 | +65.1% | 295 | 333 | +12.9% |
| Records per observer | 1.58 | 1.84 | +16.5% | 1.59 | 1.64 | +3.1% |

The active-observer result implies a net feature effect of 26.9 percentage points after subtracting comparison-group growth. Record growth was even larger. This suggests that the feature both attracted people to the platform and encouraged each active person to contribute more.

New observers rose faster than total observers. The home-screen prompt was visible to newly created accounts, but most people who had not yet contributed could not receive a push notification because notification consent is requested after the first record. The growth in first contributions therefore probably includes indirect effects, such as participants mentioning the activity to household members or sharing Glowstreak progress in local groups.

### The time profile changed sharply

The night share in rollout municipalities increased by 20.5 percentage points, from 18.2% to 38.7%. Comparison municipalities increased by 3.7 points, from 17.5% to 21.2%. The difference between these changes is 16.8 percentage points.

The increase was concentrated around prompt delivery. In the post-launch period, 22.4% of all records from rollout municipalities were uploaded between 9:30 and 11:30 p.m. on Tuesday or Friday. Before launch, only 7.1% fell in that combination of hours and weekdays. The comparable figures in comparison municipalities were 7.4% before and 8.6% after.

There was no obvious displacement from weekend mornings. Rollout municipalities averaged 812 weekend-morning records per week before launch and 835 afterward. This is important because an evening increase could otherwise represent existing participants moving their usual activity rather than adding observations.

The upload-time substitution for records without observation times could exaggerate this pattern. However, removing the 11% of records without a separate observation time still produced a rollout increase of 18.9 percentage points in night share. The corresponding comparison increase was 3.3 points. The basic result therefore remains.

### Growth occurred in all three rollout municipalities

| Municipality | Feature status | Change in active observers | Change in night share |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Alderfen | Rollout | +46% | +24.6 points |
| Merecross | Rollout | +28% | +15.3 points |
| Kestrel Ward | Rollout | +31% | +10.8 points |
| North Brindle | Comparison | +11% | +4.8 points |
| Oakhaven | Comparison | +7% | +3.1 points |
| Ternwick | Comparison | +6% | +2.5 points |

Alderfen’s effect was the largest. Its coordinator ran two informal identification sessions during the study period, which may have reinforced the prompt. Nevertheless, even the smallest rollout increase was larger than growth in any comparison municipality. The direction of the results is therefore not dependent on a single place.

### Prompt opening was associated with more contribution

The platform attempted 14,820 push deliveries during the post-launch period. Of these, 10,226 were recorded as successfully delivered and 3,681 were opened. Among accounts that opened at least one prompt, 52% submitted a record within 12 hours of an opening. Among accounts receiving but never opening a prompt, 14% submitted during any equivalent 12-hour prompt window.

Users opening four or more prompts averaged 9.2 post-launch records, compared with 4.7 among those opening one to three and 2.1 among delivered non-openers. This dose pattern supports the conclusion that repeated exposure generated additional activity rather than the results being entirely due to seasonal change.

Openers had also been more active before launch: they averaged 3.8 pre-launch records, compared with 1.4 among delivered non-openers. The difference means that opening cannot be treated as random. Even so, the fourfold gap in prompt-window contribution is too large to be explained by the baseline difference alone.

### Contributions became broader, mainly because of insects

Mean taxonomic breadth in rollout municipalities increased from 1.84 to 2.31 categories per observer-week. Comparison municipalities increased from 1.79 to 1.86. Moths and butterflies accounted for 42% of rollout night records after launch. Other insects accounted for 21%, spiders and related animals for 14%, plants for 8%, and all remaining categories for 15%.

The rollout municipalities produced 327 labels described as a first district occurrence during 2025, compared with 89 in the comparison municipalities. Adjusting for record volume reduced the difference but did not remove it: rollout municipalities had 11.5 such labels per 1,000 records, compared with 4.4 per 1,000 in the comparison group.

These labels should not be interpreted as confirmed discoveries. A first district occurrence is generated from the contributor’s initial label, and many remain unresolved. The figure is better understood as an indicator that participants supplied material outside the platform’s usual set of records.

Daytime bird activity was used as a rough negative comparison. Bird records submitted from 8:00 a.m. to noon changed by less than 3% in both groups. If a general increase in enthusiasm or favorable weather explained the entire result, a comparable rise in this established category might be expected. Its stability points toward a feature-specific nighttime effect.

### New-observer return improved

For new observers whose first contribution occurred early enough to allow 30 days of follow-up, 41.8% in rollout municipalities contributed on another day within 30 days. The comparison rate was 34.6%. Before launch, the rates were 32.9% and 33.8%, respectively. The difference in changes is therefore 8.1 percentage points.

The retention calculation includes 2,140 post-launch new observers in rollout municipalities and 1,334 in comparison municipalities. It excludes late entrants without a complete follow-up window. It also counts any later contribution, even if it was uploaded only a few minutes after midnight following the first session. Requiring the return to occur at least seven days later lowered post-launch retention to 27.3% in the rollout group and 23.1% in the comparison group, leaving a smaller but still positive difference.

This supports the idea that a simple, time-bounded task gives first-time users a reason to come back. The Glowstreak badge may contribute because it requires activity on three separate nights.

### Verification performance worsened

The verification rate shown in the export fell from 71.4% to 62.8% in rollout municipalities. Comparison municipalities declined only from 73.1% to 70.9%. Median time from upload to first moderator decision increased from 3.2 days to 5.8 days in rollout municipalities.

The decline was concentrated in moth, other insect, and unknown-organism records. Moderators frequently marked images as unresolved because they were blurred, overexposed by a light, or lacked a view of distinguishing features. Records from participants who opened four or more prompts were verified at 68.5%, compared with 57.2% for one-time prompt participants. Practice may therefore improve submission quality.

Late-August records had little time to acquire a verification decision before the 2 September export. Excluding the final two weeks raised the rollout post-launch verification rate from 62.8% to 65.7%. This does not eliminate the decline, though it shows that part of the apparent moderation problem comes from incomplete follow-up.

## Interpretation

The strongest evidence for a Porchlight Minute effect is the combination of timing, location, and outcome specificity. Activity rose most in the municipalities receiving the prompt, records clustered in the hours immediately after delivery, and the largest category increases were the small nighttime organisms named by the task. All three rollout municipalities moved in the same direction, while daytime bird activity remained almost flat.

The feature appears to have done more than shift uploads into the night. Total records, active observers, first contributors, and later returns all increased. This pattern suggests that the prompt reduced the effort required to decide what to observe. A participant did not need to plan a trip, recognize a rare species, or wait for a weekend. The task supplied a location, a duration, and a subject.

A seasonal explanation is possible because the launch occurred in mid-June, when insect activity and evening outdoor activity generally increase. The comparison municipalities also experienced summer and showed only modest gains. It is unlikely that seasonality accounts for the additional 26.9 percentage points of active-observer growth in rollout locations.

The poorer verification result is a meaningful cost but not a reason to withdraw the feature. Broad participation and immediate species-level accuracy are different platform goals. An unresolved image may still introduce a participant to observation practice. In addition, repeated prompt openers produced better records than one-time participants, suggesting that quality can rise with experience.

## Operational cost estimate

FieldLoom moderators processed an average of 34 records per paid hour during May. Using a fully loaded moderation cost of 24 platform credits per hour, an additional 1,105 weekly records above the comparison-group growth trend would require approximately 32.5 hours, or 780 credits per week, if every record required the same handling.

In practice, many records receive no paid review because volunteer moderators act first. Paid staff reported spending 9.1 additional hours per week on the rollout municipalities, implying a direct cost closer to 218 credits per week. Dividing this by the estimated 519 additional active observers gives a weekly cost of 0.42 credits per added active observer.

This calculation excludes feature design, coordinator time, notification infrastructure, and the value of volunteer labor. It also assumes the comparison-group growth rate represents the correct no-feature path. Even with those exclusions, the result suggests the prompt is inexpensive relative to field events that require staff travel.

## Limitations

The municipalities were not randomly assigned. Rollout locations were selected partly because of reliable coordinators and existing moth interest. Those characteristics could independently produce stronger summer growth. Baseline platform measures were similar, but similar averages do not establish that the groups would have followed parallel trends after June.

Only three municipalities received the feature. User-level totals make the dataset appear large, but the rollout decision occurred at the municipality level. The estimates do not account for correlation among people exposed to the same coordinator, weather, or local publicity, and no confidence intervals are reported.

Weather was not available in the export. Canceled prompts provide some protection against the wettest nights, but ordinary differences in temperature, wind, and cloud cover could affect both insect visibility and willingness to participate. Daylight also changed during the analysis period, while the night window remained fixed by the clock.

The night measure combines observation time and upload time. The sensitivity check excluding upload-only records gives a similar result, although excluding them may selectively remove participants with weak connectivity or different application habits.

Taxonomic breadth relies on initial contributor labels. The prompt’s interface placed moth, beetle, and spider buttons at the top of the label menu, so part of the measured increase may reflect interface selection rather than genuinely broader observation. First district occurrences are especially vulnerable to mistakes and are not confirmed range records.

Verification data are right-censored because status was extracted only two days after the end of August. The exclusion of the final two weeks reduces but does not solve this problem, since moderator delays changed during the rollout.

Finally, retention captures any second-day contribution, not sustained participation over several months. The study period is too short to show whether Porchlight Minute participants become durable contributors or disappear when the badge is complete.

## Recommendations

FieldLoom should expand the Porchlight Minute to North Brindle, Oakhaven, and Ternwick for an eight-week trial. The expansion should be staggered by two weeks across municipalities so that the operations team can compare each location with places not yet activated. Random assignment of eligible accounts within municipalities would provide an even stronger test, although it may disappoint users who see neighbors receiving the badge.

Before expansion, the prompt should include three short instructions: steady the device against a fixed surface, avoid pointing directly into the light, and photograph both the upper and lower side of an insect when possible. A sample preview can show the desired framing without requiring participants to identify the organism.

The platform should pause prompts when the unresolved moderation queue exceeds 4,000 records and recruit a rotating insect-review group before midsummer. The badge should remain because the retention results suggest that repeated participation is more valuable than a single response.

Future reporting should preserve the status of each record exactly 14 days after upload, distinguish capture time from sync time, and measure retention at 30, 90, and 180 days. Weather and coordinator events should also be recorded.

On the available evidence, the Porchlight Minute achieved its central purpose: it brought more people onto FieldLoom at an underrepresented time and generated observations outside the platform’s usual daytime mix. The moderation burden is manageable and can be reduced through guidance. A wider rollout is justified, provided it is accompanied by better measurement.
