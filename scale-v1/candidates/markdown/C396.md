## Artefact

# The Laboratory That Waits: Temporal Slack in Automated Experimental Workflows

## Abstract

Laboratory automation is usually evaluated through throughput, precision, and the proportion of operations performed without human intervention. Less attention is paid to waiting: idle robot arms, queued plates, stabilization periods, delayed human approvals, and instruments reserved for work that has not yet arrived. This review examines waiting as a feature of automated laboratories rather than simply as unused capacity. The literature can be divided into four broad accounts of waiting: waste to be removed, scientifically required dwell time, protective slack, and evidence of poor organizational coordination. Across these accounts, authors disagree about whether high equipment utilization indicates successful automation. Scheduling studies generally seek to compress idle periods, whereas reliability studies suggest that spare capacity can contain failures. Human-factors research adds that some machine waiting merely transfers urgency to technicians. The review argues that temporal slack should be measured separately from accidental delay, although the available literature rarely makes that distinction consistently. A provisional taxonomy is offered, followed by recommendations for designing automated workflows with visible queues, bounded recovery time, and limited spare capacity. The evidence remains fragmented, particularly for long-running biology workflows, but it indicates that the fastest automated laboratory is not necessarily the most productive or dependable one.

## 1. Introduction

The familiar image of laboratory automation is continuous motion. Plates travel between readers and incubators, liquid handlers dispense without interruption, and scheduling software fills every opening in an instrument calendar. Vendors and laboratory managers often describe this condition using the language of utilization: an expensive device creates value when it is operating and loses value when it is idle. This reasoning has encouraged laboratories to treat waiting as a defect.

Yet automated laboratories wait constantly. A sample may need thirty minutes for a reaction to develop. A robotic gripper may be held outside an enclosure while a centrifuge completes its cycle. A batch may remain in a queue because a scientist has not approved an anomalous control result. Instruments are also left deliberately unbooked so that failed runs can be repeated. These pauses are not equivalent, even though a scheduling dashboard may display each of them as an unproductive block of time.

The distinction matters because removing one kind of waiting can create another. If plates are released toward an analyzer as soon as preparation ends, they may accumulate in an uncontrolled queue beside the analyzer. If every incubator is kept full, a delayed plate may have nowhere to go. If software automatically proceeds through every decision point, scientists may discover a systematic problem only after large amounts of reagent and material have been consumed. Faster local operation can therefore produce slower or less reliable laboratory performance.

This review considers the role of temporal slack in automated experimental workflows. Temporal slack is defined here as time or capacity not assigned to immediate productive motion. The definition includes empty scheduling intervals, planned buffers, scientific dwell periods, and some forms of operator delay. This breadth makes it possible to connect literatures that are usually discussed separately, although it also risks treating unlike phenomena as parts of one category.

The central question is whether waiting should be minimized, tolerated, or designed. The literature does not provide one answer. Optimization research generally treats idle time as a cost, process-control research recognizes waiting as a physical constraint, and resilience research treats slack as an insurance mechanism. Human-factors studies further suggest that the distribution of waiting between machines and people affects fatigue and error. Bringing these positions together exposes a basic tension: laboratories want automated systems that are both highly occupied and immediately available when an exception occurs.

## 2. Scope of the Review

The review draws on publications concerning robotic laboratories, instrument scheduling, workflow orchestration, manufacturing cells, clinical sample processing, and human supervision of automation. Searches were conducted in several technical and scientific databases using combinations of terms such as “laboratory automation,” “idle time,” “scheduling,” “queue,” “incubation,” “workflow delay,” and “robot recovery.” References cited by relevant papers were also inspected. Studies were favored when they described a working system rather than only a scheduling algorithm, though several simulation papers were retained because little operational evidence was available.

Approximately two dozen sources formed the main body of the review. The literature was read narratively and grouped according to the function authors assigned to waiting. No formal quality score was applied because engineering case reports and observational laboratory studies were too different for a common scale. Manufacturing studies were included where the equipment arrangement resembled a laboratory workcell. Clinical laboratories were included selectively, particularly when they addressed sample queues or urgent insertions.

The resulting evidence base is uneven. Most reports concern short plate-based workflows or simulated jobs, while fewer examine experiments lasting days. Publications also tend to describe successful installations, and vendors rarely report how often automation is stopped, bypassed, or repaired. Nevertheless, the sources provide several recurring interpretations of temporal slack.

## 3. Waiting as Waste

The strongest tradition treats waiting as one of the principal losses that automation should remove. In this view, idle time indicates poor sequencing, inadequate equipment balancing, or failure to anticipate resource contention. Havel and Sorn’s simulation of the fictional Lattice-12 screening cell, for example, compared first-in-first-out scheduling with a priority rule based on predicted operation length. Their priority scheduler reduced average plate completion time by 17 percent, largely by preventing short jobs from becoming trapped behind long incubation-heavy jobs. The robot arm also spent less time waiting for destination instruments to become available.

Similar conclusions appear in reports on modular chemistry platforms. Neris and Volan described a synthesis system in which vessels repeatedly waited at a shared weighing station. Adding a second station raised daily output, but changing the order of weighing and solvent addition produced nearly the same gain without new hardware. The authors presented the queue as proof that the original workflow had been badly arranged.

These studies make an important contribution by showing that nominal instrument speed is not the same as system throughput. A pipetting device can operate faster while total run time remains unchanged because downstream readers or centrifuges determine the pace. Queue maps and event logs can reveal these constraints more reliably than raw utilization percentages. The literature also demonstrates that small scheduling choices may have larger effects than purchasing a faster robot.

However, the waste account tends to assume that every job has a stable duration and that the objective is known in advance. Real experiments often violate both assumptions. A liquid handler may pause because a tip cannot be detected, an imaging run may expand when additional fields are requested, and a control result may require interpretation. Optimization papers frequently model such events as random delays rather than scientifically meaningful decisions. Their solutions are therefore strongest for repetitive assays and less obviously applicable to research environments.

The emphasis on mean completion time can also obscure extreme cases. A rule that improves average throughput may leave a small number of temperature-sensitive samples waiting too long. In some studies, a reduction in robot idle time is reported as an improvement even when sample residence time is not measured. The literature thus occasionally treats machine motion as the product of the laboratory rather than validated experimental output.

## 4. Scientific Dwell Time

A second body of work concerns waiting that is required by the experiment. Incubation, settling, thermal equilibration, reaction development, instrument calibration, and washing all impose intervals during which visible progress may be limited. Such intervals are sometimes called “hard waits,” though the term is misleading because many are permitted ranges rather than exact durations.

Bellane’s review of automated immunoassay workcells distinguishes exact-time operations from minimum-time operations. An exact-time operation must occur near a specified moment, while a minimum-time operation may occur at any later time after a threshold. This distinction has scheduling consequences. A plate requiring at least twenty minutes of incubation can remain in place for twenty-five minutes if the assay tolerates it, whereas a plate that must be read within a two-minute window cannot safely join an ordinary queue.

Laboratory schedulers often represent both conditions as fixed durations. This simplifies software but may waste flexibility. In the Orison Enzyme Laboratory case described by Quill, expanding incubation requirements from single values to validated windows allowed the scheduler to interleave three assay families. Daily capacity reportedly increased from 38 to 51 plates without changes to the hardware. The result suggests that scientific protocols contain hidden temporal options that automation engineers can exploit.

At the same time, the literature sometimes implies that any protocol interval can be negotiated once enough data are collected. That is too broad. Some timing requirements arise from unstable chemistry, cell stress, or regulatory validation and cannot be altered merely because a scheduler would benefit. The Orison case involved relatively forgiving colorimetric assays, but it has been cited in discussions of cell culture and analytical chemistry where timing risks are different. Generalizing from it may encourage laboratories to widen windows without sufficient scientific validation.

There is also disagreement about whether scientific dwell time should count as equipment utilization. An incubator containing plates is clearly performing a function despite having no moving parts. A robot arm waiting beside it is not. Some dashboards count both devices as occupied because they are reserved for the workflow; others count only active operations. This inconsistency makes comparisons between laboratories unreliable. A system claiming 90 percent utilization may be reporting reservation, physical activity, or successful sample processing.

The most useful contribution of this literature is the recognition that workflow timing has several dimensions: minimum duration, maximum duration, permissible interruption, and sensitivity to variation. Yet few studies record all four. A basic operation is usually stored as a single number, leaving the scheduler unable to distinguish a harmless five-minute delay from one that invalidates a sample.

## 5. Slack as Protection Against Failure

Reliability studies reverse the usual interpretation of idle capacity. Instead of asking how to keep every instrument busy, they ask what happens when a busy instrument fails. A tightly scheduled laboratory may have no location for interrupted work, no opportunity to repeat a control, and no alternative path around an unavailable device. Under these conditions, high utilization can make a local fault propagate across the workcell.

Daro and Melsin examined fault logs from the invented MorrowBench facility, where six automated stations shared two transport robots. They found that stoppages lasting less than eight minutes caused most of the lost shift time. The delay came not from the original fault but from clearing plates that had continued arriving during recovery. Their proposed policy inserted ten-minute empty blocks every two hours. Simulations suggested lower nominal throughput but higher completed throughput once faults were included.

This finding aligns with research on manufacturing buffers, where small reserves can separate one machine’s variability from another. The analogy is appealing: a plate hotel resembles an intermediate storage buffer, and duplicate readers resemble redundant production machines. However, biological samples are not inert components. A plate hotel may protect the schedule while exposing cells or reagents to unacceptable temperature, humidity, or evaporation. Buffer capacity is therefore not automatically useful capacity.

Several authors recommend “recovery slack,” meaning time and space reserved for predictable categories of exception. Examples include an empty incubator position, an unassigned robot slot, spare consumables, and a scheduled period for reruns. This is more specific than simply lowering utilization. Recovery slack is linked to an action: holding a delayed sample, replacing a failed batch, or allowing an operator to inspect a deck.

Evidence for the ideal amount of slack is weak. MorrowBench’s ten-minute blocks were chosen from its local fault pattern, while other papers propose reserves of 5 to 20 percent with limited justification. The literature frequently repeats the idea that systems should run at about 80 percent utilization, apparently because queues rise rapidly near full capacity. That principle is plausible but not universal. A rarely failing reader with a large downstream buffer may safely operate near full occupancy, whereas a fragile transport robot could destabilize the laboratory at a much lower figure.

Some reliability papers also count unused duplicate equipment as slack even where the duplicate is required for routine throughput. This inflates the appearance of resilience. True redundancy exists only if an alternative resource remains available when the primary resource fails. Two instruments that are both continuously booked do not provide much practical redundancy, even if either can technically perform the same assay.

## 6. Human Waiting and Transferred Urgency

Automated workflows include human decisions at loading, approval, replenishment, exception handling, and data review. Waiting can therefore be shifted rather than eliminated. A machine may operate continuously because a technician is expected to respond immediately whenever a consumable runs low. Conversely, a machine may remain idle because approvals are performed in scheduled batches.

In a study of the fictional North Aster Sample Centre, Pellen and Uro observed technicians supervising three automated preparation lines. The lines generated frequent low-priority notices mixed with occasional urgent alarms. Technicians responded quickly to almost all alerts because the interface did not clearly show whether a delay would damage a sample. As a result, staff repeatedly abandoned manual tasks to check machines that could have waited. The automation reduced direct pipetting but increased fragmented attention.

This work provides a useful correction to equipment-centered studies. A scheduler that reduces robot waiting by demanding instant human intervention may improve its own performance while worsening laboratory performance. Interruptions can increase setup errors, delay documentation, and make work harder to plan. The relevant question is not simply whether the robot waits, but who waits, under what pressure, and with what consequence.

Human-factors authors commonly recommend escalation windows. Instead of issuing an immediate alarm, the system should state that a response is required within a defined period, such as “refill tips within 18 minutes.” This converts an interruption into a schedulable task. Some interfaces also group non-urgent interventions into service rounds. These approaches appear sensible, although evidence is mainly observational and based on staff preference rather than measured error rates.

The literature occasionally assumes that human waiting is inherently more costly than machine waiting because labor is flexible and expensive. This is not always true. A specialist may use a waiting period to review data or prepare another protocol, whereas an idle dedicated analyzer may have no alternate use. Cost depends on whether waiting prevents other productive activity, not merely on whether a person or device is stationary.

There is also a risk of romanticizing human judgment. Several case reports describe operators as a flexible buffer who can rescue irregular workflows. In practice, this may mean that incomplete automation is sustained through unrecorded manual work. If technicians regularly rearrange queues, carry plates past a failed transporter, or reinterpret ambiguous alerts, the system may appear more autonomous and robust than it is. Waiting records should therefore include human interventions, but most automation logs track only machine states.

## 7. Organizational Waiting

A final category lies outside the physical workcell. Automated runs wait for protocol approval, reagent release, sample registration, data transfer, or access to shared instruments. These delays can exceed the time saved by faster liquid handling, yet they receive less attention because they belong to different software and management systems.

Sivert’s study of the Veyra Materials Unit found that a robot completed formulation batches in four hours, but batches spent a median of nineteen hours awaiting analytical review. The unit initially planned to buy a second formulation robot. After mapping the end-to-end process, it introduced two review periods per day and standardized the data packet sent to analysts. Turnaround reportedly fell more than it would have under the equipment expansion scenario.

This case illustrates the value of measuring the entire workflow. Automation can accelerate one stage while leaving the governing bottleneck elsewhere. It also shows why utilization is an incomplete management measure: the formulation robot was busy, but completed knowledge moved slowly.

Nonetheless, organizational waiting is only loosely connected to the scheduling literature reviewed above. A delay caused by an approval meeting differs from a plate waiting for an available reader. The remedies involve staffing, authority, and information design rather than robotic coordination. Combining them under temporal slack may create a broad vocabulary at the expense of causal precision.

Several publications propose digital integration as the general solution. If the laboratory information system, scheduler, and analysis pipeline exchange status automatically, approvals can supposedly occur earlier and queues become visible. Integration may help, but visibility does not itself add reviewing capacity or resolve unclear responsibility. The literature often overstates what software connectivity can accomplish in organizations where decisions remain contested or under-resourced.

## 8. Toward a Taxonomy of Laboratory Waiting

The reviewed literature supports a provisional classification with five categories:

1. **Constraint waiting** arises from scientific or physical requirements, such as incubation or thermal equilibration.
2. **Contention waiting** occurs when multiple jobs need the same robot, instrument, or storage position.
3. **Recovery waiting** is time reserved or consumed while faults are diagnosed and work is restored.
4. **Decision waiting** occurs when a human or analytical system must authorize the next operation.
5. **Administrative waiting** reflects registration, release, documentation, or organizational coordination.

This taxonomy is useful because the categories suggest different responses. Constraint waiting should be scientifically characterized, contention waiting may be reduced by scheduling, recovery waiting requires fault planning, decision waiting benefits from explicit response windows, and administrative waiting may require process redesign.

However, the categories overlap. A plate awaiting review might occupy an incubator and thereby cause contention. A scientific constraint may become administrative when a validated timing window cannot be changed without formal approval. Recovery from a fault may require a human decision. It is therefore difficult to assign every delay to a single class. A better logging system might record both the immediate state and the underlying cause, though no reviewed system did so comprehensively.

The taxonomy also does not settle which waits are beneficial. Constraint waiting is necessary but not necessarily slack, while recovery slack may remain unused for long periods. Administrative waiting is usually undesirable, yet a review pause may prevent continuation of a faulty experiment. The categories clarify mechanisms more than value.

## 9. Discussion

Three conclusions recur across the literature. First, equipment utilization should not be used alone to judge laboratory automation. It can reward systems that keep devices occupied while increasing queues, sample risk, or operator interruption. Measures such as valid outputs per day, time within protocol windows, recovery duration, and human intervention load provide a broader picture.

Second, waiting should be represented explicitly in workflow models. Protocols need more than a sequence of active steps. They should identify timing ranges, holding conditions, interruptibility, decision deadlines, and acceptable recovery paths. Without these properties, a scheduler cannot distinguish useful flexibility from dangerous delay.

Third, some spare capacity appears necessary for resilience. The literature supports reserving at least a modest amount of time or storage for reruns and disruptions. A general target of 10 to 20 percent unassigned capacity seems reasonable across many laboratories, especially where workflows are variable. This recommendation should be interpreted cautiously because the studies do not directly compare reserve levels across different laboratory types. Even so, operating every critical resource at maximum occupancy is unlikely to be safe.

These conclusions are limited by the evidence. Many studies are simulations, single-site reports, or descriptions written during system deployment. Few provide raw event logs, and definitions of idle time differ. Research laboratories, diagnostic facilities, and manufacturing cells are often discussed together despite having different objectives. The literature also contains little information about failed automation projects, making it difficult to estimate whether slack prevents failure or merely accompanies well-funded systems.

More empirical work should compare laboratories before and after changes to scheduling slack. Such studies should report completed valid experiments, sample losses, staff interruptions, energy use, and recovery time rather than only robot utilization. Long-running workflows deserve particular attention because a short interruption can interact with multi-day biological processes in ways not captured by plate-handling simulations.

Despite these gaps, managers can take several immediate steps. They can separate active processing from reservation in equipment dashboards, record why a sample is waiting, validate timing windows instead of using single durations, and identify where interrupted samples can be held. Alert interfaces should display response deadlines. Finally, capacity planning should include ordinary faults rather than calculating output from perfect cycle times.

## 10. Conclusion

Waiting in automated laboratories is neither a single form of waste nor an uncomplicated source of safety. Some waiting is imposed by science, some results from contention, some preserves room for recovery, and some reveals organizational delay. The literature is strongest when it maps queues and timing constraints at the level of the whole workflow. It is weakest when it assumes that greater motion means greater productivity or, conversely, that an arbitrary reserve guarantees resilience.

Laboratory automation should therefore be designed around controlled waiting. Pauses should have known causes, acceptable durations, and defined consequences. A laboratory that can explain why its machines are idle may be better automated than one in which every instrument is busy and no resource is available when the experiment departs from plan.
