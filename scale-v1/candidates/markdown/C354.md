## Artefact

# Procurement Policy for Sign-Language Access at Alderwick Service Kiosks

**To:** Director, Civic Service Delivery  
**From:** Digital Access Policy Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Adopt a tiered standard for signed interaction rather than a universal signing avatar

## Recommendation

Alderwick should require every new public-service kiosk to provide a clearly marked route to sign-language access, but it should not require one technical method for every transaction. Procurement should instead divide kiosk interactions into three tiers:

1. **Fixed public information:** human-recorded signed video, reviewed by local signers.
2. **Short, predictable transactions:** a constrained signing interface may be used, provided users can inspect and correct the kiosk’s interpretation before submission.
3. **Open-ended or consequential transactions:** immediate connection to a qualified remote human interpreter or signing service agent.

The city should prohibit autonomous signing avatars from handling open-ended explanations, eligibility decisions, complaints, or consent. Vendors must disclose the exact “interaction envelope” within which automated signing has been tested. This approach directs automation toward tasks it can perform consistently while preserving human support where errors carry greater cost.

## Why a single interface requirement would fail

A procurement rule stating only that a kiosk must “support sign language” is too vague. It allows a vendor to satisfy the requirement with a small library of generic animated clips, even when those clips do not match the language, regional usage, pace, or information needs of Alderwick residents.

It also collapses two different functions. **Receptive access** means presenting information in sign language. **Expressive access** means allowing a person to ask or answer in sign language. The first can often be met with carefully produced recordings. The second may require camera input, turn-taking, clarification, and interpretation of spatial grammar. A system that signs outward competently does not necessarily understand signing inward.

The distinction matters most at kiosks for housing appointments, permit disputes, transit refunds, and identity-document replacement. A mistranslated opening time is inconvenient; a mistranslated declaration or consent step may change the outcome of a case.

## Evidence from the Lantern kiosk pilot

The Digital Access Policy Unit tested three prototype workflows with 24 signing residents across 180 tasks. Participants included daily and occasional kiosk users, with ages ranging from 19 to 71. The pilot was intended to identify failure patterns, not to certify a product.

Human-recorded clips performed best for stable content such as office directions, required-document lists, and queue instructions. Participants frequently replayed individual steps, supporting the inclusion of pause, speed, and chapter controls.

The automated avatar was generally understood when presenting a restricted set of short prompts, but comprehension fell when prompts contained administrative terminology or several conditions. Participants also reported that an avatar could appear confident even when its output was awkward or ambiguous. Adding a visible “show another version” control helped, but did not resolve uncertainty about which version was authoritative.

Camera-based recognition was adequate for selecting among a small number of anticipated answers. It was unreliable for names, dates, corrections, and unanticipated questions. Performance also varied with framing, sleeves, background movement, and whether a signer stood or sat. In two consequential test scenarios, the system converted a correction into confirmation. Those results support a narrow role for recognition rather than a general signed conversation interface.

## Proposed procurement standard

### 1. Require a signed-access entry point

The kiosk home screen must display a persistent, language-neutral access symbol. Selecting it must open an access menu without requiring text literacy. The menu should offer signed video, text, audio, and human assistance; no option should be described as the default or “normal” route.

### 2. Define the permitted interaction envelope

For every automated signing function, the vendor must list:

- supported tasks and signed languages;
- whether the system presents, recognizes, or translates signing;
- environmental conditions used in testing;
- known unsupported inputs;
- error rates by task, not merely an overall accuracy figure; and
- the route available when confidence is low.

The contract should treat use outside this envelope as a defect. Vendors may not describe a constrained command recognizer as a conversational interpreter.

### 3. Match the interface to consequence

Automated recognition may submit low-consequence selections, such as choosing a destination or requesting a queue ticket. Before submission, the kiosk must present its interpretation back to the user in sign and in a second selectable format. Silence, hesitation, or low-confidence recognition must never be treated as consent.

Transactions affecting money, eligibility, identity, deadlines, or formal complaints must offer a human signed interaction before any binding response is recorded. The handoff should carry the transaction stage and menu selections, but not camera footage.

### 4. Make local review continuous

The city should establish a paid Sign Interface Panel of eight to twelve residents serving staggered one-year terms. The panel would review new clips, terminology changes, pacing, camera placement, and failure reports. At least two panel members should review each major content release, with disagreement triggering a broader review rather than a simple majority choice.

Signed content should be versioned alongside the underlying service policy. When a form, deadline, or eligibility rule changes, the associated signed content must be flagged automatically for review. This avoids the common failure in which accessible material remains visible after the source information has changed.

### 5. Apply privacy by default

Camera input should begin only after an explicit signed prompt and visible countdown. Recognition should occur on the kiosk where technically feasible. Raw video must not be retained by default, used to train vendor systems, or attached to a resident’s case file. For human assistance, the interface must explain that live video will be transmitted and provide a private-screen mode that reduces visibility to bystanders.

## Implementation and measurement

The city should apply the standard first to the twelve kiosks scheduled for replacement in the Civic Hall and North Quay service centres. A six-month rollout budget of **420,000 alder crowns** should cover content production, kiosk cameras and privacy screens, integration, panel compensation, and remote-support capacity. Procurement should require separate prices for each component so the city can replace a recognition vendor without reproducing all signed content.

Success should be measured by task completion, requests for clarification, abandoned sessions, incorrect submissions caught before confirmation, handoff wait time, and user-rated confidence. Results should be reported separately for fixed information, constrained transactions, and open-ended cases. The city should suspend automated submission for any task that produces two uncaught consequential errors during the first six months.

## Risks and mitigations

Remote human assistance may be unavailable during network outages or periods of high demand. Kiosks should therefore show current wait times and permit users to reserve a signed video appointment without restarting the transaction. Fixed signed guidance must remain available offline.

The Lantern pilot was too small to establish performance across all signing styles or physical settings. The initial deployment should consequently be treated as monitored service introduction, with quarterly panel review and authority to narrow—not only expand—the automated interaction envelope.

## Decision requested

Approve the tiered standard for the forthcoming kiosk tender, authorize the six-month deployment budget, and require Digital Access Policy to return after two quarters with disaggregated performance results and any recommended changes to the permitted uses of automated signing.
