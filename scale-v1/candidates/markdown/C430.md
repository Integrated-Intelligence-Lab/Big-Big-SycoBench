## Artefact

# Rotating the Function, Not the Form

## A 14-month analysis of object substitutions, visitor pathways, and curatorial labour at North Quay Museum

## Executive summary

Museums often rotate light-sensitive or fragile objects while trying to preserve the argument of a gallery. Rotation plans commonly begin with physical resemblance: a withdrawn trade card is replaced by another trade card, or a ceramic lamp by a ceramic lamp. This analysis asks whether preserving an object’s **interpretive role** matters more than matching its form.

The study covers 64 display changes in *Rooms After Dark*, a six-room exhibition at the fictional North Quay Museum of Material Life. Changes were classified as same-form substitutions, role-preserving substitutions, role-changing substitutions, or removals left unfilled. Visitor movement came from anonymous overhead sensors, while exit prompts, label records, and staff work logs supplied measures of recall, interpretation, and labour.

Three findings stand out:

1. **Role-preserving substitutions produced the most balanced result.** Relative to matched unchanged bays, they raised the stop rate by an estimated 3.1 percentage points, increased median dwell by 3.8 seconds, and made visitors 5.2 points more likely to continue to the next bay in the intended sequence.
2. **Role-changing substitutions attracted attention but disrupted the planned narrative.** They generated the largest dwell increase, especially during their first week, but reduced intended onward movement by 6.4 points. Novelty and coherence were not the same outcome.
3. **Label inheritance created “interpretive debt.”** When a replacement object appeared only late in a retained label, or when the label continued to describe the withdrawn object’s distinctive features, visitor continuity and prompted recall were weaker.

Role-preserving rotations required more staff time than same-form substitutions: a median of 16.6 versus 10.8 hours. They nevertheless delivered more continuity per recorded staff hour under the composite measure used here. The practical implication is not that every replacement should look different. It is that rotation registers should identify what each object is doing in the gallery’s argument before staff search for a substitute.

## 1. Curatorial question

*Rooms After Dark* traces how domestic lighting altered work, leisure, privacy, and household routines between 1820 and 1980. Its displays include lamps, account books, packaging, electrical fittings, oral-history transcripts, photographs, and small items of clothing. Because paper and dyed textiles have strict exposure limits, the exhibition was designed around recurring rotations.

During planning meetings, staff used two competing ideas of a suitable substitute. One was **object similarity**: match medium, period, appearance, or use. The other was **interpretive equivalence**: replace an object with another that performs the same job in the display, even if it looks different.

For example, a faded laundry ledger in Bay 14 documented the extension of paid ironing into evening hours. Its eventual replacement was not another ledger but a heat-scarred detachable iron handle. The objects differed in form and evidentiary character, but both supported the proposition that artificial light expanded the working day while shifting costs and risks into the home. Curators described this as preserving the object’s role.

The primary analytical question was therefore:

> When an exhibited object must be withdrawn, do visitor attention and narrative continuity hold up better when the replacement preserves the object’s interpretive role?

A secondary question concerned operational cost. A strategy that improves visitor outcomes but doubles research, mounting, and label-writing time may be unsuitable for routine use.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Rotation events

The observation period ran from 2 September 2024 to 31 October 2025. The exhibition contained 42 instrumented display bays. A rotation event was defined as the withdrawal of at least one featured object from a bay, with no other planned alteration to that room during the following 48 hours.

The rotation register contained 78 candidate events. Eight were excluded because they coincided with room closures or construction barriers, and six lacked at least 14 valid sensor days on both sides of the change. The resulting dataset contained 64 events.

| Rotation category | Definition | Events |
|---|---|---:|
| Same-form substitution | Replacement shared the withdrawn object’s broad medium and use, whether or not it served the same argument | 18 |
| Role-preserving substitution | Replacement retained the same coded interpretive role; medium or use could differ | 21 |
| Role-changing substitution | Replacement introduced a materially different claim or narrative function | 15 |
| Unfilled removal | Object was withdrawn and no replacement installed during the post-change window | 10 |
| **Total** |  | **64** |

The categories were mutually exclusive. Four same-form replacements also plausibly retained the original role, but the analysis assigned them to the same-form category because their rotation briefs described physical matching as the selection principle. This decision makes the categories easier to interpret operationally, though it separates selection intent from the replacement’s observable properties.

### 2.2 Coding interpretive role

Each withdrawn and replacement object was coded into one primary role:

- **Evidence:** direct support for a historical claim;
- **Contrast:** complication or contradiction of a nearby object;
- **Orientation:** establishment of time, place, or process;
- **Emotional anchor:** personal detail intended to make an abstract theme tangible;
- **Transition:** connection between the current bay and the next one.

Two coders reviewed the installation brief, object record, label, and a short curator account. They were not shown sensor or survey outcomes. Agreement on the five-way role classification was 81%, with Cohen’s kappa of 0.73. Disagreements were resolved in a meeting with the exhibition editor.

The curator accounts were usually written within five working days after installation rather than before it. They clarify decisions that were not fully documented in the original register, but they also allow the finished display to influence how its intention was described.

A separate **curatorial distance score** gave one point for each of six differences between withdrawn and replacement objects: medium, period by more than 40 years, region, producer’s social position, original use context, and visual scale. Scores ranged from zero to six. The score was intended as a compact description, not as a validated measure of difference; its equally weighted components are contestable.

### 2.3 Visitor movement

Ceiling-mounted thermal sensors generated anonymous movement tracks. The system recorded location and time but did not retain video or device identifiers. A “stop” occurred when a track remained within 1.5 metres of a display for at least four seconds. The four-second threshold had been selected during the museum’s commissioning tests because shorter pauses frequently represented congestion or route negotiation.

Of 52,418 detected gallery sessions, 48,612 met the inclusion rules: entrance through a public doorway, at least three minutes in the exhibition, and no more than 25% missing coordinates. The system could not reliably distinguish adults from children, members of the same close-moving group, or some users of mobility aids from companions. School visits were therefore flagged at the day and hour level rather than identified within individual tracks.

The movement outcomes were:

- **Stop rate:** percentage of tracks passing within range that stopped;
- **Median dwell:** median seconds among tracks that stopped;
- **Planned-next transition:** percentage of stoppers whose next recorded stop was the bay designated by the curatorial route map;
- **Label interaction:** percentage of stoppers who pressed the bay’s illuminated label-audio control.

“Planned-next transition” measures conformity to the exhibition’s intended sequence. A visitor who deliberately compared a display with an earlier room would count against this measure even if that route was thoughtful and productive.

### 2.4 Exit prompts and work logs

On 38 sampled days, every fifth departing group was invited to complete a two-question card. Of 1,126 invitations, 684 usable cards were returned. Visitors were shown a thumbnail of one focal bay encountered that day and asked to write what they thought its principal point had been. Two research assistants, unaware of event category, coded whether the response matched the bay’s stated proposition. Agreement was 86%; disagreements were adjudicated by the learning coordinator.

Response was lower after 16:00 and during school-heavy periods. No demographic information was collected, limiting assessment of whose interpretations are represented.

Staff recorded rotation work in quarter-hour increments under research, conservation, mounting, label production, installation, and administration. Logs were complete for all 64 events, although they excluded earlier collections-development work and the standing cost of maintaining the sensor system.

## 3. Analytical approach

For each event, outcomes were calculated for the 21 valid days before and after installation. The focal bay was matched to an unchanged bay in the same exhibition using pre-change stop rate, label length, case type, and distance from the room entrance. A control bay could be used for more than one event if its display remained unchanged for eight weeks around each event.

The main estimates use a difference-in-differences model at bay-day level. Each estimate compares the pre-to-post change at the altered bay with the contemporaneous change at its matched control. Models include bay fixed effects, calendar-week fixed effects, day of week, total attendance decile, and a flag for scheduled school groups. Standard errors are clustered by rotation event.

This setup reduces bias from stable differences between popular and quiet bays, as well as broad seasonal changes. It does not make rotation assignments random. Difficult displays were more likely to receive ambitious interventions, while readily available objects were more likely to produce same-form substitutions.

Survey estimates use individual cards with standard errors clustered by sampled day. Because only 684 cards were available, recall results are less precise than movement results. Confidence intervals are 95%. No correction was applied for testing several related outcomes, so isolated marginal findings should be treated cautiously.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Attention increased after both role-preserving and role-changing substitutions

| Rotation category | Stop rate, change in percentage points | Median dwell, change in seconds | Label interaction, change in points |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Same-form | +0.7 (-1.2 to +2.6) | +0.6 (-1.1 to +2.4) | +0.3 (-1.0 to +1.6) |
| Role-preserving | **+3.1 (+1.0 to +5.2)** | **+3.8 (+1.4 to +6.3)** | +2.2 (+0.2 to +4.2) |
| Role-changing | **+5.4 (+2.0 to +8.8)** | **+8.9 (+4.8 to +13.1)** | **+4.7 (+1.5 to +7.9)** |
| Unfilled removal | **-8.2 (-11.9 to -4.5)** | -2.7 (-5.2 to -0.2) | **-3.1 (-5.4 to -0.8)** |

Role-changing substitutions had the largest immediate attention gains. In one example, a restrained row of lamp manufacturers’ invoices was replaced by a repaired child’s night-light with visible soldering and a handwritten household warning. It became a visual and emotional focus in a bay previously organised around retail distribution.

The timing of the gains suggests that novelty explains part of this result. For role-changing events, the stop-rate estimate was +8.7 points during days 1–7 but +2.6 points during days 8–21. Role-preserving substitutions were steadier: +2.9 points in the first week and +3.2 thereafter. Same-form substitutions did not produce a clear change in either period.

An unfilled position reduced stopping even when the case and the general label remained in place. Sensor traces showed that visitors often crossed the empty bay’s detection zone, but fewer met the four-second stopping rule. Blank space may be a meaningful curatorial device, yet these ten removals were not designed or labelled as intentional absences.

### 4.2 Narrative continuity diverged from attention

The pathway result reversed the ranking seen for dwell time.

| Rotation category | Planned-next transition, change in points | Proposition recall, change in points |
|---|---:|---:|
| Same-form | +0.4 (-1.8 to +2.6) | +1.5 (-4.0 to +7.0) |
| Role-preserving | **+5.2 (+2.0 to +8.4)** | **+8.6 (+1.7 to +15.5)** |
| Role-changing | **-6.4 (-10.6 to -2.2)** | +3.0 (-5.4 to +11.4) |
| Unfilled removal | **-9.8 (-14.5 to -5.1)** | -10.7 (-21.2 to -0.2) |

Role-preserving replacements made visitors more likely to continue to the next intended bay and more likely to state the focal proposition in an exit prompt. This pattern appeared even where replacement objects were visually unlike the withdrawn material.

Role-changing replacements, by contrast, seem to have acted as local attractions. Tracks frequently moved from them back to earlier displays or directly to another visually prominent object. That behaviour need not signify confusion; visitors may have been making comparisons of their own. However, it did weaken the sequence designed to carry visitors from household labour into the following room on leisure and privacy.

Same-form substitutions were close to neutral across both outcomes. A physical match may reduce visible disturbance, but it did not by itself preserve the relationship between one bay and the next. Review of the briefs found cases in which the replacement matched medium while changing evidentiary purpose—for example, replacing a worker’s annotated electricity bill with a promotional bill insert printed by a utility company.

### 4.3 Greater object difference was not inherently disruptive

Among the 21 role-preserving substitutions, 11 had a curatorial distance score of four or more. These high-distance substitutions increased the planned-next transition rate by 6.1 points, compared with 4.3 points for the ten lower-distance substitutions. The difference between those estimates was not statistically clear, but it provides no indication that cross-medium or cross-period replacement was necessarily harmful.

What appeared more important was whether the replacement maintained a relation already established in the bay. Role-preserving substitutions performed best when they retained at least one of three relationships documented in the installation brief:

- object-to-object comparison;
- object-to-label proposition;
- focal bay-to-next-bay transition.

The seven substitutions coded as preserving all three relations increased planned-next transitions by 8.0 points. The six preserving only one relation increased them by 2.7 points. These are small subgroups, and relation count was coded from some of the same documents used to classify interpretive role, so the gradient should be treated as descriptive rather than as an independent validation.

### 4.4 Retained labels sometimes accumulated interpretive debt

In 31 events, at least half of the previous label text was retained. Nineteen of those labels contained a referential mismatch, defined before analysis as either naming a distinctive feature no longer visible or delaying identification of the replacement object until after the first 60 words.

Relative to the other retained labels, mismatched labels were associated with:

- 2.4 percentage points fewer label interactions;
- 4.1 points fewer planned-next transitions; and
- 7.3 points lower proposition recall.

The confidence interval for the recall difference was wide, from -15.0 to +0.4 points. Still, the direction was consistent across the three measures. Field notes give a plausible mechanism: visitors occasionally looked between the label and the case, then moved on before reaching the paragraph that explained the new object.

Within role-preserving events, the 12 displays receiving substantially rewritten labels had a +7.0-point transition estimate, compared with +2.1 points for the nine that largely reused the old text. The interaction test had a p-value of 0.08. This is suggestive rather than decisive, particularly because complex rotations were more likely to receive editorial attention.

### 4.5 Role preservation required moderate additional labour

| Rotation category | Median recorded staff hours | Interquartile range |
|---|---:|---:|
| Same-form | 10.8 | 8.2–14.5 |
| Role-preserving | 16.6 | 12.4–21.8 |
| Role-changing | 27.4 | 19.1–35.6 |
| Unfilled removal | 5.2 | 3.8–7.7 |

The main extra cost of role-preserving work was not installation. It was research and label production. Staff spent a median of 4.8 hours on those tasks for role-preserving substitutions, compared with 1.9 hours for same-form substitutions. Role-changing events consumed still more time because they often required new approvals, revised room introductions, or altered mounts.

For planning purposes, a simple **continuity yield** was calculated by adding the estimated change in planned-next transition to the change in proposition recall, then dividing by median staff hours. Role-preserving substitutions yielded 0.83 combined percentage points per hour, compared with 0.18 for same-form substitutions, -0.12 for role-changing substitutions, and -3.94 for unfilled removals.

This composite is a rough prioritisation device. It adds two outcomes with different sampling bases, gives them equal weight without visitor consultation, and assumes that marginal gains scale linearly with staff time. It should not be interpreted as a monetary benefit-cost ratio. Its value is mainly in making the labour trade-off visible rather than treating interpretation as costless.

## 5. Sensitivity checks

Four checks were used to test whether the main pattern depended on particular analytical choices.

First, shortening the event window from 21 to 14 days produced a role-preserving stop-rate estimate of +3.4 points and a transition estimate of +5.5 points, close to the main results.

Second, redefining a stop as seven rather than four seconds reduced all stop rates but retained the category ordering. Role-preserving and role-changing estimates were +2.5 and +4.6 points respectively.

Third, 11 events with another rotation elsewhere in the same room during the 42-day window were removed. The estimated transition gain for role-preserving events was +4.7 points, while the role-changing estimate was -5.8 points.

Fourth, placebo change dates were assigned 21 days before installation. None of the four category-specific placebo transition estimates exceeded 2.3 points in absolute size. With only 64 events, however, the placebo analysis has limited ability to identify moderate pre-existing trends.

A simple unmatched before-and-after comparison produced larger attention estimates than the matched analysis, especially for role-changing events. Attendance tended to rise around several high-profile rotations, confirming the importance of contemporaneous controls.

## 6. Implications for rotation practice

The evidence supports five changes to routine curation at North Quay Museum.

### Record function before searching the store

The rotation register should include a one-sentence statement of the outgoing object’s role. “Provides evidence that evening work moved into kitchens” is more useful for substitution than “small paper item, 1910s.” Medium, size, and conservation requirements remain necessary filters, but they should follow rather than replace the interpretive statement.

### Preserve a relationship, not merely a category

A replacement should retain at least one explicit relationship to another object, the focal proposition, or the next display. The best-performing rotations preserved several. This can be checked in a short pre-installation review without requiring a full gallery redesign.

### Treat inherited labels as drafts

Even when the broad argument remains stable, the first 60 words should identify the replacement and explain why it is present. Reusing text can save editorial time, but a label that points to absent features shifts work onto the visitor.

### Separate novelty monitoring from continuity monitoring

A one-week increase in stopping should not be taken as sufficient evidence of success. Role-changing substitutions often achieved that result while weakening the intended route. A practical review should examine both local attention and what visitors do next.

### Make absence deliberate

Unfilled removals performed poorly under every measured outcome, but these were untreated gaps rather than intentionally curated absences. If a vacancy must remain, the case and label should explain whether the absence reflects conservation, loss, incomplete collecting, or a deliberate pause in the narrative.

## 7. Limitations

This was an observational study at one institution and within one exhibition. The displays shared a chronological structure and an emphasis on domestic material culture; open-storage galleries, art-historical hangs, or exhibitions designed for free exploration may value sequence differently.

Rotation category was not randomly assigned. Although matched controls, fixed effects, and timing checks address several alternatives, they cannot remove selection bias. Curators may have chosen role-preserving treatment where they already saw a strong replacement opportunity. Some interpretive-role accounts were written after installation, which may also have made successful displays easier to describe as coherent.

Sensor measures are behavioural proxies. A four-second stop is not equivalent to attention, comprehension, or enjoyment, and the planned route is only one legitimate path. Group movement, accessibility needs, and children’s behaviour were imperfectly represented. The exit cards provide a more direct interpretive measure, but their convenience sample was modest and uneven across the day.

Finally, several outcomes and subgroup comparisons were examined without a multiple-testing correction. The repeated pattern across attention, pathway, recall, and labour is more persuasive than any single borderline estimate, but precise numerical rankings should not be treated as settled.

## 8. Conclusion

The rotation that most closely resembles a withdrawn object is not necessarily the rotation that best preserves a gallery’s argument. At North Quay Museum, substitutions organised around interpretive role sustained both attention and narrative movement, while visually dramatic role changes often produced a short-lived attention spike at the expense of the planned sequence.

This does not make physical similarity irrelevant. Form affects mounting, conservation, visual rhythm, and visitor expectation. The analysis instead places interpretive function earlier in the decision process. Before asking what can occupy the same patch of case or wall, curators should record what would disappear from the exhibition’s argument if the outgoing object were simply removed.
