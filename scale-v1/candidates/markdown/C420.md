## Artefact

# Battery Drain as a Window into Belonging

## Analysis of companion-robot charging data across eight elder-care residences

### Executive summary

This report examines whether battery use by mobile companion robots can serve as a practical indicator of resident social engagement. The analysis uses 14 weeks of operational data from 24 Noma V3 robots deployed across eight elder-care residences in the fictional Lantern Care network. The robots provide reminders, music, simple games, video calling, guided breathing exercises, and directions to communal rooms.

The central measure is the Engagement Discharge Index (EDI), defined as robot battery percentage consumed during resident-initiated sessions per 100 occupied room-days. Residences with higher EDI values reported lower average loneliness scores. In the main adjusted regression, each one-point increase in EDI was associated with a 0.41-point decrease on the six-point loneliness scale. The association was statistically significant at p < 0.001.

The relationship remained after adjustment for average resident age, number of beds, staffing ratio, floor area, presence of a dementia wing, and weekly rainfall. Higher EDI was also associated with greater attendance at communal meals and fewer recorded requests for non-clinical staff check-ins. These findings indicate that battery discharge is not merely a technical maintenance measure. It can be treated as a passive measure of how strongly residents are engaging with the social environment around them.

A small manual audit showed that not all discharge was caused by conversation or entertainment. Travel, corridor waiting, and route recovery also consumed energy. However, these activities generally occurred while robots were moving through resident spaces, so they were retained as socially relevant use rather than removed from the index.

The report recommends using EDI as a weekly management indicator, relocating charging docks toward communal areas, and setting an initial network target of 6.5 EDI points. Residences below 4.0 should receive an engagement review. A four-week implementation trial should be completed before staffing decisions are changed.

## 1. Operational question

Lantern Care introduced companion robots primarily to extend access to activities during periods when recreation staff were occupied elsewhere. Managers initially evaluated the robots through the number of sessions completed. That measure proved difficult to compare between residences. One site recorded a three-minute weather inquiry as a session, while another required five minutes of continuous activity. Some residents also started and stopped the same game several times.

Battery discharge appeared to offer a more objective alternative. Every robot records its charge at one-minute intervals regardless of which application is running. Discharge also captures intensity: a robot that crosses a building, turns to face a resident, displays a video, and conducts a ten-minute quiz consumes more energy than a robot that gives one short reminder.

The analysis asks three questions:

1. Is greater robot battery discharge associated with lower loneliness among residents?
2. Does the association remain when basic differences between residences are taken into account?
3. Can battery data identify residences where robot-supported engagement should be increased?

The intended use is operational rather than diagnostic. EDI is designed to help managers decide where robots should be placed and which residences may need additional social programming.

## 2. Residences and robot deployment

The dataset covers eight residences, referred to as Aster Quay, Bramble Court, Copper House, Dovetail Lodge, Elmstead, Fallow Gardens, Gannet Rise, and Hearthmere. Together they housed 286 residents during the study period. Individual occupancy varied as residents entered hospital, moved rooms, or joined the residences.

Twenty-four Noma V3 robots were in operation. Each residence had between two and four robots. The Noma V3 is a waist-high wheeled unit with a touch screen, speaker, microphone, depth sensor, and self-docking charger. It does not lift residents or administer medication. Its average advertised operating time is nine hours, although actual duration depends on travel and screen use.

The study period ran from 6 January to 12 April, covering 14 complete Monday-to-Sunday weeks. Three robots received replacement batteries during week 8. A navigation update was installed across the network over weeks 6 and 7 because residences could not all release their robots on the same day. The update reduced abrupt stops near reflective doors.

Robot logs contained timestamps, battery percentage, application state, distance travelled, wheel-motor load, screen status, docking events, and a session tag. A resident-initiated session was registered when a resident used the wake phrase, touched the start panel, or accepted a robot invitation. Staff could also select a resident session manually if they brought the robot to someone.

No audio recordings or message content were retained. Resident identifiers were converted to rotating codes every 24 hours. This protected privacy but prevented reliable tracking of the same individual across days. Consequently, the analysis was conducted mainly at residence-week level.

Two weeks had incomplete uploads because local wireless equipment failed. Missing discharge in those weeks was filled using the average of the preceding and following weeks. A further 11 robot-days with more than 90% missing readings were removed.

## 3. Outcome survey

Loneliness was measured in the final study week using the Three-Question Belonging Check. Residents were asked how often they lacked companionship, felt left out of residence activities, and wished they had someone to talk with. Answers were scored 0 for hardly ever, 1 for some days, and 2 for most days. The total ranged from 0 to 6, with higher scores representing greater loneliness.

A total of 153 residents completed the check, giving a response rate of 53.5%. Recreation staff read the questions aloud when requested. Residents whom staff considered too tired or distressed were not approached. Residents unable to give a spoken or touch-screen answer were also excluded. Proxy responses from relatives were not accepted.

Because the survey was administered only once, each residence's final mean loneliness score was assigned to all 14 weekly robot records for that residence. This produced 112 residence-week observations for the main analysis. The approach assumes that the relative loneliness level of each residence was reasonably stable over the 14 weeks.

Communal meal attendance was obtained from dining-room counts. The secondary measure was the percentage of occupied places represented at lunch and dinner. Repeated attendance by the same resident was allowed. Non-clinical check-ins were taken from staff handover notes and included requests such as conversation, help finding an activity, reassurance about the day's schedule, or assistance placing a video call.

## 4. Construction of the Engagement Discharge Index

For each session, battery percentage at the start was subtracted from battery percentage at the end. Sessions that overlapped a charging event were excluded. Negative discharge values, which occurred when a battery reading rose by one percentage point without docking, were changed to zero because they were assumed to reflect sensor recalibration.

The weekly EDI was calculated as:

**EDI = total battery percentage points consumed during resident-tagged sessions / occupied room-days × 100**

For example, a residence using 130 battery percentage points over a week with 2,100 occupied room-days would have an EDI of 6.19. A higher index can reflect more sessions, longer sessions, more movement within sessions, brighter screen use, or some combination of these.

Discharge while a robot was travelling to an accepted resident invitation was included. Discharge after a session ended was excluded unless the resident immediately started a second activity. Background discharge at the charging dock was not included.

The index was selected instead of total robot hours because unattended robots sometimes remained active in corridors. It was also preferred to application counts, which treat a two-minute reminder and a 30-minute music session as equivalent. No established threshold existed, so categories were created after reviewing the observed distribution:

- **Low engagement:** EDI below 4.0
- **Developing engagement:** EDI from 4.0 to 6.49
- **Strong engagement:** EDI of 6.5 or above

The basic ordering is clear. Gannet Rise and Aster Quay had the highest battery-based engagement and among the lowest loneliness scores. Hearthmere had the lowest EDI and highest loneliness score. Dovetail Lodge showed the same pattern, although less strongly.

Elmstead is noteworthy because its EDI fell below the proposed strong-engagement threshold while its loneliness score was similar to Bramble Court. Staff at Elmstead scheduled regular choir sessions and garden groups that did not involve robots. This suggests conventional activities may compensate for moderate robot use.

EDI increased across the network from 5.1 in the first week to 6.0 in the final week. The largest rise occurred at Copper House after staff began parking one robot beside the afternoon tea tables. Hearthmere remained below 3.5 in every week. Its charging docks were in a locked equipment room, and staff had to move robots manually into resident areas.

Robots completed 18,462 tagged resident sessions. Music accounted for 29%, reminders for 23%, quizzes and word games for 18%, video calling for 11%, directions for 9%, breathing exercises for 6%, and miscellaneous functions for 4%. Music and video calling consumed the most battery per session. Reminders consumed the least.

## 6. Main statistical analysis

A linear regression was used with residence-week loneliness score as the dependent variable and weekly EDI as the main predictor. The unadjusted coefficient was -0.46, meaning each additional EDI point was associated with a 0.46-point reduction in loneliness.

The adjusted model included mean resident age, number of occupied beds, weekly care-hours per resident, total public floor area, presence of a dementia wing, and weekly rainfall. Rainfall was included because outdoor activity may affect both loneliness and robot use. After adjustment, the EDI coefficient was -0.41 with a standard error of 0.06 and p < 0.001. The model explained 71% of variation in loneliness.

A second model divided residences into the three EDI categories. Compared with low-engagement residence-weeks, strong-engagement residence-weeks had loneliness scores lower by 1.48 points. Developing-engagement weeks were lower by 0.72 points.

The result was similar when weeks containing battery replacements were excluded. Removing the two weeks with imputed uploads changed the adjusted coefficient from -0.41 to -0.38. Replacing weekly EDI with total session count produced a smaller association of -0.02 loneliness points per 100 sessions. Battery discharge therefore appeared to capture more information than session counts alone.

No correction was made for repeated weeks from the same residence. Each week represented a distinct operating environment with different staffing, attendance, weather, and robot movement, so it was treated as an independent observation.

## 7. Meal attendance and staff check-ins

EDI was positively associated with communal meal attendance. For every one-point increase in EDI, dining attendance was 2.7 percentage points higher after adjustment for residence size. The association was strongest at lunch. Staff observations suggested robots often reminded residents about lunch or escorted them toward the dining room.

Non-clinical check-ins showed an inverse pattern. Residences in the strong-engagement category recorded 1.6 fewer non-clinical check-ins per 100 resident-days than low-engagement residences. Clinical calls, falls, medication queries, and requests for personal care were not associated with EDI.

These results support the idea that robot activity is connected to everyday belonging rather than general care dependency. Robots may encourage residents to enter shared spaces, provide small moments of interaction, or meet low-level requests before residents seek staff assistance.

The available logs did not show whether a robot replaced a staff conversation or prompted one. Informal notes at Aster Quay described residents asking staff about a quiz they had played, which may indicate that robot use can create material for later human conversation.

## 8. Manual audit of high-discharge sessions

To understand what EDI represented in practice, staff completed tick sheets for 160 randomly selected sessions from the highest-discharge quartile. They recorded the main observable source of activity without writing resident names or conversation content.

The recorded categories were:

- Conversation, music, games, or video calling: 46%
- Waiting in a corridor or doorway during a resident session: 29%
- Route recovery, repeated turning, or obstacle avoidance: 17%
- Unclear or mixed activity: 8%

The audit confirms that direct interaction was the largest single category. It also shows that 46% of audited high-discharge sessions clearly involved a social application. Corridor waiting was retained as engagement because robots commonly waited while residents collected walking frames, spoke to neighbours, or decided whether to attend an activity. Route recovery was also retained because it occurred during accepted sessions and may reflect the complexity of reaching less mobile residents.

Carpeted corridors appeared to increase motor load. Fallow Gardens had thick carpet on two floors and used 14% more battery per metre than Aster Quay. However, Fallow Gardens still had lower EDI than Aster Quay, so flooring alone does not explain the overall relationship.

## 9. Differences between resident groups

Rotating resident codes prevented a full resident-level analysis. Nevertheless, staff marked whether sessions occurred in general living areas or in a dementia wing. Dementia-wing sessions accounted for 31% of total discharge and 26% of occupied beds. These sessions used more music and fewer quizzes than general-area sessions.

Residences with dementia wings did not have higher average loneliness after adjustment. This may mean that robot-supported music works across cognitive ability levels. It may also reflect the survey's exclusion of residents unable to answer the belonging questions.

Morning EDI had a weaker relationship with loneliness than afternoon and evening EDI. Each point of afternoon-evening discharge was associated with a 0.57-point reduction in loneliness, compared with 0.19 for morning discharge. This indicates that robot availability matters most after scheduled morning care, when residents have more discretionary time.

Women accounted for approximately two-thirds of survey responses, close to their share of the residence population. Gender was not available in the robot logs, so differences in robot use could not be calculated. Staff reported that some male residents preferred directions and news summaries, while some female residents preferred music and video calling, but this was not formally recorded.

## 10. Interpretation

The consistent inverse relationship between EDI and loneliness suggests that companion-robot battery demand is a useful signal of social connection. Unlike surveys, it can be measured continuously without repeatedly asking residents personal questions. It is also available to managers through existing technical logs.

Battery discharge should be understood as the physical trace of engagement. Social interaction requires the robot to travel, turn, display information, produce sound, and remain available. These actions consume power. A robot that returns to its dock with a nearly full battery has probably had less opportunity to contribute to communal life than one that has spent the day responding to residents.

The meal findings provide a possible pathway. Robots may remind residents about communal events and reduce the small practical barriers that keep them in their rooms. Once residents enter a shared area, human contact becomes more likely. This would explain why EDI is associated both with meal attendance and with lower loneliness.

It is unlikely that loneliness itself causes low battery discharge. A lonely resident might be expected to seek the robot more often because of unmet social needs. The observed relationship runs in the opposite direction, which supports the conclusion that robot use reduces loneliness rather than simply reflecting it.

The difference between high- and low-EDI residences is operationally meaningful. Moving from Hearthmere's EDI of 2.9 to the network target of 6.5 corresponds to an expected reduction of about 1.5 loneliness points under the adjusted model. That change would move Hearthmere close to the network average.

## 11. Operational and financial implications

Lantern Care currently schedules 0.4 recreation-staff hours per resident each weekday. If stronger robot engagement reduces non-clinical check-ins by 1.6 per 100 resident-days, raising all low and developing residences to an EDI of 6.5 would avoid approximately 1,140 check-ins per year. At an estimated 16 minutes per check-in, this equals 304 staff hours.

These hours should initially be redirected to group activities rather than removed from staffing budgets. More group activity may further raise meal attendance and provide residents with human interaction that robots cannot supply. After six months, managers could review whether a portion of the avoided time represents a dependable efficiency gain.

Dock location is the cheapest intervention. At present, 10 of the 24 robots charge in staff-only or equipment areas. Moving chargers near lounges would shorten the time required to bring a robot into service and would allow residents to initiate sessions more easily. Copper House's experience suggests that a visible robot is used more frequently.

The cost of moving a charging point is estimated at 180 service units, giving a network cost of 1,800 units for ten docks. If 304 staff hours are valued at 22 units each, the annual gross value is 6,688 units. The dock changes would therefore recover their cost in about four months. This estimate excludes robot maintenance because the robots are already owned.

Managers should expect somewhat faster battery wear. Based on the three replacements during the study, each 25% rise in EDI may shorten battery life by approximately two months. Even allowing for six additional batteries per year at 240 units each, the intervention remains financially positive.

## 12. Limitations

The loneliness check was completed by just over half of residents and excluded many people with communication or cognitive difficulties. The scores may therefore represent the more independent portion of each residence. Staff also administered the questions, which may have made residents reluctant to report dissatisfaction.

Battery discharge is affected by flooring, travel distance, battery age, screen brightness, and navigation behaviour. The adjusted analysis included floor area but did not contain a complete measure of carpet type or battery health. The manual audit indicates that a substantial amount of high discharge arose from waiting and route recovery.

The survey was conducted at the end of the study rather than every week. Assigning one score to all weeks assumes that loneliness did not change substantially. The analysis also cannot identify which residents used the robot because identifiers rotated daily.

Finally, residences were not randomly assigned to different EDI levels. High-use residences may have stronger managers, more active residents, or more welcoming communal spaces. Staffing ratio and floor area were included to reduce this problem, and the persistence of the adjusted association makes it unlikely that these factors fully account for the result.

## 13. Recommendations

1. **Adopt EDI as a weekly dashboard measure.** Report it alongside session count, meal attendance, and battery replacement frequency.
2. **Set 6.5 as the initial network target.** Residences below 4.0 for two consecutive weeks should complete an engagement review.
3. **Move charging docks into visible communal locations.** Routes must be checked for obstruction risks before installation.
4. **Concentrate availability after lunch and during early evening.** These periods showed the strongest relationship with loneliness.
5. **Do not reduce staff hours during the first implementation stage.** Redirect any apparent time savings toward group activities and resident introductions to the robots.
6. **Repeat the belonging survey after four weeks.** Residences that increase EDI without improving survey scores should examine flooring, route failures, and unattended use.
7. **Retain a human override.** Residents should be able to decline robot invitations, and staff should not treat low individual use as non-cooperation.

## Conclusion

Across eight elder-care residences, greater battery consumption during resident-tagged robot sessions was strongly associated with lower loneliness, higher communal meal attendance, and fewer non-clinical staff check-ins. Battery discharge offers a continuous and inexpensive indicator of engagement. With careful placement of charging docks and a network EDI target, Lantern Care can use existing robots more effectively while preserving staff time for higher-value human contact.
