## Artefact

# The Median That Hummed

On her first morning with the Department of Civic Quiet, Mara Vale was told to measure what was no longer there.

Blue Finch Avenue had received six blocks of hush asphalt, a black material advertised as swallowing the scrape of tires before it could climb apartment walls. Mara set her microphone on a tripod beside the planted median. The morning traffic passed with the distant softness of coats being brushed. Without the usual reflections from the road, footsteps sounded strangely separate from the people making them.

Her supervisor, Alden Prill, handed her the certification form.

“Write forty percent quieter,” he said. “That is what the mayor is announcing Thursday.”

Mara had not yet switched on the meter.

At noon she measured buses, vans, bicycles, shoe heels and a terrier barking from a third-floor window. The numbers were lower, though not always by forty percent. She wrote them carefully in her field book. Between measurements she noticed a thin metallic chirping from the median, where dusty grasshoppers lived among the sedges. Each chirp was answered farther down the avenue, making a chain too orderly to be accidental.

Her grandmother had once lived above Blue Finch Bakery. When Mara visited as a child, they opened the kitchen window at dusk and listened to what Grandmother called the seam: insects in the median, drain water below them, spoons striking teacups in the cafés, and the high wire-song of buses turning beneath the lamps.

“A city stays together by answering itself,” Grandmother used to say.

Mara had considered this merely an old person’s sentence until that afternoon.

The new asphalt ended at Lark Street. On the untreated side, the grasshoppers answered one another from median to pavement garden to median again. On the treated side, the calls stopped after a few metres. Mara crouched near the curb and watched one insect lift its wings, sing, wait, then sing again. No answer came. It hopped in a small circle as if it had misplaced an object.

Mara recorded both sides. The difference was obvious through her headphones: one was a conversation; the other was a single dry click repeated into nothing.

Back at the office, Alden was selecting pastries for the mayoral ceremony.

“The surface is eating their calls,” Mara said.

“That is its job.”

“They may use the road as a sounding board.”

“They are bugs, Mara.”

“The city’s sound is habitat.”

Alden sighed. He had the smooth, patient face of a man who believed patience was the same as kindness. “The residents asked for less noise. We gave them less noise. Put forty percent on the form.”

That evening Mara returned to Blue Finch Avenue. She found a delivery rider named Sio beside a toppled bicycle. A silent electric bus had passed too close, he said, and he had not heard it until its mirror clipped his bag. He was unhurt except for a red mark on his elbow.

“The old road sent bus noise up the shop windows,” Sio explained. “You heard it twice—once behind you, once above. Now it just appears.”

Mara wrote this down as proof that the asphalt was dangerous, although she did not ask whether Sio had been looking at the road or whether any collisions had occurred before the resurfacing. Farther along, a café owner complained that customers no longer sat outside because the avenue felt “dead.” A woman walking a white dog said it felt peaceful. Mara did not write down the woman’s remark.

By Wednesday, Mara had developed a theory. The median insects warned cyclists of buses. Their chirps bounced from approaching vehicles and changed pitch, and generations of residents had unconsciously learned the pattern. The new asphalt absorbed the chirps, removed the reflections and therefore weakened an ancient warning system connecting people, insects and streets.

She explained this to Alden without conducting another test.

“That is impossible,” he said.

“It happened to Sio.”

“One bicycle is not a transportation study.”

“One grasshopper is not silence either.”

Alden took away her field microphone and told her to attend the ceremony as a member of the audience.

On Thursday, the mayor stood behind a green lectern in the median. A banner called Blue Finch Avenue **THE QUIETEST STREET IN Veyra**. Officials applauded. Cars whispered by, and the mayor praised the asphalt for defeating unnecessary sound.

Mara had hidden a small loudspeaker inside her lunch bag. Before the mayor could cut the ribbon, she played her recording of the untreated street. Grasshoppers passed their bright pulse down the median. Cups rang. A bus hummed beneath a balcony. The sounds were not beautiful by themselves, but together they gave the air depth, like windows opening one behind another.

Then Mara played the treated side: one insect calling, waiting and calling again.

The audience became still. Even Alden looked toward the median.

“That one is looking for the others,” Mara said loudly.

The mayor stepped away from the lectern. She listened for several seconds and declared that a city should not purchase quiet at the cost of belonging. She ordered the hush asphalt removed immediately, despite the expense and without consulting engineers, residents or the council.

Workers arrived that afternoon with cutters. By sunset they had peeled up one narrow strip beside the median. The first exposed stones were still warm when a grasshopper chirped. Another answered from across the avenue. Soon the entire median hummed again, louder than Mara remembered it.

Sio rang his bicycle bell. Café customers came outside. Apartment windows opened one after another, and the people of Blue Finch Avenue leaned out to hear their street recognize itself.
