## Artefact

# Policy Memo: Leaf-Litter Islands for Downtown Soil Recovery

**To:** Brindleford Parks Operations Director  
**From:** Urban Grounds Policy Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Retaining autumn leaves in selected street-tree beds

## Purpose

Authorize a one-year program that converts 60 downtown tree beds into “litter islands” by retaining fallen leaves rather than removing them. The program is intended to rebuild soil-food webs around street trees without purchasing compost or fertilizer.

## Rationale

A grounds crew observed noticeably more mites and springtails beneath leaves left beside the Fenwick Depot than in nearby swept beds. Because these organisms break down organic matter, their presence indicates that the depot soil is recovering. Expanding the same conditions should therefore improve tree-root growth and reduce watering needs across the downtown district.

Leaf cover will also keep soil temperatures stable. Once decomposition begins, fungi should move nutrients directly into tree roots, making supplemental fertilizer unnecessary. The city currently spends approximately $18,000 each year removing leaves from tree beds, so retaining them should recover most of that amount.

## Program Design

Crews should place a 7-centimetre layer of locally collected leaves in each selected bed every November. Leaves may be moved between blocks so that all beds receive equal coverage. Beds near cafés should receive low wooden edging to prevent wind dispersal. Plastic waste and visibly diseased leaves should be removed by hand.

Staff should count mites in one soil sample per bed in November and again in May. If average mite abundance rises by 20 percent, the program should be considered successful and expanded to all downtown tree beds. If abundance declines, crews should return to normal sweeping.

## Recommendation

Approve the program before autumn scheduling begins. It requires no specialist contractor, uses material already collected by the city, and offers a simple route to healthier urban soil.

Consequently, a November-to-May change in average mite abundance cannot distinguish the treatment’s effect from seasonal shifts, weather, or sampling variability. The memo also sets no evaluation measures for soil moisture, nutrients, root growth, canopy condition, watering demand, or other soil organisms. Thus, even a genuine 20 percent increase in mites would not demonstrate healthier trees, reduced watering, or broader soil recovery. *Basis: The memo’s expansion rule relies on an uncontrolled November-to-May change in one narrow indicator, which cannot establish the broader claimed outcomes.*

### Argument 3

A subtler but serious defect is the instruction to move leaves between blocks to equalize coverage. Even though the memo limits material to “locally collected leaves,” cross-block movement can still transport fungal pathogens, insect eggs, invasive seeds, road contaminants, or other organisms from one site to another. Removing only “visibly diseased” leaves is a weak safeguard because some pathogens and propagules may not be apparent during hand sorting. The memo provides no narrower sourcing criteria, screening protocol, traceability process, or rule against moving material from affected sites. A soil-recovery pilot should manage this avoidable transfer risk rather than assuming local collection makes redistribution safe, so 30 may still overstate its policy readiness.
