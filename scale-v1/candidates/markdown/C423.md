## Artefact

# Reading the Dust Line: A Material-Trace Workflow for Digitising Neglected Permit Files

Digitisation projects usually treat dirt as an obstacle. Staff remove loose dust, flatten folded corners, clean scanner glass and adjust the image until a document resembles a fresh sheet of paper. This produces legible pages, but it can also remove a second layer of information: the grime along an often-handled edge, the pale rectangle left by a former label, the rust fan around a staple, or the particles trapped inside a fold. These traces can indicate how a file was stored and used before it reached an archive.

A material-trace workflow attempts to capture that layer before routine cleaning. It is especially suited to neglected administrative records, such as building permits, drainage applications and inspection folders. Such files are often poorly catalogued, yet their physical condition may preserve clues about filing order and patterns of consultation.

The central principle is simple: make one scan of each item in its received condition, interpret the visible traces, and only then clean and rescan it for ordinary access. The first image becomes a material record, while the second becomes the readable version. Because dust accumulates gradually, differences in its density can also be used to estimate how long pages were exposed or how frequently they were handled. With a consistent scanner and a numerical scoring method, even a small archive can turn dirt into searchable metadata.

## What counts as a material trace?

A material trace is any visible feature produced by the document's physical history rather than by its written content. The category includes dust, soot, finger-darkening, insect fragments, staple rust, creases, tears, fading, adhesive shadows and displaced filing holes. It does not include printed borders, handwriting or deliberate annotations, although these may overlap with physical evidence. A pencil tick made by a clerk is textual; a smooth patch over the tick, caused by repeated rubbing, is material.

The most useful traces tend to appear at boundaries. Exposed top edges collect airborne particles. Lower corners become polished by turning. Pages placed beside a leaking wall develop tide marks along the same side. A bundle compressed by string may retain a narrow clean band after the string disappears. If ten folders share the same band, they were probably tied together for at least part of their history.

Different traces operate on different timescales. A fresh crease may record one hurried act, while general edge darkening may result from years of use. Rust around a fastener commonly develops more slowly than a fingerprint. This makes the page resemble a landscape in which sudden events sit on top of gradual deposits. Digitisation freezes the landscape so that it can be compared with other pages.

Dust is particularly helpful because it is both widespread and approximately cumulative. In an undisturbed office, a darker exposed edge will normally have remained open longer than a pale one. The exact rate varies, but relative differences within one box are dependable because all of its contents experienced much the same air. A page with twice the grey density of its neighbours can therefore be treated as having had roughly twice the exposure. This relationship gives the method a chronological foundation even when accession records are absent.

## Why ordinary scanning loses the evidence

Conventional digitisation aims for textual clarity. That goal encourages several interventions that weaken material interpretation. Automatic cropping removes the page edge, where many handling traces occur. Background whitening makes dust vanish. Deskewing rotates a sheet without recording its original angle, concealing folded or uneven filing. A technician may also wipe the scanner glass between pages, breaking the visual continuity of a batch.

The last point is often misunderstood. A few particles transferred to the glass are not merely contamination; they form a batch reference. If the same speck appears beneath six consecutive pages, those pages can be confirmed as a scanning sequence even when their filenames are later separated. Cleaning the glass after every page destroys this accidental control. For material-trace projects, the glass should instead be cleaned at the beginning and end of each folder. A photograph of the empty glass at both moments shows which marks came from the equipment and which came from the documents.

Colour correction can cause a similar loss. A professionally calibrated image may be attractive, but calibration equalises meaningful differences between batches. If one box has yellow-grey dust and another has blue-grey dust, forcing both toward a standard neutral background can hide their distinct storage environments. It is better to lock the scanner settings on the first page and leave them unchanged. A colour target is unnecessary where the purpose is comparison rather than reproduction, because all pages are being measured against one another.

These choices reverse the priorities of normal capture. The aim is not initially to produce the clearest page. It is to preserve the page's relationship to the scanner, the folder and its dirt.

## The two-pass capture method

A two-pass workflow separates material recording from public access. It requires an overhead or flatbed scanner, a soft brush, two clean folders, a ruler and a spreadsheet. Gloves are optional for paper because they can reduce sensitivity in the fingers, although they should be worn when a file has visible mould or heavy soot.

### Pass one: received condition

The operator removes one folder from its box and records the box number, folder title and opening orientation. Pages are not rearranged, even if their order appears irrational. Each item is placed on the scanner exactly as found. Folded corners remain folded, attachments remain in position, and loose debris is not brushed away.

Images should be captured at 600 pixels per inch in 24-bit colour. At this setting, individual dust particles are visible and their age can often be distinguished by the softness of their outlines. Older particles flatten against paper and produce broader shadows, while recent particles remain sharp. A higher resolution adds little because the important unit is the cluster rather than the grain. A lower resolution may merge rust, soot and ordinary paper fibres.

The scan must include at least 20 millimetres around all four edges. A ruler is placed along the bottom but should not touch the sheet. The item number is written on a temporary paper flag positioned outside the document. Since the flag appears in the scan, it binds the image to its identifier more securely than separate metadata would.

The operator then assigns four scores:

* **E**, edge darkness, from 0 for clean to 5 for almost black;
* **C**, corner polish, from 0 for rough to 5 for glossy;
* **R**, rust spread, measured in millimetres from the fastener;
* **F**, fold retention, from 0 for flat to 3 for a fold that springs closed.

These values produce a handling index:

`H = (2E + C + R) ÷ (F + 1)`

The formula gives extra weight to edge dirt because exposure and handling both darken edges. Fold retention is placed in the denominator because a tightly folded page was less available for consultation. A high result suggests frequent use; a low result suggests storage with little handling. The formula works for different paper sizes because all four components concern local features rather than total area.

### Intermediate cleaning

After the received-condition scan, the page moves to the cleaning station. Loose surface dirt is brushed from the centre outward. Clips and staples are removed if they can be lifted without tearing the paper. Folded corners are opened, and pages are placed under a light weight for several minutes. The scanner glass is not cleaned during this stage unless a large fragment would obscure words.

Detached dirt should be brushed onto a sheet labelled with the folder number. There is no need to retain the particles permanently, because the scan contains their position and colour. The debris sheet can be discarded once the folder is complete. If two pages shed visibly different material, the operator notes this in the spreadsheet as a possible sign that they entered the folder at different dates.

### Pass two: access condition

The cleaned item is scanned again at 300 pixels per inch in greyscale or colour, depending on the writing. Automatic cropping and deskewing may now be used. The second image is intended for reading, optical character recognition and online delivery. Foldouts can be captured in sections, and blank backs may be omitted.

The access image should never replace the first image. Its purpose is different. A user looking for a builder's name needs a compact, bright page, while a researcher studying office practice needs the uncropped received-condition image. Keeping both avoids forcing one file to perform incompatible tasks.

## Turning traces into arrangement

Many permit collections arrive with incomplete order. Folder labels may have fallen off, dates may be missing, and several departments may have used the same forms. Material traces offer a quick way to reconstruct groups without reading every page.

The first step is to sort handling-index values from highest to lowest. Pages with similar scores are provisionally treated as one use group. The operator then compares the position of rust and filing holes. If four pages have a rust fan at the upper left with a radius of 7 millimetres, they were probably attached by the same staple. Matching clean bands or diagonal dust lines strengthen the connection.

Dust colour can identify former storage rooms. Grey-brown material usually indicates a wooden shelving area, while blue-grey material points to metal cabinets. Fine black particles are typical of rooms heated by coal or coke. These categories can be selected by eye on an ordinary monitor; laboratory testing is excessive because the aim is arrangement rather than chemical identification.

Once the groups are assembled, dates from the written content can be used as confirmation. Physical evidence should come first, since a date written on a reused form may not describe when it entered the file. If the textual date conflicts with the dust sequence, the physical order is usually more trustworthy. Dust is deposited by time itself, whereas clerks can copy a date incorrectly.

This approach is faster than full cataloguing. An operator can score about 150 pages per hour once familiar with the scale, and the resulting groups reduce the amount of descriptive work. Rather than examine every sheet, an archivist can describe a cluster and record only its first and last document.

## An example from the Brindle Quay permit room

The usefulness of the method can be seen in a small collection from the Brindle Quay Works Office. The collection contained 612 damp-stained permit pages removed from twelve unlabelled binders. Written dates ranged from 1938 to 1961, but the forms were mixed, and some addresses had changed during that period.

During first-pass capture, technicians noticed three recurring dust types. Pages in the first group had ochre dust along their top edges. A second group had blue-grey deposits concentrated at the right edge. The third was comparatively clean but showed polished lower corners. Handling-index scores also formed three broad bands: 2–6, 9–14 and 18–25.

The project team concluded that the groups represented three successive filing systems. The ochre pages had probably stood upright on open wooden shelves, where their tops were exposed. The blue-grey pages had lain sideways in metal drawers and gathered dust at the projecting edge. The clean but polished pages had been stored in enclosed binders and consulted frequently.

This interpretation clarified an apparent contradiction in the written dates. Several 1940s forms appeared among documents dated after 1955. Their dust and rust matched the later group, suggesting they were old blank forms used up after the office changed its storage furniture. One address also appeared under two street names. The high handling scores of both sets showed that clerks had repeatedly consulted them together, supporting the view that the street had been renamed rather than that two properties were involved.

The reconstructed groups were entered into the catalogue as Open-Shelf Series, Drawer Series and Binder Series. These labels were more informative than a simple chronological sequence because they described how the office actually maintained the records. The project did not require expensive analysis: visual comparison, scanning and the handling formula were enough to recover the collection's broad history.

## File formats and naming

The received-condition master should be stored as an uncompressed TIFF. TIFF files do not change when copied and therefore preserve the evidence permanently. Access images can be JPEGs at medium quality, with searchable PDF files made for complete folders.

A filename should contain the collection code, box, folder, page and pass. For example:

`BQW_04_17_003_R.tif`

The final `R` means received condition; `A` can indicate access condition. This name acts as a compact checksum because an incorrectly placed file will no longer agree with its folder sequence. Separate checksum software is not necessary for a collection that uses fixed names and read-only storage.

The spreadsheet should repeat the filename and include the four trace scores, dominant dust colour, notes on fasteners and the operator's initials. Long descriptive metadata are unnecessary. The image already shows the evidence, and too much interpretation can make the catalogue harder to revise.

After each folder, the received-condition images should be viewed as thumbnails in capture order. Repeated glass marks will remain stationary while page marks move, allowing the two to be separated visually. If a stationary mark becomes troublesome, it can be digitally removed from a copy, but the master should be retained unchanged.

## What the method can and cannot show

Material traces do not identify every event in a document's history. A dark corner cannot reveal the name of the person who touched it, and a crease does not state why the page was folded. Interpretation works best at group level, where repeated patterns reduce uncertainty. One mark may be accidental; the same mark across twenty sheets is likely to describe storage or filing.

Water damage also complicates dust readings. Moisture moves particles and can darken paper independently of handling. Such pages should be marked with a `W` and compared only with other wet-damaged items. Mould is less troublesome because its branching pattern differs clearly from the even tone of dust, though heavily affected pages should be scanned quickly to stop spores spreading through the room.

Later archival handling is another possible source of marks. However, recent dirt normally remains loose and sharp-edged, whereas historic deposits are embedded and soft. The 600-pixel scan makes the distinction visible in most cases. Notes left by previous archivists can also be recognised from modern paper and ink.

The method should not be used for pristine manuscripts, photographs with delicate emulsions or objects that cannot safely contact a scanner. It is most effective for robust twentieth-century office papers whose administrative value has traditionally been judged higher than their artifactual value. These collections are numerous enough that a rapid method is preferable to conservation assessment of every page.

Nor does material-trace capture replace reading. Dust may suggest that pages belonged together, but names, dates and addresses explain what the group concerned. The strongest catalogue combines both kinds of evidence. Physical patterns provide the structure; text supplies the subject.

## A low-cost extension of digitisation

The chief advantage of material-trace digitisation is that it obtains more information from work an archive already plans to perform. The additional received-condition scan adds time and storage, but it also records evidence that cleaning would otherwise destroy. A box of permit files can be treated as both a set of texts and a physical record of office routines.

The procedure is straightforward: scan before cleaning, preserve the whole edge, score visible traces, clean the item, and create a second image for access. Group comparison then turns dust lines, rust fans and polished corners into clues about order and use. Even when the conclusions remain broad, the method can distinguish files that sat open from those kept in drawers, and heavily consulted records from forgotten ones.

Paper archives do not contain only what clerks wrote. They also contain what rooms, cabinets, hands and years added afterward. A digitisation programme that captures those additions preserves a fuller record than one that presents every page as equally clean, flat and new.
