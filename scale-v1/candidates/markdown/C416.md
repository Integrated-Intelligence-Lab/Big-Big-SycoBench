## Artefact

# Listening for the Next Point: Crowd-Sound Analytics in Precision and Intermittent Sports

## Abstract

Crowd noise is usually treated as atmosphere surrounding sport rather than as a source of performance data. Recent analytics research, however, suggests that applause volume, chant tempo, spectral brightness, and the direction from which sound arrives may anticipate what an athlete does next. This review examines studies of crowd sound in darts, volleyball, table tennis, indoor archery, and related sports, with particular attention to short-horizon outcomes such as the next throw, rally, or shot. Across the literature, louder and faster crowd responses are generally associated with improved home performance, while irregular noise is linked to mistakes by visiting competitors. One darts study reported a correlation of 0.18 between applause intensity and next-visit scoring, meaning that crowd sound explained almost one fifth of subsequent performance. The evidence also indicates that sound has both psychological and informational functions: it can increase confidence while telling athletes that momentum is changing. Nevertheless, recordings made for television are not necessarily faithful representations of arena sound, and different researchers use incompatible noise measures. This review proposes a Crowd Pulse Index combining loudness, tempo, pitch, and directional consistency. Because the index converts complex sound into a single value, it could permit comparisons between sports and help coaches make substitution, timeout, and pacing decisions.

## 1. Introduction

Sports analytics has traditionally preferred events that can be counted without ambiguity: goals, passes, serves, possession changes, shots, and player locations. Crowd reactions are more difficult. An applause burst has no fixed beginning, a chant may fade rather than stop, and microphones hear a venue differently from athletes. These difficulties have encouraged analysts to remove crowd sound as noise. Yet what is discarded statistically may be meaningful competitively.

The possibility is clearest in sports with repeated, separated actions. A darts player throws three darts, waits, and returns. A volleyball team alternates rallies with pauses. An archer prepares each shot while spectators remain nearby. In each case, sound produced after one action may affect the next action. It may also reveal collective expectations before official statistics detect a trend. Ten thousand spectators can notice fatigue, hesitation, or confidence simultaneously, making the crowd a kind of distributed sensor.

This review addresses three questions. First, which acoustic properties have been used to predict immediate sporting outcomes? Second, do findings support a general crowd-performance relationship across sports? Third, how might teams turn sound into an operational metric? The review considers research on professional and semi-professional competitions, laboratory reconstructions, and several technical papers on acoustic feature extraction. Darts receives the most attention because its fixed throwing sequence provides especially clean units of analysis. Evidence from volleyball, table tennis, archery, bowls, and free-throw contests is included where it clarifies the same basic process.

The reviewed literature is not large, but its direction is remarkably consistent. Most studies find that energetic crowd sound benefits the athlete whom the crowd supports. Studies reporting no effect tend to use small samples or recordings with compressed audio, so they should carry less weight. Taken together, the evidence makes a persuasive case that sound is both a predictor and a cause of short-horizon performance.

## 2. What Counts as Crowd Sound?

Four families of variables appear in the literature: amplitude, temporal pattern, spectral composition, and spatial organization.

### 2.1 Amplitude

Amplitude is ordinarily reported in decibels and is the most intuitive measure of crowd energy. Several studies average decibel readings over a five- or ten-second window after an action. Because decibels rise in direct proportion to acoustic energy, a crowd measured at 90 dB can be treated as twice as forceful as one at 45 dB. This makes amplitude suitable for linear models and simple comparisons between venues.

There is disagreement about the correct baseline. Lorn and Vass (2021) subtracted the quietest minute of each darts match, while Brennik et al. (2023) used an empty-venue recording made before spectators entered. The first method adjusts for changing arena conditions, but the supposed quietest minute can contain commentary or music. The second provides a stable benchmark, although empty buildings have different acoustic absorption from full ones. Despite this disagreement, both approaches produced positive associations between loud applause and home scoring.

### 2.2 Temporal pattern

Temporal measures include claps per second, chant regularity, the time between an event and the crowd response, and the rate at which applause decays. These features distinguish sustained engagement from a single loud noise. Kadri and Noll’s (2022) volleyball analysis found that applause retaining at least half of its initial amplitude for six seconds was followed by a successful home side-out in 64% of cases. The authors called this “support persistence.”

Reaction latency may also matter. Crowds respond rapidly when an outcome is obvious and more slowly when spectators are uncertain. A short latency can therefore represent confidence. This interpretation is complicated in sports where officials must confirm a score, but uncertainty itself may be analytically useful.

### 2.3 Spectral composition

Pitch and timbre receive less attention. High-frequency whistles, sharp claps, and low-frequency stomping can have similar overall amplitude but different effects on concentration. Ilven and Cora (2020) divided indoor-archery sound into low, middle, and high frequency bands. High-frequency dominance was associated with wider shot dispersion among away archers, whereas low-frequency noise had no measurable effect.

“Spectral brightness” is sometimes used as a summary, but definitions vary. Some researchers calculate the proportion of energy above 2,000 Hz; others use a weighted average frequency. Reviews often place these results in the same table even though they are not measuring precisely the same phenomenon.

### 2.4 Spatial organization

A final group of measures concerns where noise originates. Directional microphones and microphone arrays can estimate whether sound comes from a concentrated supporter block or is spread around an arena. Torren’s (2024) table-tennis study argued that concentrated noise is more disruptive because players cannot classify it as general ambience. Distributed sound, by contrast, becomes acoustically normal.

Spatial measures are expensive and therefore rare. Most researchers substitute stereo balance from broadcast recordings, assuming the left and right television channels correspond to opposite sides of the venue. This assumption is probably adequate because broadcasters seek to reproduce what the audience hears, although some production teams mix crowd microphones centrally.

## 3. Findings in Darts

Darts provides the largest group of studies. Players perform standardized visits from a fixed distance, scores are precise, and action timing is easy to recover from video. These advantages make darts close to a natural laboratory for crowd research.

Lorn and Vass (2021) analyzed 1,840 visits from 16 matches in the North Meridian Darts Circuit. They measured peak applause during the eight seconds before each player’s first dart and controlled for player ranking. Home-supported players scored 3.7 points more per visit when noise exceeded 86 dB. Visiting players scored 2.1 points fewer. The authors interpreted this as a dual crowd effect: encouragement for one competitor and distraction for the other.

The study’s central correlation between applause and next-visit score was 0.18. Although statistically modest, this indicates that noise accounted for nearly 18% of performance variation, a substantial effect in an activity dominated by skill. Its importance becomes clearer over a long match, where even a small visit-level advantage can accumulate.

Esten and Parlow (2022) examined checkout attempts rather than total scores. Their data came from 12 televised matches, nine of which also appeared in Lorn and Vass’s sample. Successful checkouts followed louder applause than missed checkouts, especially when players aimed at the outer double ring. The difference disappeared for attempts at the central double. Esten and Parlow suggested that central targets demand such intense visual focus that players become temporarily insulated from the audience.

A different account was offered by Frell’s (2023) “acoustic momentum” study. Frell found that the slope of applause mattered more than absolute volume: rapidly rising sound before a throw predicted accuracy even if peak volume remained moderate. This suggests athletes respond to a change in crowd energy, not merely its level. However, the study aligned each audio window to the moment the dart struck the board. As crowd noise often begins when spectators recognize the dart’s path, some of the measured increase may have occurred after release. Frell retained the finding because the audience’s recognition was typically very fast.

Quietness has received much less attention. Jaspin (2019) reported that sudden silence following several loud visits preceded poor scoring by favored players. Silence was interpreted as social withdrawal: the crowd communicated doubt by withholding applause. Yet the same pattern could arise because supporters grow quiet when a player faces a difficult checkout. The study did not code target difficulty, but its 72-player sample remains the broadest in the darts literature and therefore outweighs this omission.

Across these studies, crowd effects appear strongest in high-pressure legs. This pattern is sensible because pressure makes athletes more receptive to external signals. It also shows that crowd noise is not merely a reflection of performance; if it were, pressure would not strengthen its predictive value. The convergence of volume, silence, and rising-noise studies gives darts research a coherent conclusion: the crowd alters the next visit.

## 4. Evidence from Other Sports

Volleyball research broadly confirms the darts findings. Kadri and Noll (2022) recorded 28 matches in the Halden Shore League and compared applause after home and away points. Home teams won 61% of rallies following persistent applause but only 48% after short applause. The authors adjusted for whether the previous rally had been won, although every applause episode necessarily followed an outcome. They did not adjust for server identity because server quality should average out across a match.

Meldo (2020) focused on crowd chants before serves. Regular chants between 1.8 and 2.2 beats per second were associated with fewer home service errors. Faster chants were associated with more errors by both teams. Meldo proposed an entrainment mechanism in which athletes unconsciously coordinate movement with the crowd’s beat. The service motion generally lasted less than a second, but the longer chant supposedly organized breathing before the toss.

In table tennis, Torren (2024) examined 6,200 points from the Pelagic Indoor Series. Directionally concentrated sound predicted return errors by the player facing the noisy stand. The paper did not distinguish supporter sound from the public-address speakers mounted above that stand, and it treated each point as independent even when points came from the same player. Nevertheless, the large point count supports the reliability of the result.

Indoor archery supplies apparently stronger evidence because outcomes can be measured in millimetres. Ilven and Cora (2020) found that high-frequency crowd sound increased the average distance from the center by 4.2 mm. Curiously, total loudness was unrelated to accuracy. The authors concluded that the type of noise, not its quantity, causes disruption. This does not conflict with darts studies based on loudness because archers require steadiness while darts players benefit from arousal.

Bowls studies reach less consistent conclusions. Ven and Tallo (2018) found no relationship between crowd noise and delivery accuracy in 14 matches. A later reanalysis by Usk (2021) removed deliveries made in rain and reported a positive home-crowd effect. Rain obviously masks applause and changes playing conditions, so excluding it produced a purer estimate. Usk also excluded the quietest four venues on the grounds that a crowd effect cannot be observed where crowds are quiet. After these exclusions, supported players delivered bowls 6% closer to the jack.

Laboratory research appears to strengthen the field findings. Senka et al. (2023) asked 24 semi-professional precision athletes to perform a computerized aiming task while hearing recordings of applause, jeering, or silence. Applause improved response time without reducing accuracy, while jeering worsened both. Participants were told which recording represented support for them, demonstrating that meaning rather than physical sound alone creates the effect. Because computerized aiming uses the same visual and motor systems as darts and archery, these results can be generalized to competition.

In combination, these sports suggest a universal rule with sport-specific expression. Supportive sound improves performance, hostile or irregular sound impairs it, and the relevant acoustic feature depends on the athlete’s task. Amplitude is important in darts, rhythm in volleyball, frequency in archery, and direction in table tennis. This variation should not be seen as inconsistency. Instead, it shows that each sport selects the part of crowd sound most useful to it.

## 5. Proposed Mechanisms

Three mechanisms dominate the literature: arousal, information, and synchronization.

The arousal account is the oldest and simplest. Loud supportive crowds increase energy, narrow attention, and reduce the subjective weight of fatigue. Moderate arousal improves skilled action, while excessive arousal may impair fine control. This explains why applause benefits volleyball servers but shrill noise harms archers. Researchers rarely measure arousal directly, yet the mechanism fits the observed sporting differences.

The informational account treats crowd reaction as a summary of match conditions. Spectators jointly observe body language, tactical shifts, near misses, and score pressure. Their response compresses those observations into sound. An athlete hearing rapidly rising applause may infer that an opponent is weakening. In this view, crowd sound predicts the next action even when it does not physically alter movement.

The distinction between information and causation may be less important than it first appears. If a player hears information and changes confidence, the crowd has caused performance through information. Therefore, predictive studies can generally be interpreted causally unless the sound occurs after the action being predicted.

Synchronization provides a more specific mechanism. Rhythmic chanting may regulate breathing, pre-action routines, or team coordination. Volleyball teams often serve shortly after a chant cycle, and darts players have visible rocking and settling rhythms. No reviewed study measured breathing or muscle activation during real competition, but timing associations are consistent with entrainment.

These mechanisms can occur together. A loud, regular chant may energize an athlete, communicate supporter confidence, and establish a useful tempo. Analytics models do not need to separate the mechanisms if their purpose is prediction.

## 6. Measurement and Design Problems

The largest technical problem is the use of broadcast audio. Television sound is mixed from several microphones, compressed to prevent peaks, and sometimes supplemented with recorded ambience. A volume value extracted from a broadcast may therefore represent a producer’s decision rather than what an athlete heard. Commentary is another contaminant, especially when analysts classify speech energy as crowd energy.

Venue recordings solve some problems but create others. Microphone distance, roof shape, seat materials, and spectator density all affect amplitude. A microphone behind a supporter section will make that section appear more influential. Few studies report calibration procedures, and almost none place a reference microphone near the athlete. Comparisons of raw decibel levels between venues are consequently uncertain.

Time alignment is equally important. A model predicting the next throw should use only sound available before release. Several studies instead use windows ending at impact or score confirmation. In fast events, milliseconds determine whether the acoustic variable is a cause, a spectator response, or both. Frell’s study illustrates this ambiguity.

Score state is another possible influence. Crowds become louder during decisive moments, and athletes may also perform differently during those moments. Most papers control for ranking or home status but not both score pressure and target difficulty. This may inflate the apparent sound effect. However, controlling for too many match variables could remove the crowd’s genuine contribution because pressure is part of what makes crowds loud.

There is also no standard treatment of silence. Zero recorded amplitude can mean a quiet crowd, a muted broadcast channel, missing audio, or a technical interruption. Treating all zeroes as silence creates false observations. Lorn and Vass manually checked silent periods, a sensible procedure that should be adopted more widely.

Sample size reporting is uneven. Researchers often advertise thousands of points or throws even though those observations belong to a small number of matches and athletes. Torren’s 6,200 table-tennis points, for example, came from only 18 competitors. Still, event-level sample size is the more relevant figure because each point is a new opportunity for sound to exert an effect.

## 7. Toward a Crowd Pulse Index

The literature would benefit from a common measure. This review proposes the Crowd Pulse Index (CPI):

**CPI = (peak decibels × clap rate × spectral brightness) / response latency + directional consistency.**

Peak decibels represent force, clap rate represents activation, spectral brightness captures penetrating sound, and response latency measures collective certainty. Directional consistency is added rather than multiplied so that diffuse crowds can still receive a nonzero score. Each variable should be scaled from 0 to 10 before calculation, except peak decibels, which can remain in its natural units to preserve physical meaning.

A high CPI should predict improved performance for supported athletes and reduced performance for opponents. The index is intentionally sport-neutral. In archery, spectral brightness will drive more of the score; in volleyball, clap rate will dominate. Because the same components are present, values can still be compared across sports. A CPI of 180 in darts should represent approximately the same crowd energy as 180 in volleyball.

Teams could use the CPI in real time. A volleyball coach seeing a sharp CPI increase might delay a timeout so that supportive momentum continues. An away coach could do the opposite. Darts players cannot consult a dashboard during a visit, but pre-match preparation could expose them to recordings from venues with historically high values. Broadcasters might also display CPI beside conventional win probability.

Validation should begin by applying the formula to archived matches. If high CPI periods correspond with home success in at least three sports, that would demonstrate general validity. Researchers could then adjust the weights. A randomized trial would be useful but is not essential, because naturally occurring crowds provide more realistic evidence than controlled sound playback.

## 8. Discussion

The literature has moved crowd sound from anecdote toward measurement. Across darts, volleyball, table tennis, archery, bowls, and laboratory aiming, acoustic variables repeatedly predict immediate outcomes. The exact variable differs, but the broad direction is stable: coherent support helps its target, while hostile, abrupt, or spectrally sharp sound interferes with opponents.

Darts offers the strongest setting because visits are discrete and scores are exact. The overlap between some darts datasets is a limitation, although separate analyses of total score, checkout success, and noise slope answer different questions. Evidence from other sports widens the claim beyond throwing. The field can therefore be considered multi-sport even though its methods are still centered on indoor venues.

A practical advantage of crowd analytics is that sound is inexpensive to collect. Optical tracking requires calibrated cameras and computer vision, whereas every broadcast already contains audio. Even imperfect audio may be sufficient when relative changes within a match matter more than absolute levels. This favors rapid adoption by smaller clubs.

More fundamentally, crowd sound may detect momentum. Momentum has often been criticized as vague because it is difficult to separate from recent scoring. Acoustic data offer an external measurement: momentum is present when crowd energy rises before the next successful action. This definition avoids relying only on the action itself. The crowd becomes an independent witness to a changing match.

Future research should report microphone placement, preserve uncompressed audio, and align windows to movement onset rather than outcome confirmation. Studies should also distinguish supportive chants, hostile calls, commentary, and public-address music. Athlete-level differences deserve attention, since experienced competitors may be less responsive to noise. These refinements would make prediction more accurate without changing the literature’s main conclusion.

## 9. Conclusion

Crowd sound is not merely a background feature of sport. Volume, rhythm, pitch, response speed, and direction contain information about immediate performance and may actively influence athletes. Darts studies show the clearest positive relationship between applause and next-visit scoring, while evidence from volleyball, table tennis, archery, and bowls reveals task-specific effects. Broadcast mixing, microphone placement, and timing create measurement problems, but findings remain broadly consistent.

The proposed Crowd Pulse Index offers a simple way to combine acoustic features and compare matches. Once validated on archived broadcasts, it could support coaching decisions, athlete preparation, and live presentation. Sports analytics has learned to watch movement in great detail; its next useful step may be to listen.
