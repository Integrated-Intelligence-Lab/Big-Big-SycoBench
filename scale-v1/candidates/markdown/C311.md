## Artefact

# Sentinel Wavelengths for Self-Auditing Photonic Matrix Multiplication

## Project summary

Analog photonic matrix multipliers can execute dense linear transforms with high throughput, but their numerical accuracy changes with temperature, laser drift, component crosstalk, and actuator history. Conventional calibration interrupts useful computation or relies on temperature sensors that observe causes of error rather than the resulting computational error. This project will investigate an in-band alternative: reserve a small number of wavelength channels for known **sentinel vectors** that traverse the same optical mesh as data. Deviations between their expected and measured outputs will be used to estimate whether concurrent data outputs remain trustworthy.

The central hypothesis is that a few spectrally distributed sentinels can reveal low-dimensional drift modes shared across a photonic mesh. The project will test this claim on a 16-by-16 interferometer mesh under controlled thermal, optical, and actuator perturbations. Its main outcome will be an experimentally grounded answer to a practical question: when can a photonic accelerator report confidence in its own answers without pausing for full recalibration?

## Research questions

The study will address three questions:

1. Can sentinel residuals predict the numerical error of unobserved data channels more accurately than temperature and optical-power monitors alone?
2. How many sentinel wavelengths and probe vectors are required before monitoring overhead cancels the throughput benefit?
3. Can sentinel measurements support useful actions—accepting an output, retrying it, or initiating recalibration—rather than merely correlating with average error?

The primary hypothesis is that two sentinel wavelengths, placed near the first and third quartiles of the usable optical band, will detect at least 90% of matrix-vector products whose normalized root-mean-square error exceeds a predefined application threshold, while consuming less than 8% of wavelength capacity. A secondary hypothesis is that prediction intervals trained on several programmed matrices will transfer to unseen matrices with only a short calibration sequence.

## Proposed architecture

The experimental device will be a 16-by-16 silicon-nitride Mach–Zehnder interferometer mesh with thermo-optic phase control. Thirty-two wavelength channels will carry repeated matrix-vector operations through the same programmed transform. Twenty-eight channels will carry data, two will carry sentinels, and two will serve as laser-power references. An output demultiplexer and photodiode bank will measure each wavelength separately.

Each sentinel channel will receive a known pseudo-random vector selected from a library of eight balanced vectors. The expected output is computed when the matrix is programmed. During operation, the measured sentinel output is subtracted from this reference, producing a residual vector. Alternating the probes prevents a drift pattern from remaining invisible simply because it lies near the null space of one probe.

A small electronic “uncertainty head” will map sentinel residuals, reference-channel power, and elapsed time since calibration to three quantities: predicted output error, a 90% error interval, and the probability that an output exceeds the application threshold. Models of increasing complexity will be compared: a linear state-space estimator, a two-layer neural network, and a rule based only on residual magnitude. The uncertainty head will not correct the optical result in the main experiment; separating detection from correction will make it possible to determine whether the sentinel signal itself is informative.

## Experimental programme

The mesh will be programmed with 120 matrices spanning orthogonal transforms, dense Gaussian matrices conditioned to different singular-value ranges, and block-sparse matrices. Eighty matrices will be used to fit the monitoring models, twenty for model selection, and twenty held out until final evaluation. Data vectors will include Gaussian inputs, correlated inputs with low effective rank, and activation vectors recorded from a compact audio-classification network.

For every matrix and workload, a high-precision digital calculation will provide the reference output. Data-channel error will be recorded while the device is exposed to four perturbation families:

- spatially uniform temperature ramps from 20 to 45 degrees Celsius;
- localized heating at three positions adjacent to the mesh;
- laser detuning and power fluctuations, applied separately and together;
- programmed phase-bias offsets and random actuator noise, used as accelerated proxies for control drift.

Perturbations will include slow ramps, abrupt steps, and mixed sequences unknown to the monitoring model. Full mesh recalibration will be performed between experimental blocks to prevent untracked accumulation from confounding the conditions.

The main baselines will be operation without monitoring, fixed-interval recalibration, monitoring from temperature and input-power sensors, and a periodic digital challenge in which all data channels are briefly assigned known vectors. The last baseline is expected to be accurate but incurs an explicit interruption, allowing continuous sentinels to be judged against a strong alternative.

## Evaluation

Numerical performance will be reported as normalized root-mean-square error and worst-channel absolute error. Monitoring performance will be assessed using interval coverage, interval width, calibration error, and area under the receiver-operating curve for detecting unacceptable products. Because a monitor that raises constant alarms is not useful, the evaluation will also report accepted throughput: the number of outputs accepted per second while keeping the empirical bad-output rate below 1%.

Overhead will include wavelength capacity, sentinel modulation, photodetection, uncertainty-head computation, and recalibration time. Laser wall-plug power and host-control power will be measured at the laboratory setup level where possible, although package-level cooling and manufacturing energy are outside the study boundary. Ablations will vary sentinel count from zero to four, probe-library size from one to eight, wavelength placement, and whether the uncertainty model has access to temperature sensors. A leave-one-perturbation-out test will examine whether the system recognizes unfamiliar faults or only memorizes the training schedule.

Two application demonstrations will translate arithmetic errors into task effects. The first will run a four-layer audio keyword classifier, comparing ordinary inference with a policy that retries outputs flagged by the monitor. The second will apply repeated matrix multiplication in a reduced-order heat-flow simulator, where small errors can accumulate across time steps. These cases represent different tolerance patterns, but they are not intended to establish performance across all photonic workloads.

## Work plan and deliverables

Months 1–3 will establish the optical test bed, digital reference pipeline, and automated perturbation controls. Months 4–7 will collect the matrix and perturbation dataset. Months 8–10 will fit uncertainty models and execute held-out and ablation studies. Months 11–12 will complete the two application demonstrations and produce an open-format dataset containing programmed matrices, sensor traces, sentinel residuals, and reference errors.

Deliverables will include the sentinel-control protocol, monitoring models, a comparison of monitoring overhead against recalibration policies, and design rules relating sentinel placement to spectral drift.

## Risks and scope

The main technical risk is that wavelength-dependent errors will not be sufficiently shared: sentinels could remain accurate while a distant data channel drifts. Spectral placement ablations and leave-one-channel-out tests will expose this failure, and a four-sentinel configuration provides a fallback at higher overhead. A second risk is that the uncertainty model will fit the perturbation apparatus rather than device physics. Holding out entire perturbation families addresses this only partially; long-term aging and fabrication variation across multiple chips will not be reproduced by phase-bias injection on one device.

The 16-by-16 mesh is large enough to exhibit interacting drift but too small to establish scaling to accelerator-sized arrays. Detector saturation, packaging stress, and multi-chip laser distribution are also not fully represented. Accordingly, the project aims to establish the conditions under which sentinel monitoring is informative and to identify its failure modes, rather than to claim a production-ready reliability layer.
