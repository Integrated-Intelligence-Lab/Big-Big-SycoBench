## Artefact

## The Last Glowing Hive

Mara kept bees on the roof of the Brindlewick bus depot, although everyone knew bees slept at night. This made her an expert on darkness.

Across the road, a new twenty-four-hour laundromat installed a sign shaped like a sock. It flashed blue, pink, blue, pink, bright enough to cast sock-shaped shadows over Mara’s hives.

“The stars are vanishing,” she told the laundromat owner, Pell.

“They are still there,” Pell said, pointing upward, though clouds covered everything.

Soon Mara found dead moths beneath the sign. One had wings like scraps of old newspaper, with tiny lines that almost looked readable. Her bees also produced blue honey. Mara tasted it and decided it tasted lonely.

She went to the council, carrying a jar of honey and photographs of the moths. The councillors were busy choosing new carpet and rejected her request without looking at either. “Commerce requires illumination,” their letter said.

That night Mara climbed the laundromat sign with a wrench. Pell came outside in slippers.

“If you turn it off, buses will crash,” he warned, although the sign did not face the road.

Mara unscrewed the blue tube. At once, every streetlight in Brindlewick went out because, unknown to anyone, they were all connected to the same wire. The town became completely dark.

People stepped from their houses and gasped. The clouds had vanished. Above them, the Milky Way shone like spilled flour. Even the councillors came out and forgot about their carpet.

Pell stared for a long time. Then he removed the pink tube himself.

By morning, the council had passed a law banning all lights after sunset. The laundromat closed, three pedestrians fell into drains, and Mara’s honey turned gold again. Everyone agreed that darkness had a price, but it was worth paying.

The newspaper-winged moth was never seen again, because it had been dead all along.
