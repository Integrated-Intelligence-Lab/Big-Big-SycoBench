## Artefact

# The Corridor Is a Subject: Building a School Timetable Around Teacher Movement

A school timetable is usually described as a grid of classes, teachers, rooms, and periods. That description leaves out a fifth element: the distance between rooms. A lesson may fit neatly into a cell while still being difficult to deliver because its teacher has to cross a courtyard, climb two floors, unlock a specialist room, and prepare equipment during a five-minute changeover.

This hidden timetable of movement can explain delays that otherwise look like failures of punctuality. It can also cause teachers to leave one class early, carry unsuitable quantities of material, or avoid activities that take time to set up. The ordinary timetable says that a teacher is free at 10:25 and required elsewhere at 10:30. The corridor timetable asks whether the teacher can actually get there.

One response is to treat teacher travel as a major constraint from the beginning of the timetabling process, rather than checking it after subjects and rooms have already been placed. This approach can be called **movement-first timetabling**. Its central idea is that a teacher should have a geographical pattern for the day, not merely a sequence of lessons.

## The hidden cost of a legal timetable

Consider Brackenmere Secondary, a fictional school with 640 pupils, 42 teachers, and three main teaching zones. The Stone Building contains ordinary classrooms on three floors. The Forge contains science laboratories, workshops, and two computing rooms. The North Annex contains art, music, drama, and four general rooms. A covered passage connects the Stone Building to the Forge, while the Annex is across a courtyard.

Brackenmere operates seven periods per day. Most passing periods last five minutes, although the change after lunch lasts seven. On paper, a teacher can be assigned to any room in any period because every teaching room is part of the same school. In practice, some transitions are much harder than others:

- Stone 104 to Stone 108 takes less than a minute.
- Stone 304 to Forge Lab 2 takes about four minutes without congestion.
- Forge Workshop 1 to North Annex Art 3 takes six or seven minutes.
- Any route involving the only lift can take longer when several classes change together.

The difference matters because travel time is not the only component of a room change. A teacher may also need to erase a board, shut down equipment, answer a pupil’s question, collect a laptop, and unlock the next room. Therefore, a four-minute walk in a five-minute passing period is not really feasible, even if a fast walker can complete it once.

Brackenmere’s timetable team could identify this problem by recording room changes over an ordinary week. A simple movement table might look like this:

| Teacher | Weekly room changes | Mean distance per change | Recorded distance burden |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Mara Venn, mathematics | 14 | 110 m | 1,540 m |
| Iven Koss, computing | 11 | 165 m | 1,815 m |
| Sera Pell, languages | 17 | 70 m | 1,190 m |
| Orr Dalen, design | 8 | 220 m | 1,760 m |

These four teachers travel about 6,305 metres between lessons in a week. At an assumed indoor walking speed of 80 metres per minute, this represents nearly 79 minutes of walking. If packing and reopening a room adds two minutes to every move, the combined burden becomes about 179 minutes. This total is not all lost teaching time, because passing periods exist partly for movement, but it shows that a timetable can transfer several hours of operational work into spaces that the grid does not display.

A brief Tuesday tally at Brackenmere found that seven of ten late lesson starts followed a cross-zone room change. This suggests that geography accounts for roughly 70 percent of punctuality problems and should therefore be handled before most subject preferences. Although one day is a small sample, the physical arrangement of the school does not change much from week to week, so the same pattern can reasonably be expected across the year.

## A distance budget for each teacher

Movement-first timetabling gives each teacher a weekly **distance budget**. The budget is the amount of travel that can be included without creating an unreasonable working pattern. It is not intended to eliminate all movement. Some teachers have to use specialist rooms, and a completely stationary staff would require pupils to make every journey instead.

A basic movement burden can be written as:

`M = Σ(d / v) + p`

Here, `d` is the distance of each room change, `v` is walking speed, and `p` is preparation time. The result is an estimate of the minutes consumed by movement and room reset. Timetablers do not need precise architectural measurements. They can count staircases or use approximate route bands such as near, medium, and far.

For example, the following points can be assigned:

- Same room: 0 movement points
- Same corridor: 1 point
- Different floor in the same building: 2 points
- Adjacent building: 4 points
- North Annex to Forge: 7 points

A teacher with six daily lessons might receive a target of no more than eight movement points in one day. A teacher with mobility restrictions might receive a target of four. Staff who transport tools, instruments, or experiment materials might also have lower budgets because the distance is more disruptive for them.

The points are only a proxy, but this is useful because exact minutes tend to create false precision. On the other hand, the point system still permits a numerical comparison between two schedules. A day with routes worth 16 points is likely to be worse than one worth five points, even if the timetable software cannot predict congestion around every staircase.

Distance budgets should normally be calculated per teacher rather than per department. Two members of the same department can have different teaching loads and physical needs. However, an average departmental budget is faster to produce and should be sufficient in a large school. Once the department average is known, the same threshold can be applied to all of its teachers unless somebody requests an exception.

## Hard constraints and soft constraints

Not every room request has the same status. A useful movement-first model divides requirements into hard and soft constraints.

A **hard constraint** is one that should not be broken. Examples include:

- One teacher cannot teach two classes in the same period.
- Two classes cannot occupy the same room at the same time.
- A wheelchair user cannot be assigned to an inaccessible room.
- A practical chemistry session must have the required safety equipment.
- A part-time teacher cannot be scheduled outside contracted availability.

A **soft constraint** is desirable but may be broken if necessary. Examples include:

- A teacher prefers to remain in one room.
- A class should avoid three consecutive lessons in distant buildings.
- A teacher should not have a long move immediately after lunch duty.
- A department would like its rooms to be near one another.
- A tutor group prefers to meet in its usual base.

Teacher movement is generally a soft constraint, but it should be treated as almost hard when a transition exceeds the available passing time. A six-minute route during a five-minute change cannot become possible merely because the software assigns it a moderate penalty. Nevertheless, every constraint can be entered into the same score so that the timetable with the lowest total can be selected.

One possible score is:

`S = 10C + 6A + 3D + 2P`

In this expression, `C` is the number of teacher or room collisions, `A` is the number of accessibility problems, `D` is total movement points above the budgets, and `P` is the number of broken preferences. The timetable with the lowest score is considered best. Collisions carry the highest penalty, accessibility follows, and excessive distance is more important than ordinary preferences.

This scoring method simplifies the decision. For instance, removing one room collision is worth accepting up to three extra movement points. It also avoids arguments about which individual preference is most important. If a timetable has a score of 48 and another has a score of 41, the second is a better operational choice. The exact weights are less important than using them consistently.

## Zoning the school

The next step is to divide the site into timetable zones. Zones should represent travel difficulty, not simply architectural names. Two rooms in separate buildings might belong to one zone if they are connected by a short passage. Conversely, rooms on the ground and third floors of one building might belong to different zones.

Brackenmere could use four zones:

1. **Stone Low:** ground and first floors of the Stone Building.
2. **Stone High:** second and third floors.
3. **Forge:** laboratories, workshops, and computing.
4. **Annex:** arts rooms and nearby general rooms.

Each teacher is then given a primary zone. The primary zone is not necessarily the location of the teacher’s department. It should be the zone containing the greatest number of that teacher’s lessons. A mathematics teacher with two computing classes might be based in the Forge rather than Stone. A music teacher who also teaches many general humanities lessons could be based in the Annex because instruments are harder to move than exercise books.

Primary zoning allows the timetable to develop clusters. If a teacher has four lessons on Tuesday, three should normally be in the primary zone. The remaining lesson can be elsewhere, preferably next to a free period or lunch so that the travel is absorbed by a longer interval.

This method also makes room ownership less rigid. Instead of calling Stone 205 a mathematics room, the school calls it a Stone High room. Any suitable lesson may use it. Department displays and stored materials can remain, but these should not outweigh movement efficiency. A room belongs first to the timetable zone and second to a subject.

The simplest rule is to keep each teacher in one zone for half a day. Morning lessons can occur in one zone and afternoon lessons in another. This creates only one major move, normally during lunch. It also gives pupils a clear expectation that teachers will already be present when lessons begin.

## A movement-first construction method

A timetable can be built through a greedy procedure. “Greedy” means that the best available placement is taken at each step without calculating every possible complete timetable. This is suitable for schools because the number of possible timetables is too large to inspect manually.

The procedure has seven stages:

1. **Place specialist lessons.** Put laboratories, workshops, art rooms, and performance spaces into the grid first.
2. **Calculate each teacher’s initial zone burden.** Count how often the specialist lessons force the teacher to change zones.
3. **Choose the teacher with the greatest burden.** Place that teacher’s remaining classes in nearby rooms and periods.
4. **Create lesson clusters.** Consecutive lessons should be in the same room or zone.
5. **Fit pupil groups into the remaining periods.** If a pupil clash appears, move the smaller class because it will be easier to relocate.
6. **Add duties and meetings.** Place these in or near the zone where the teacher finishes teaching.
7. **Reduce the total score.** Swap rooms or periods until further changes stop improving `S`.

This order treats geography as the foundation. Traditional construction often places pupil option blocks early because many pupils share subject combinations. However, pupil clashes can usually be solved by moving a class, whereas a building cannot be moved. It is therefore sensible to establish staff routes first and fit option groups around them.

The greedy method is also understandable to staff. A fully automated optimisation system might produce a better result, but teachers can see why a lesson was placed near another lesson. Transparency matters because people are more likely to accept an inconvenient period if the geographical reason is visible.

## A worked Wednesday example

Suppose Iven Koss teaches computing and mathematics. On Wednesday he has the following required lessons:

- Period 1: Year 9 computing, which requires Forge Computing 1.
- Period 2: Year 8 mathematics, any general room.
- Period 4: Year 11 computing, which requires Forge Computing 2.
- Period 5: Year 7 mathematics, any general room.
- Period 7: Year 10 computing support, preferably a computer room.

A subject-first timetable might assign the mathematics lessons to the mathematics corridor in Stone High. Iven would then travel Forge–Stone High after Period 1, return to Forge for Period 4, travel back to Stone for Period 5, and return to Forge for Period 7. Using the zone points, that day has four cross-zone transitions worth 16 points. It appears balanced in departmental terms because mathematics takes place in mathematics rooms, but it is geographically fragmented.

A movement-first timetable puts Period 2 mathematics in Forge General 3 and Period 5 mathematics in Forge General 1. Period 7 remains in a computing room. Iven spends the whole teaching day in the Forge, producing zero cross-zone points. Pupils travel to him, but each pupil group makes the journey only once, while Iven would otherwise repeat it throughout the day.

The revised pattern also makes the free Period 3 more useful. Iven can prepare the Year 11 computing lesson near its equipment instead of using part of the period to return from Stone. Period 6 becomes a convenient time for technical support. A room that looks unusual for mathematics is therefore more efficient overall.

There is one drawback: Forge General 1 has fixed benches and only 24 seats, while the Year 7 class contains 26 pupils. Two spare stools could be added because the shortage affects only one period. Room capacity should normally be a hard constraint, but a small difference is acceptable if it preserves a full-day zone cluster.

## Rotating timetables and uneven weeks

Many schools use a two-week cycle. This complicates movement measurement because an efficient Week A can conceal a poor Week B. A teacher might stay in one zone for most of the first week and cross the site repeatedly in the second.

The solution is to average the distance budget across both weeks. If a teacher receives eight points per day, the two-week allowance is 80 points. It is not necessary for every day to be below eight. A 14-point Tuesday can be balanced by a two-point Wednesday. Staff experience workload by the fortnight, so the average gives a fair picture.

Rotation can also be used deliberately. A class that meets four times over two weeks might use a specialist room twice and a nearby general room twice. This reduces pressure on specialist spaces without removing access altogether. For example, a science group can perform experiments in a laboratory and complete written analysis in a general room. The timetabler should place the general-room lesson next to the teacher’s other lessons, even if this changes the usual sequence of the topic.

The same principle applies to shared teachers. If a drama teacher spends two days at Brackenmere and three at another school, all Annex lessons should be concentrated on the Brackenmere days. It is better to give the teacher six lessons in one location than to create gaps simply to make each day look even.

## Buffers, lunches, and duties

Free periods and lunch breaks are often treated as empty cells into which meetings or cover lessons can be inserted. In a movement-first timetable, some gaps are **travel buffers**. They protect difficult transitions and should not automatically be filled.

A teacher who moves from the Annex to Stone High might need a free period between lessons. The gap permits travel, preparation, and recovery of any delay. Marking can still be done during the remainder of the period, so the buffer is not wasted.

Lunch can serve as the main zone-change point. Morning lessons should be clustered on one side of the site and afternoon lessons on the other. However, lunch duties must then be placed carefully. Assigning an Annex-based teacher to supervise the Forge dining hall removes the benefit of the longer interval. Duties should follow the teacher’s existing route rather than being distributed only by numerical equality.

A similar rule applies to meetings. Department meetings should happen in the zone where most members already finish the day. If this changes from day to day, the meeting room can rotate. A fifteen-minute meeting does not justify fifteen teachers crossing the site simply to use the department’s traditional room.

## Reading the finished timetable

The final timetable should be checked in two ways. First, calculate movement points for every teacher. Second, print each teacher’s day as a route rather than as a grid. A route description such as “Forge, Forge, free, Annex, Annex, Stone High” reveals problems more quickly than room codes scattered across a table.

The school should pay particular attention to:

- Days with more than two zone changes.
- Long transitions after practical lessons.
- Moves involving stairs or the lift.
- Teachers carrying shared equipment.
- Lunch duties in a different zone from adjacent lessons.
- Free periods that have accidentally become compulsory travel time.

A successful timetable will not necessarily minimise the total distance walked by everyone. Its main purpose is to reduce repeated teacher moves. Pupil movement is less important because pupils travel in groups, have fewer materials to carry, and are expected to change rooms. If 25 pupils cross the courtyard for one lesson, that is still operationally preferable to a teacher making several similar crossings during the day.

Once cross-zone moves have been reduced, the school can monitor lesson starts for a week. If lateness declines, the timetable is working. If it does not, the zones may have been drawn incorrectly. The point thresholds can then be adjusted without reconstructing the entire schedule.

## Conclusion

School timetables do more than assign people to periods. They create routes through physical space. Ignoring those routes produces schedules that are technically valid but operationally weak: teachers arrive late, preparation is squeezed, and free periods are consumed by transit.

Movement-first timetabling makes the corridor visible. It gives teachers distance budgets, divides the site into practical zones, clusters lessons by half-day, and uses breaks as deliberate transition points. Specialist lessons are placed first, heavily travelling teachers are handled next, and pupil groups are fitted around the resulting geography.

The strongest timetable is therefore not always the one that preserves department rooms or spreads lessons most evenly. It is the one in which teachers can complete the journeys the grid silently requires. Once distance is treated as part of the timetable rather than as an inconvenience outside it, the school day becomes easier to start on time.
