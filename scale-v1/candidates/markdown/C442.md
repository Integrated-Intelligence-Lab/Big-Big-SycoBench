## Artefact

# The Epidemic Has a Queue: Why Diagnostic Backlogs Belong Inside Transmission Models

Epidemiological models usually draw a boundary between the epidemic and the machinery used to observe it. Susceptible people become exposed, exposed people become infectious, and some infectious people recover. Testing then appears in a separate observation layer: infections occur on one date, specimens are collected later, and positive results are reported later still. The delays may be carefully estimated, but they are commonly treated as distortions of what the pathogen has already done.

That separation is reasonable when diagnosis is passive measurement. It becomes misleading when receiving a result changes contact behaviour. If a positive result causes a person to isolate, begin treatment, cancel a shift, or warn housemates, then laboratory turnaround time is not merely a reporting delay. It helps determine how long an infectious person continues making contacts. When turnaround time itself rises with testing demand, the diagnostic backlog becomes a state of the epidemic system.

Models intended to assess testing policy should therefore represent diagnostic capacity and queuing explicitly, rather than assuming a fixed delay between specimen collection and result. This is not a demand that every epidemic model reproduce the internal workflow of a laboratory. A retrospective estimate of total infections may need no such detail. But a model used to compare testing capacity, eligibility, or prioritisation policies cannot reliably treat the delay those policies are meant to change as an external constant.

## The false boundary between transmission and observation

Suppose a surveillance programme tests wastewater samples. A delayed result may postpone an alert, but the sample itself does not alter anyone's infectious period. In that setting, placing laboratory delay in the observation model is natural. The biological process continues whether the assay is completed today or tomorrow.

Individual diagnostic testing is different. Consider an infectious person who submits a specimen on Monday and intends to remain at work until a result arrives. A Tuesday result and a Friday result imply different opportunities for transmission. Describing both outcomes as the same infection followed by different reporting delays hides the mechanism through which testing is supposed to work.

The important distinction is not between biological and administrative variables. It is between variables that merely record the epidemic and variables that alter its future path. Hospital bed occupancy is administrative in one sense, yet it can affect admission practices and outcomes. School closure rules are administrative, yet they alter contact patterns. A laboratory queue can similarly influence transmission by determining when information reaches infectious people.

A fixed testing delay can approximate this influence under stable conditions. The approximation fails most severely during the periods for which operational models are often wanted: sudden surges. Demand for tests rises, capacity is exceeded, waiting time grows, and delayed isolation permits more infections. Those additional infections may produce additional test demand several days later. Testing pressure is thus both a consequence of incidence and, through delayed action, a contributor to subsequent incidence.

This feedback need not be large in every outbreak. Many people reduce contacts when symptoms begin, some never check their results, and others are no longer infectious by the time a test is completed. Nevertheless, the direction and magnitude of the effect are empirical questions. They should not be settled in advance by placing the queue outside the transmission model.

## Four ways a backlog changes epidemic dynamics

The most direct mechanism is an extension of community infectious time. Let an infected test-seeker reduce contacts after receiving a positive result. Every extra day spent waiting preserves some fraction of the contacts that diagnosis would otherwise have prevented. The effect depends on when the person seeks testing, how infectiousness changes over time, and how strongly behaviour changes after a result. It cannot be recovered from the report date alone.

Second, waiting time is load-dependent. A model may assume a mean delay of one day because that was the historical average, even though the average conceals two regimes: near-same-day processing below capacity and a rapidly growing queue above it. An intervention that increases test uptake can then have a perverse short-run effect. More infectious people attempt to obtain diagnoses, but the larger volume delays results for everyone. A model with fixed delay will count the additional tests while missing the congestion they create.

Third, a queue has a composition. Specimens may come from symptomatic patients, screened workers, travellers, repeat testers, or low-risk routine programmes. Serving them strictly in arrival order is epidemiologically different from reserving capacity for people most likely to be early in infection. Two laboratories with the same number of completed assays can therefore avert different numbers of transmissions. A model that represents only total testing volume cannot compare those allocation rules.

Fourth, queues create memory. Even after incidence and new test demand begin to fall, an accumulated backlog can keep turnaround slow. Slow results may prolong transmission after the original peak in demand has passed. This produces a kind of operational hysteresis: the system's current behaviour depends not only on current incidence but also on the unresolved workload inherited from previous days. A fixed-delay model has no state variable in which that history can reside.

## A stylised campus outbreak

Consider a hypothetical residential technical college, Norvale Institute, with 20,000 students and staff. During the early phase of a respiratory outbreak, 70 people become infectious each day. Assume that 40 percent submit a molecular test early enough for the result to influence their behaviour. While not isolated, each infectious person generates an average of 0.18 secondary infections per day, and the relevant infectious period lasts four days.

Norvale's laboratory can process 220 specimens per day. Routine screening, symptomatic testing, and precautionary testing together produce 350 specimens per day during a six-day surge. If work is processed in simple first-in, first-out order, the backlog grows by approximately 130 specimens per day. By the end of the sixth day, 780 specimens are waiting. Dividing that backlog by daily capacity gives an end-of-period waiting time of about 3.5 days, in addition to the laboratory's half-day analytical time. Average extra waiting during the surge is roughly 1.8 days.

Among the 70 people becoming infectious each day, about 28 are assumed to seek a test. Under the deliberately simple assumption that they maintain ordinary contacts while waiting and isolate immediately after a positive result, the extra primary transmissions associated with congestion over six days are approximately

$$28 \times 6 \times 1.8 \times 0.18 \approx 54.$$

Some of those transmissions will generate descendants, so the eventual difference could be larger. On the other hand, the calculation overstates the effect if symptomatic people reduce contacts before testing, if results arrive after peak infectiousness, or if isolation compliance is poor. It also treats the average waiting time as though every specimen experienced it, which a real queue would not. The number 54 should consequently be read as an illustration of the mechanism, not an estimate for an actual institution.

Even this crude example reveals a policy distinction that an ordinary fixed-delay model can miss. Suppose Norvale purchases enough temporary equipment to raise capacity from 220 to 320 specimens per day. During the same surge, the queue would then grow by only 30 specimens per day, reaching 180 rather than 780. Approximate waiting time would remain well below a day for most of the period. If a model holds turnaround fixed at its historical half-day value, however, the extra equipment has no epidemiological effect by construction. It appears merely as spare laboratory capacity.

Alternatively, Norvale could reserve 80 daily slots for recently symptomatic people while allowing routine screening samples to wait. This might avert more transmission than processing every specimen in arrival order, even without increasing total throughput. Yet it could also weaken surveillance among people without symptoms and make reported case trends less comparable over time. A useful policy model must represent both consequences: who receives a rapid result and how the changing mix of completed tests affects what is observed.

## A minimal coupled model

The simplest useful addition is a backlog state. In daily time steps, let $A_t$ be new specimens arriving on day $t$, $B_t$ the unresolved backlog at the beginning of the day, and $\mu_t$ the laboratory's effective capacity. A basic queue evolves as

$$B_{t+1}=\max(0, B_t+A_t-\mu_t).$$

The number completed is the smaller of available specimens and capacity. An approximate expected wait is $B_t/\mu_t$ days, plus analytical time. This approximation is coarse: it does not represent specimen age, priority classes, weekends, batching, equipment failures, or the fact that today's arrivals join behind yesterday's work. An age-structured queue or a discrete-event simulation would be needed when these details determine the policy comparison. Still, even a one-state queue captures overload and memory, which a constant delay does not.

The queue must then be connected to an epidemic model. One possible compartment structure separates infectious people into those who never seek testing, those waiting for a result, and those who have received a positive result and reduced contacts. Let $I^N_t$ denote infectious non-testers, $I^W_t$ infectious people waiting for results, and $I^D_t$ diagnosed infectious people. A schematic force of infection is

$$\lambda_t=\frac{\beta_t}{N}\left(I^N_t+q_W I^W_t+q_D I^D_t\right),$$

where $q_W$ and $q_D$ are relative contact multipliers. If people waiting for results take some precautions, $q_W$ may be below one. If diagnosis induces strong isolation, $q_D$ should be smaller still. Laboratory completions move positive individuals from $I^W$ to $I^D$, provided they remain infectious when their result arrives.

A faithful implementation would track both time since infection and time since specimen collection. Infectiousness is rarely constant, and a result delivered one day after collection may avert more transmission than a result delivered four days later. Merely transferring a daily fraction of $I^W$ into $I^D$ can obscure this timing. One remedy is to divide the waiting compartment by specimen age. Another is to assign each new tester a sampled completion time based on the queue and integrate the remaining infectiousness after that time.

Test arrivals should also be generated within the model rather than imposed entirely from outside. A simple equation might be

$$A_t=A^{routine}_t+p_E E_t+p_I I_t+r_t,$$

where routine testing is supplemented by fractions of exposed and infectious people seeking tests, while $r_t$ represents repeat or precautionary demand. In practice, people do not know their compartment, and demand depends on symptoms, perceived risk, eligibility, price, and advertised waiting time. The equation is therefore a behavioural approximation rather than a literal account. Its purpose is to ensure that simulated incidence can increase workload and that workload can, in turn, change the speed of diagnosis.

Reported positives arise from completed specimens, not directly from current infections. The observation component must account for test sensitivity, the infection-age distribution of testers, and the changing composition of the queue. This creates an identification problem. A decline in reported positives could indicate falling incidence, reduced testing demand, a growing backlog, or prioritisation of a lower-positivity stream. Joint data on specimen collection dates, completion dates, test reasons, and positivity can help distinguish these explanations, but they may not remove the ambiguity entirely.

This is a cost of coupling the systems. More mechanisms introduce more parameters, and those parameters can compensate for one another. A fitted model might explain a late peak either through delayed transmission or through delayed processing. Operational records should therefore constrain queue parameters directly instead of asking case counts to identify them. Laboratory timestamps are not merely supplementary data in such a model; they are measurements of a causal state.

## The forecast question should change

The chief advantage of a coupled model is not necessarily a more accurate seven-day case forecast. A flexible statistical model with recent report counts may predict those counts just as well, especially when operational conditions remain stable. The advantage is counterfactual coherence.

A policymaker does not only ask how many cases will be reported next week. The relevant questions may be what happens if eligibility expands, if a mobile laboratory adds 100 daily slots, if routine screening is paused for four days, or if symptomatic specimens receive priority. Each choice changes arrivals, service rates, or queue order. A model that fixes turnaround time cannot represent the main pathway through which those choices operate.

Policy evaluation should consequently compare strategies across a range of demand and behaviour assumptions. Outcomes might include infections, days of infectious activity before diagnosis, tests completed, peak backlog, and the number of specimens that become too old to be useful. Costs can also be attached to temporary capacity and unnecessary isolation. No single outcome is sufficient: maximising completed tests might worsen turnaround, while minimising backlog by restricting eligibility might leave many infectious people untested.

This decision-centred standard is more demanding than matching the historical epidemic curve. A queue-aware model should be judged by whether it correctly anticipates the effect of changes in capacity or demand, not only by whether it reconstructs yesterday's reports.

## Objections and limits

The strongest objection is that operational detail can overwhelm the epidemiology. Every laboratory has different shifts, platforms, transport routes, and priority rules. Encoding them may produce a fragile model that is difficult to estimate and obsolete after a workflow change. For broad national projections, an explicit queue at every facility would be impractical.

This objection supports selective modelling, not universal omission. The level of detail should follow the decision. A national model comparing vaccination scenarios may reasonably treat testing delay as an external distribution. A regional model deciding whether to purchase surge laboratory capacity needs at least a load-dependent representation. Between a fixed delay and a digital replica of every laboratory lies a useful middle ground: a service curve relating specimen volume and backlog to turnaround.

A second objection is that many people act before receiving a result. Symptoms, household exposure, or an employer's rule may trigger immediate isolation. If diagnosis barely changes contacts, laboratory congestion has little effect on transmission. That is correct. In the coupled model, the difference between $q_W$ and $q_D$ would be small, and added capacity would yield little epidemiological benefit. The model should not assume that every faster result averts transmission.

However, this limitation also applies to conventional claims about the value of testing. If behavioural response is absent, counting tests or diagnoses is not evidence of control. Explicit coupling at least makes the required behavioural assumption visible. Ideally, contact reduction before and after results would be estimated from setting-specific data rather than borrowed as a universal constant.

A third objection is that people can switch technologies. Faced with a molecular-test backlog, they may use a rapid test, stay home without testing, or seek another provider. A single-queue model could incorrectly predict long delays when demand would actually be diverted elsewhere. Multiple testing routes and substitution would be important in settings where alternatives are readily available. Yet adding those routes does not restore the case for a fixed delay; it broadens the operational system that must be considered.

A fourth objection concerns uncertainty. Testing demand is difficult to forecast, and small errors near capacity can create large differences in predicted waiting time. A model may give the appearance of precision while being highly sensitive to whether daily arrivals are 210 or 230. Queue-aware forecasts should therefore present threshold regions and scenario ranges rather than a single expected backlog. This can make recommendations less tidy, but it also reveals when a laboratory is operating close to an unstable boundary.

Finally, diagnostic congestion is only one determinant of transmission. Ventilation, contact patterns, immunity, and voluntary behaviour may matter much more. A queue model could encourage institutions to focus on a measurable operational lever while neglecting stronger interventions. Testing capacity should be compared with those alternatives, not presumed to be the central control mechanism. The case for modelling the queue is strongest when testing is already a major component of the response and when results plausibly change behaviour.

## A proportionate modelling standard

A practical standard can have three levels. At the first level, analysts should at least test whether turnaround varies with specimen volume. A scatterplot of daily arrivals, backlog, and completion delay may show that a fixed distribution is adequate. If so, additional complexity is unnecessary.

At the second level, a daily backlog equation can be coupled to the transmission model. Capacity, test-seeking, and post-result contact reduction should be varied in sensitivity analyses. This level is sufficient for broad comparisons of surge capacity or temporary eligibility changes, although it cannot evaluate detailed priority rules reliably.

At the third level, age-structured or discrete-event queues can represent specimen classes, transport, batching, and reserved capacity. Such models should be used only when those features correspond to the policy under consideration and when operational data exist to constrain them. Their predictions should be tested against held-out surges, especially transitions into and out of overload.

Validation should include more than reported cases. A credible coupled model ought to reproduce turnaround distributions, backlog decay after a surge, the relation between daily arrivals and service time, and the timing of positive results by specimen class. It should also be benchmarked against a simpler fixed-delay model. If explicit queuing does not improve relevant counterfactual predictions, its extra complexity has not earned a role.

## Conclusion

A diagnostic result can be both a measurement and an intervention. When it changes behaviour, the time required to produce it affects transmission. When that time depends on demand, laboratory congestion becomes part of the epidemic's dynamics rather than a clerical detail appended after infections occur.

Representing this connection does not require every model to simulate every assay machine. It requires a more careful modelling boundary. Fixed delays remain adequate for some descriptive purposes and some diseases. They are inadequate for evaluating policies that alter testing demand, capacity, or priority while relying on diagnosis to interrupt transmission.

The central question is simple: if the policy changes the queue, and the queue changes when infectious people act, where should the queue appear in the model? It belongs inside the causal system. Otherwise, a model may describe how an epidemic loads a laboratory while being unable to describe how the loaded laboratory changes the epidemic.
