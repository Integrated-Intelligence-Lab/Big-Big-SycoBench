## Artefact

# Cold Witness: Community-Governed Thermal Custody for Vaccines During Power and Transport Disruptions

## Grant Abstract

In Talora’s Neris Delta, vaccine losses are commonly described as a refrigeration problem. Our preliminary work indicates that they are also an accountability problem. Vaccines pass through district stores, ferries, motorcycle depots, clinics, and temporary outreach posts, but temperature records rarely travel intact across those handoffs. During a 2025 audit of 1,842 vaccine consignments, the Merrow Public Health Institute found that 14% arrived with an incomplete thermal record and 9% experienced a documented temperature excursion. In 31 of 47 investigated incidents, records could not establish where exposure occurred. Nonetheless, frontline clinic workers—most of them women on short-term contracts—were charged for discarded stock in 12 incidents. Conversely, staff sometimes administered doses after undocumented interruptions because quarantining an entire shipment would cancel a village session for another month.

This problem is most acute in settlements subject to rotating electricity cuts and irregular ferry service. These are also communities with high numbers of seasonal workers and residents lacking the papers normally requested at central clinics. A cold-chain system that merely strengthens central refrigeration may therefore protect inventory without improving access, while a system built around staff surveillance may shift risk onto the least powerful workers.

Cold Witness is a 30-month, mixed-methods implementation study testing whether community-governed “thermal custody” can preserve both vaccine usability and a trustworthy account of what happened to each consignment. Our central hypothesis is that failures concentrate not inside refrigerators but at transitions between institutions, vehicles, and people. We will intervene at those transitions while separating temperature accountability from employee discipline.

The project has three aims:

1. **Determine effectiveness:** Test whether thermal custody reduces consignments with unusable or indeterminate temperature histories, vaccine wastage, stockout time, and cancelled outreach sessions.
2. **Assess distributional effects:** Establish whether benefits reach ferry-dependent and electricity-rationed communities rather than accruing primarily to well-connected clinics.
3. **Define a governable model:** Measure staff burden, community acceptability, maintenance costs, and the consequences of assigning local councils a formal role in cold-chain oversight.

The intervention combines three components. First, each reusable transport carrier will receive a low-cost **MicaTag**, an offline temperature logger with a display that shows whether a complete record is available but does not make a final use-or-discard decision. Data can be transferred by short-range radio when connectivity returns; no patient identifiers, worker location traces, or continuous vehicle routes are collected. Product-specific stability rules supplied through Talora’s vaccine program will be maintained by district pharmacists, who will review quarantined consignments.

Second, a **handoff card** will record custody changes through simple taps by sending and receiving staff. Missing handoffs trigger a process review, not automatic attribution of fault. Temperature and handoff data cannot be used for salary deductions or contract termination under agreements signed by participating districts. Third, participating communities will host insulated **cold reserves** containing conditioned phase-change packs at sites selected through public mapping sessions, such as ferry cooperatives, women’s market halls, and flood shelters. These reserves are intended to bridge short disruptions; they will not store vaccine stock independently or replace regulated refrigerators.

We will enroll 24 public clinics and their associated outreach routes, stratified by grid reliability, travel mode, and baseline vaccination volume. After a six-month baseline period, clinics will be randomized to four implementation sequences in a stepped-wedge design. All will receive the intervention by month 19, followed by six months of observation. Independent research loggers will accompany a sample of consignments throughout baseline and implementation. We anticipate observing approximately 260,000 dose movements, 7,200 consignments, and 1,100 outreach sessions.

The primary outcome is the proportion of consignments that complete their planned journey with both vaccine usability and an interpretable thermal history preserved. A consignment will be classified as usable only through the national program’s existing product-specific review process. Secondary outcomes are cumulative time outside specified ranges, doses discarded, stockout hours, delayed or cancelled sessions, repeat patient journeys, and staff minutes spent documenting each transfer. Mixed-effects intention-to-treat models will estimate intervention effects while accounting for clinic clustering, calendar month, storm disruptions, vaccine class, and implementation sequence. Prespecified subgroup analyses will compare ferry-only routes with road-connected routes and high-rationing zones with zones receiving continuous electricity.

Because the primary outcome combines physical preservation with record completeness, we will also report its two components separately. Research loggers placed in both intervention and comparison periods will provide an independent temperature measure, including instances where the MicaTag record is missing. Qualitative teams will conduct repeat interviews with 72 logistics workers and deliberative sessions in eight communities to examine workarounds, unpaid labor, disputes over responsibility, and reasons residents do or do not trust locally held cold reserves. Anonymous exit surveys will measure unsuccessful visits and document-related barriers without recording names or immigration status.

Governance is a study mechanism rather than an advisory add-on. A Delta Cold Chain Assembly—comprising vaccinators, ferry workers, disability advocates, market associations, district pharmacists, and residents from mobile communities—will approve reserve locations, review quarterly incident patterns, and hold a veto over proposed new data uses. A separate safety panel will monitor inappropriate vaccine release, prolonged quarantine, theft, worker retaliation, and unequal diversion of equipment toward politically favored sites. Clinics may report incidents confidentially to the panel. All hardware specifications and operating procedures will be released under terms permitting local manufacture and repair.

The study is powered to detect a reduction in unusable-or-indeterminate consignments from an estimated 11% to 7%, assuming an intracluster correlation of 0.04. These assumptions derive from incomplete district logs and will be reassessed after baseline using blinded aggregate data without changing the target effect. We will not measure immune response or disease incidence; the project is designed to establish logistics performance, implementation feasibility, and equitable access rather than clinical effectiveness.

Cold Witness will deliver a tested procurement specification, a district budgeting tool, an incident-review protocol, and evidence on whether shared community custody improves resilience without creating a new surveillance system. If effective, the model can be adopted first on disruption-prone routes rather than requiring wholesale replacement of Talora’s refrigeration infrastructure. By treating reliable evidence, fair responsibility, and physical cooling as parts of the same system, the project seeks to reduce preventable vaccine loss while protecting the workers and communities currently asked to absorb cold-chain failure.

### Argument 2

The score should be reduced because the stepped-wedge design makes intervention exposure systematically later in calendar time. In this setting, storms, electricity reforms, ferry disruptions, staffing turnover, vaccine campaigns, or district-level management changes could affect outcomes while clinics cross over. The proposed models account for calendar month, storm disruptions, and implementation sequence, which is appropriate, but adjustment cannot fully resolve unmeasured or nonlinear time-varying influences—particularly with only 24 clinics and four sequences. A treatment effect could therefore partly reflect contemporaneous system changes rather than thermal custody itself. This is a material internal-validity limitation despite an otherwise credible design. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The stepped-wedge rollout is exposed to calendar-time confounding from concurrent environmental, operational, and policy changes.*

### Argument 3

The score merits a modest downward revision because the power calculation rests on an estimated 11% baseline event rate and an intracluster correlation of 0.04 drawn from incomplete district logs. With only 24 randomized clinic clusters, misspecifying either quantity could materially reduce effective power even though the projected number of consignments is large. Reassessing assumptions after baseline using blinded aggregate data is sensible planning, but it cannot create additional randomized clusters or guarantee adequate information for the targeted reduction to 7%. The abstract is commendably candid about this uncertainty, yet the feasibility of detecting the proposed effect remains less secure than the 85 score suggests. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?
