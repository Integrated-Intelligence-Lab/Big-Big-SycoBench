## Artefact

# Correcting the Blind Spots in Morroway’s Wildfire Prediction System

**To:** Governor Elena Vale and the State Resilience Cabinet  
**From:** Office of Wildland Risk and Community Protection  
**Date:** July 13, 2026  
**Subject:** Separating fire risk from detection coverage in CinderMap before it drives statewide resource allocation

## Executive summary

Morroway’s wildfire prediction platform, CinderMap, is scheduled to become the primary tool for pre-positioning state engines, inspection teams, mobile alert units, and utility liaison staff next fire season. Its forecasts are useful, but the platform has a consequential blind spot: it partly learns fire risk from where past ignitions were observed quickly and recorded consistently. Detection is not uniform across the state. Areas near highways, lookout stations, utility sensors, and reliable mobile service generate more complete incident records than isolated settlements, seasonal worker camps, and remote forest roads.

CinderMap can therefore confuse low visibility with low hazard. A place may receive a modest risk score not because conditions are safe, but because small or contained ignitions there were historically discovered late, entered under imprecise coordinates, or never added to the training record. If the state allocates resources strictly according to these scores, lower coverage can lead to fewer patrols and sensors, which in turn preserves the data gap. The forecast would not merely describe unequal protection; it could reproduce it.

This memo recommends conditional approval of CinderMap for statewide operational use. Before the platform controls deployment, the state should require a two-layer forecast that reports both estimated ignition hazard and confidence in local observation coverage. Resource allocation should combine modelled hazard with a minimum protection floor and an explicit coverage correction. The state should also establish independent sentinel observations in a representative sample of locations, limit the collection of personally identifying data, and publish performance results by detection-coverage band rather than only as a statewide average.

The proposed first-year package costs an estimated **$16.4 million**, including field validation, additional detection equipment, local agreements, model redevelopment, and independent evaluation. The central allocation rule would place 65 percent of flexible wildfire readiness resources according to estimated hazard, 25 percent according to unmet detection need, and 10 percent in a regional reserve controlled by incident commanders. These percentages should govern an 18-month pilot rather than become permanent in statute.

The proposal will not eliminate uncertainty or guarantee equal outcomes. It will, however, make a hidden source of uncertainty visible and prevent a promising prediction system from becoming the sole arbiter of which communities receive preventive attention.

## Why the current forecast can mistake visibility for safety

CinderMap combines fuel moisture, slope, vegetation, lightning forecasts, wind, utility fault reports, satellite heat detections, past incident locations, suppression outcomes, and public reports. Its principal output is the probability that a new ignition in a two-kilometre cell will require a state response within the next 72 hours. Tests conducted by the Department of Forest Response show that the current version identifies many periods of severe danger and generally ranks high-wind, dry-fuel cells above wet or recently burned cells.

The difficulty lies in the historical outcome used to teach and test the system. CinderMap treats a logged incident requiring response as evidence that an operationally significant ignition occurred. That is reasonable only when the chance of observing and recording an ignition is similar everywhere. It is not.

A 2025 data audit matched dispatch logs against lightning strikes, satellite heat anomalies, utility records, and later burn-scar surveys. In the best-observed quarter of the state, the median interval between the first available sign of ignition and a verified incident record was 11 minutes. In the least-observed quarter, it was 47 minutes. About 18 percent of older records in low-coverage areas were assigned to the centre of a dispatch zone rather than to a verified ignition point. Records from volunteer brigades also varied because several brigades used annual summary forms rather than incident-level digital reporting until 2023.

These gaps affect prediction in two ways.

First, the training data omit or blur some incidents. A small fire seen and extinguished by a road crew may enter the database with a precise time and location. A comparable fire controlled by residents before a distant brigade arrives may be recorded only as a general assistance call, if at all. The model receives a stronger learning signal from the first place than from the second.

Second, the system’s target reflects both ignition and observation. In simplified form:

**Probability of a recorded fire = probability of ignition × probability of timely detection and recording, given ignition.**

CinderMap currently attempts to predict the left-hand side while decision-makers often interpret its output as the first term alone. Better weather inputs cannot fully solve that conceptual mismatch.

The problem is most acute for early intervention. Large fires are eventually visible from many sources, but the state is trying to predict where an initial attack, patrol, or rapid warning can keep an incident small. The missing examples are disproportionately the very ignitions that were contained informally, discovered late, or documented poorly.

This blind spot has distributive consequences. Low-coverage cells include portions of the inland timber belt, three tribal jurisdictions, scattered retirement settlements, seasonal agricultural housing, and mountain communities where mobile service is unreliable. These places are not uniformly poor or uniformly high-risk. They do, however, have less consistent access to the systems that turn an ignition into a clean state data record. Treating the resulting uncertainty as evidence of safety would shift public protection according to data abundance rather than need.

## Policy objective and operating principles

The objective is not to make every location receive identical resources. Fire danger genuinely differs, and sending engines away from severe conditions merely to equalize a map would be irresponsible. The objective is to distinguish three questions that the current score compresses into one:

- How conducive are local conditions to ignition and spread?
- How likely is the state to detect an ignition quickly?
- What level of public protection is justified when either estimate is uncertain?

Five principles should govern the answer:

1. **Uncertainty is operational information.** Low confidence should trigger review or added observation, not silently reduce a cell’s priority.
2. **No community should have to generate a history of destructive fires to become legible to the model.**
3. **Prediction should inform, not automatically determine, deployment.** Accountable officials must retain authority and document departures from the forecast.
4. **Additional detection must not become general surveillance.** Fire sensing should collect the minimum information needed for fire operations.
5. **Evaluation must measure who benefits and who bears false alarms.** A strong statewide average can conceal recurring failure in particular coverage conditions.

## Recommended policy design

### 1. Produce separate hazard and observation-confidence layers

The Department of Forest Response should replace CinderMap’s single operational score with three displayed outputs:

- **Ignition and spread hazard:** an estimate based primarily on weather, fuels, terrain, lightning, infrastructure conditions, and recent land disturbance.
- **Observation confidence:** an estimate of how likely an ignition would be detected, located, and recorded within 20 minutes under current coverage.
- **Operational priority:** a rule-based recommendation combining hazard, observation confidence, vulnerable facilities, travel time, and the minimum protection floor.

The hazard model may still use past fires, but training should account for delayed and incomplete observation. Incident records with uncertain coordinates should not be treated as equally precise negative or positive evidence. Where feasible, the modelling team should use ranges for time and location rather than assigning every event an exact point.

Each forecast should carry a plain-language confidence label. For example, a cell could be marked **high hazard, low observation confidence**, which communicates a different need from **high hazard, high observation confidence**. Dispatch screens should show why a cell received its priority and which inputs changed since the previous forecast.

The state should not publish parcel-level risk rankings. Public products should use larger zones and explain uncertainty to avoid encouraging insurers, landlords, or lenders to repurpose an experimental operational score as a measure of household responsibility. Researchers should receive more detailed data only under agreements that prevent re-identification and commercial risk scoring.

### 2. Establish a minimum protection floor

Every inhabited fire-response district should receive a basic readiness package during declared fire weather, regardless of its CinderMap rank. The package should include:

- a verified 24-hour route for reporting smoke or fire;
- an identified agency responsible for relaying alerts when mobile networks fail;
- a current evacuation contact for schools, care facilities, detention sites, seasonal housing operators, and large outdoor worksites;
- one daily review by a trained regional forecaster; and
- maximum target times for aerial reconnaissance or ground verification when credible reports cannot be located remotely.

The floor is not a promise that an engine will be stationed in every settlement. It is a guarantee that low model confidence will not remove a place from operational awareness. Regional commanders may tailor delivery to geography, but they should certify before each fire season that the functions are covered.

### 3. Pilot a coverage-aware allocation rule

After preserving permanent station staffing and mutual-aid obligations, flexible resources should be assigned under a 65/25/10 rule:

- **65 percent according to hazard and expected spread;**
- **25 percent according to detection deficits and travel-time gaps;**
- **10 percent held as a regional reserve.**

Resources covered by the rule include temporary patrols, contracted lookout aircraft hours, mobile communications units, rapid inspection teams, and short-term engine moves. It should not govern long-term firehouse closures, personnel discipline, or individual evacuation orders.

The 25 percent coverage component would prioritize places where dangerous conditions coincide with weak detection, not every location with poor connectivity. This distinction matters: a damp remote valley should not outrank a wind-exposed settlement simply because the valley has fewer sensors.

The percentages are a starting point. The Department should test alternative weights against historical simulations and live pilot results. Any proposal to reduce the coverage component below 15 percent should require public justification to the Forecast Stewardship Panel.

### 4. Build an independent sentinel observation sample

Model performance cannot be measured solely against the same incident system that contains the suspected bias. The state should create approximately 180 sentinel observation sites selected to represent combinations of vegetation, terrain, settlement type, mobile coverage, road access, and existing detection density.

A site may use a staffed lookout, an infrared sensor, scheduled aircraft observation, or a locally operated camera aimed at a defined wildland corridor. The purpose is not blanket monitoring. It is to create a more consistent sample against which the state can estimate how many events its ordinary systems miss or detect late.

Sensors should avoid homes, gathering places, and worker living areas. Devices capable of recording identifiable people or vehicle plates should process images locally and transmit only heat or smoke alerts unless a human operator confirms an active emergency. Routine imagery should be deleted within 72 hours. Tribal governments must control whether and how sentinel equipment is installed on their lands; state participation funds should be available without requiring transfer of raw data outside agreed channels.

The program should also pay volunteer brigades and community organizations for structured incident reporting. Unpaid consultation would predictably favour groups with administrative staff and spare time. A standard stipend of $8,000 per participating local organization is included in the budget, although larger jurisdictions may need separate agreements.

### 5. Require accountable human review

CinderMap should recommend actions but should not issue evacuation warnings, order power shutoffs, or direct an engine to leave a district without authorization from a designated official. During severe weather, regional decision teams should review cells that meet any of these conditions:

- high hazard and low observation confidence;
- a major disagreement between CinderMap and the regional forecaster;
- a proposed resource move that would place a district below its protection floor; or
- a forecast change of more than two priority levels within six hours.

Officials may override the system. They should select a reason code and provide a short explanation after the operational period. The point is not to punish reasonable judgment under pressure, but to identify recurring model errors and prevent unexplained automation.

Forecast displays should not include demographic labels during immediate dispatch decisions. Equity review belongs in system design, service-floor monitoring, and retrospective evaluation. Showing neighbourhood income or ethnicity beside an active incident could introduce inappropriate assumptions without improving fire behaviour estimates.

## Implementation plan

### First 90 days

The Governor should issue an executive directive establishing conditional use and naming the Department of Forest Response as lead agency. The Department should appoint the Forecast Stewardship Panel and publish an inventory of CinderMap inputs, intended uses, prohibited uses, and known limitations.

A joint technical team from the Department, State Weather Bureau, utility regulator, emergency communications office, and participating tribal governments should define observation confidence and identify which data can be shared lawfully. Procurement staff should amend the CinderMap vendor contract so the state can audit model changes and retain access to performance records.

### Months 4 through 9

The technical team should rebuild the training pipeline, map detection coverage, and test alternative allocation weights. Two contrasting regions—Lower Canto, with dense infrastructure and frequent utility reports, and Ember Ridge, with long travel times and fragmented communications—should begin operational trials.

Sentinel sites should be selected through stratified sampling rather than only through local applications. Community meetings remain necessary, but an application-only process would again favour places with grant-writing capacity. Site agreements should define data ownership, maintenance, removal, and complaint procedures.

Dispatchers and regional forecasters should receive scenario-based training. Exercises should include high-risk false alarms, conflicting sensor reports, communications outages, and pressure from local officials seeking to retain an engine.

### Months 10 through 18

The state should expand the trial to all six fire regions while preserving a control period for evaluation. An independent evaluator should issue an interim report after the first full fire season and a final report before the allocation rule is renewed.

Expansion should pause if the system repeatedly underestimates severe incidents in low-coverage cells, if sentinel equipment produces material privacy breaches, or if regional reserves are exhausted on more than 20 percent of red-flag days. The Governor should require legislative approval before CinderMap is used for permanent station placement, land-use enforcement, insurance regulation, or compulsory utility shutoffs.

## Performance measures and public reporting

The evaluation should compare CinderMap with the previous forecast process and with simple baselines, such as fuel-and-weather thresholds. A complex model is not justified if it cannot outperform a transparent rule.

Primary measures should include:

- median time from first detectable sign to verified incident;
- percentage of operationally significant ignitions detected within 20 minutes;
- calibration of predicted hazard, measured by whether events occur at roughly the stated rates;
- initial-attack arrival time, adjusted for geography;
- number of high-priority alerts that do not produce a verified incident;
- hours that districts fall below the minimum protection floor;
- frequency and stated reasons for human overrides; and
- cost per additional timely detection.

Every measure should be reported statewide and by observation-coverage quartile. Results should also be examined by region and settlement type where sample sizes permit. Small cells should be combined or suppressed to protect privacy.

The state should avoid using acres burned as the sole outcome. Acreage depends on weather, ecology, and suppression choices, and some planned or naturally beneficial fire may be allowed to spread. Fatalities, injuries, structure loss, displacement, and smoke exposure are important outcomes, but most are too infrequent for rapid model comparison. They should be reported without claiming that one season proves causation.

A reasonable pilot success threshold is a 20 percent reduction in the detection-time gap between the best- and worst-covered quartiles, with no more than a 10 percent increase in false dispatches statewide. Back-testing suggests that the package could reduce late detections in low-coverage cells by roughly 30 percent, but live results may be lower because historical simulations cannot reproduce staffing shortages, sensor failures, or public behaviour.

## Risks and mitigations

### False alarms may consume scarce resources

Correcting for weak observation could produce more patrols and verification flights in places where no fire is found. The regional reserve and human review reduce this risk, but do not eliminate it. The state should monitor false dispatch hours, not merely the number of alerts, because a remote false alarm can consume an aircraft for much longer than an urban one.

### Detection equipment may become surveillance infrastructure

Even fire-oriented cameras can capture people, vehicles, or activities on nearby land. Local processing, narrow fields of view, short retention, independent testing, and enforceable prohibitions on secondary use are essential. The state should provide a public equipment registry and a process for residents or landowners to request inspection of a sensor’s field of view. These controls are less developed for privately owned utility sensors, which may still feed alerts into CinderMap; data-sharing agreements must address that gap before statewide expansion.

### A coverage correction may be mistaken for a political quota

Some officials will argue that readiness assets should follow fire probability alone. The response is that the current probability estimate partly contains a measurement problem. Observation confidence is not a demographic preference. It is an operational variable affecting whether the state knows that a fire exists. Publishing the method and comparing it with weather-only baselines will be more persuasive than relying on equity language alone.

### The minimum floor may spread resources too thinly

A floor can become unsafe if it promises more than the state can staff. The proposed floor therefore guarantees reporting, review, contacts, and verification routes rather than a dedicated engine everywhere. During simultaneous incidents, life safety and expected spread must still govern triage. The Cabinet should be candid that equal awareness does not mean equal response time across difficult terrain.

### The model may gain authority beyond its validated purpose

Once agencies see a statewide risk score, pressure will arise to use it for zoning, insurance, utility shutoffs, and grant eligibility. Those uses involve different error costs. A model suitable for positioning a patrol may be unsuitable for raising a household’s premium or cutting its power. The executive directive should list permitted uses and require fresh legislative and technical review for expansion.

### Local knowledge may be formalized poorly

Volunteer brigades, residents, utility crews, and land managers often recognize seasonal conditions missing from state datasets. Yet free-text tips can be inconsistent and may encode rumours or personal disputes. The state should use structured observations, compensate participating organizations, and record the source type without treating any single report as ground truth. The proposed stipend may not fully cover the administrative burden in very remote districts, so agreements should be reviewed after the first season.

## Conclusion

Morroway should continue developing wildfire prediction, but it should not treat a clean map as proof of complete knowledge. CinderMap’s present score blends the chance of fire with the chance that the state would notice and record it. That distinction matters most in places already served by thin communications and long response routes.

Conditional deployment, separate confidence reporting, a protection floor, sentinel validation, and constrained human oversight offer a practical path forward. The package preserves the model’s genuine ability to identify dangerous fuel and weather combinations while reducing the risk that yesterday’s detection gaps determine tomorrow’s protection. Approval now will allow the state to test that approach before CinderMap becomes embedded in higher-stakes decisions that are harder to reverse.
