## Artefact

# The Mine That Should Refuse to Sell

The familiar economic story of asteroid mining begins with scarcity. An asteroid contains water, nickel, cobalt, or platinum-group metals; those materials are expensive to lift from Earth; therefore, extracting them in space should create a valuable new supply. The usual debate then concerns whether mining machinery can be made reliable enough and launch costs low enough for the arithmetic to work.

That story misses a less dramatic but more decisive problem. The first asteroid mine would enter a market with almost no inventory, few repeat buyers, long delays between orders, and customers whose missions must be designed years before delivery. Its product might be physically abundant yet commercially unusable. A mine capable of producing hundreds of tonnes of water annually could fail because it sells only dozens, while spacecraft operators could decline to depend on asteroid water because no supplier has yet demonstrated dependable production. Each side would be waiting for the other.

The first viable business model should therefore treat asteroid water not primarily as a commodity but as **reserved logistical capacity**. The miner should be paid partly for maintaining a tested ability to supply water at specified locations and dates, whether or not customers consume the entire amount. Some output should remain in orbital storage rather than being pushed onto a thin spot market. In effect, the mine should sometimes refuse to sell its inventory immediately.

This resembles a capacity market more than a conventional mining market. Electricity systems pay some generators for being available during rare peaks, even when those generators do not run continuously. Warehouses are paid to hold spare parts that may never be needed. Cloud-computing customers reserve processing capacity before they know their exact workloads. Asteroid mining has important differences from all three, but the shared principle is useful: when fixed costs are large and the cost of unavailability is catastrophic, reliability itself is a product.

## Why a spot market would punish the first supplier

Commodity-market reasoning works best when many buyers can purchase standardized goods from several sellers at short notice. Early in-space resource markets will have almost none of those properties.

Consider a robotic cargo vehicle designed to refuel in high orbit. Its operator cannot simply decide on the morning of departure whether to buy terrestrial or asteroid-derived propellant. Tank interfaces, water purity, electrolysis equipment, thermal management, navigation, insurance, and launch mass all depend on that decision. If asteroid supply is unavailable, redesigning or launching replacement propellant may take months or years. The relevant purchase is not merely a tonne of water. It is confidence, established during mission design, that a tonne will be present at a particular depot within a particular window.

The miner faces an equally severe commitment problem. Prospecting, processing equipment, transport tugs, communications, and storage tanks must be financed before substantial sales occur. Once installed, much of this capital cannot be redeployed. A customer can threaten to delay its mission or switch to a terrestrial launch; the miner, having already sunk its investment, may accept a price that covers variable operating costs but contributes little to construction costs. Anticipating this outcome, investors may refuse to fund the mine.

A pure spot market also creates a paradox of abundance. Suppose one mine produces 400 tonnes per year while current in-space customers collectively consume only 120 tonnes. Selling all 400 tonnes would require a steep price cut, assuming the extra volume could be sold at all. The producer would undermine the price on which its financing case depended. Producing less would leave expensive equipment idle. Neither outcome resembles the smooth equilibrium implied by a simple comparison between the cost of mining water and the cost of launching it from Earth.

This does not mean the mine should manufacture scarcity indefinitely. Rather, it should distinguish between three things that a single spot price conceals:

1. the variable cost of extracting and delivering another tonne;
2. the fixed cost of keeping the extraction and transport system operational;
3. the option value to customers of knowing that additional supply is available.

A two-part contract can price these separately. Customers pay an annual reservation fee for access to a defined quantity and a lower usage price for each tonne actually withdrawn. Unused production is stored, allocated to a common reserve, or left unprocessed when storage is full. The reservation fee supports fixed costs; the usage price encourages customers to consume water when it is economically useful rather than merely to justify the mine’s existence.

## An illustrative mine

Take a hypothetical venture, Sable Arc Resources, targeting an unnamed carbon-rich near-Earth asteroid. After prospecting, Sable Arc expects to recover water equal to 8 percent of processed material. Its mining unit, transfer vehicles, and depot are designed to make 420 tonnes per year available in a high orbit useful to several exploration and cargo missions.

The following figures are not a forecast. They are a deliberately simplified test of the proposed business structure:

| Item | Assumption |
|---|---:|
| Construction and deployment cost | $1.15 billion |
| Service life after commissioning | 12 years |
| Required return on deployed capital | 10% |
| Annual fixed operating cost | $72 million |
| Variable extraction and delivery cost | $140,000 per tonne |
| Annual available capacity | 420 tonnes |
| Expected annual withdrawals in early years | 280 tonnes |
| Usage price | $400,000 per tonne |
| Annual capacity payments | $210 million |

Annualizing the initial investment over twelve years at 10 percent produces a capital charge of roughly $169 million. Adding fixed operations gives about $241 million before a tonne is withdrawn. At 280 tonnes, variable costs add approximately $39 million, for total annual costs near $280 million.

If Sable Arc relied only on the stated usage price, revenue would be $112 million. Even if all 420 tonnes were sold, revenue would reach only $168 million, still well below annualized cost. The mine would need a spot price above roughly $700,000 per tonne at full utilization and about $1 million per tonne at the expected early utilization rate. It might attempt to demand such a price, but a handful of buyers would have strong incentives to postpone missions, bargain collectively, or retain Earth launch as a fallback.

Under the capacity model, Sable Arc receives $210 million for maintaining tested availability and $112 million for actual withdrawals. Total revenue of $322 million leaves a modest operating and capital margin. Customers collectively pay an effective average of about $1.15 million for each tonne they actually use, once reservation fees are included. That can still be attractive if their alternative is launching water from Earth and delivering it to a demanding orbit at several times that amount.

The apparent bargain should not be confused with proof that Sable Arc is feasible. The assumed ore concentration, recovery rate, equipment life, transport cadence, and financing cost could all be wrong. The point of the example is narrower: even under favorable technical assumptions, the pattern of payment matters. A mine with low marginal costs can still be unfinanceable if it must recover nearly all fixed costs through irregular commodity sales.

Capacity payments also reveal information that enthusiastic market forecasts tend to hide. A customer willing to say that it may buy 100 tonnes someday is not necessarily willing to reserve 100 tonnes for $50 million per year. The reservation auction forces prospective users to attach money to their claimed demand. If they will not, the mine probably should not be built at its proposed scale.

## What an orbital materials reserve would do

Individual bilateral contracts could provide capacity payments, but they would expose both parties to concentrated risk. A better arrangement would be an **Orbital Materials Reserve**, a clearinghouse funded by participating spacecraft operators. It would procure supply commitments from miners and depot operators, then sell reservation rights to users.

The reserve need not own an asteroid, a mine, or even most of the stored water. Its main function would be to standardize promises. A qualifying supplier might be required to demonstrate that it can deliver 50 tonnes within ninety days, maintain water within agreed contamination limits, provide telemetry showing inventory, and compensate customers after a failed availability test. The reserve could conduct periodic test withdrawals rather than accepting a producer’s claim that dormant equipment remains functional.

Procurement should occur in tranches. For example, the reserve might auction contracts for the first 100 tonnes of annual capacity, observe actual reservation demand, and add later tranches only when customers commit. This would be slower than financing a large mine on optimistic projections, but it would reduce the risk of constructing a stranded facility. Contracts could decline in price as cumulative capacity grows, reflecting learning and competition.

A reserve could also pool demand with different schedules. One science mission may need water in year four, a cargo tug in year five, and a habitat program intermittently from years six through ten. No single customer may justify the infrastructure. Together, their partially overlapping reservations can support it. Pooling is especially valuable when customers want insurance against delay but are unlikely to draw their full allocation simultaneously.

The reserve should hold some physical stock at one or more depots. Stored inventory protects customers from temporary mining failures, while operating mine capacity replenishes the buffer after withdrawals. Yet the reserve should not attempt to hold every contracted tonne in advance. Storage tanks, station-keeping, monitoring, and losses are not free. A sensible product might promise a small immediately available buffer plus a larger quantity deliverable after a defined lead time.

This arrangement changes the miner’s incentives. In a spot market, Sable Arc benefits from announcing large resources and selling as much as possible before a rival arrives. Under an availability contract, it benefits from conservative production plans, maintainable equipment, and transparent inventory. The ability to pass a delivery test becomes more important than a spectacular estimate of the asteroid’s total water content.

## The case for deliberately withholding supply

Withholding output usually sounds like monopolistic behavior, and it can be. An asteroid miner must not receive a public license to restrict supply merely to raise prices. The economic defense for reserve inventory is different: some material has greater value as insurance than as an immediate input.

Imagine that a depot contains 160 tonnes and near-term missions need only 90. Selling the remaining 70 tonnes cheaply for low-value shielding could leave no buffer against a delayed mining cycle. A later cargo mission might then spend hundreds of millions redesigning its trajectory or launching replacement propellant. Keeping those 70 tonnes in reserve can create more expected value than selling them now, even though they generate no immediate commodity revenue.

This option value is easy to omit because it depends on events that may not happen. If the mining unit never fails and customer schedules remain stable, the reserve appears wasteful. But a useful comparison multiplies the cost of a shortage by its probability. A 10 percent annual chance of a disruption causing $600 million in delay costs represents an expected exposure of $60 million, before considering reputational damage or the loss of scientific opportunities. Spending less than that amount to maintain a buffer can be rational.

The calculation is not exact. Delay costs are difficult to verify, mission failures may have several causes, and customers may exaggerate their vulnerability to obtain favorable terms. Even so, pretending the option value is zero is not neutral. It systematically favors high utilization and fragile logistics.

Reserve rules should therefore be mechanical rather than discretionary. Inventory below a specified threshold would not be offered on the spot market. Inventory above the threshold could be auctioned, with the threshold adjusted for demonstrated mining reliability and scheduled missions. Such rules would prevent the reserve manager from casually favoring one buyer while still allowing surplus stock to find productive uses.

## Objections and limits

The strongest objection is that capacity payments could disguise a subsidy for a market that does not exist. If governments, research programs, or wealthy spacecraft operators guarantee revenue, a miner may build technically impressive infrastructure that is never economically useful. A reservation system does not eliminate this danger. Indeed, annual payments can keep an uneconomic project alive longer than spot-market losses would.

This is why reservations must come primarily from users with control over mission budgets, not from general expressions of interest. Contracts should include deposits, cancellation charges, and deadlines for integrating the reserved material into mission designs. Procurement should also be technology-neutral: an Earth-launched tanker, a lunar producer, or an asteroid miner should be able to compete if each can satisfy the same location, timing, and reliability requirements. The reserve’s purpose is dependable supply, not the vindication of asteroid mining.

A second objection is that falling launch costs could erase the advantage before a mine is commissioned. This risk is real because launch services improve incrementally while an asteroid project may require years of development. Long contracts could then trap customers into buying expensive asteroid water when terrestrial delivery has become cheaper.

Contracts can partly address this through price reopeners linked to observable delivery bids. If Earth-supplied water becomes cheaper, reservation prices would fall or buyers could terminate after paying a predefined charge. However, generous exit rights would weaken the financing value of the contract. No clause can simultaneously give investors complete revenue certainty and customers complete protection from technological change. The parties must divide that risk, and the division will affect the cost of capital.

A third objection concerns substitutes. Lunar polar water, reusable tankers, more efficient electric propulsion, and missions designed to avoid refueling could all reduce demand. The capacity model accommodates competing suppliers better than a miner-specific subsidy does, but it does not predict which source will win. Asteroid water may be attractive for some destinations because it avoids lifting material from a gravity well, while lunar resources may benefit from shorter communication delays and easier inspection. These advantages vary by orbit and mission architecture. Speaking of a single “price of water in space” conceals crucial location and delivery terms.

Technical reliability poses a deeper challenge. A reserve contract is useful only if availability can be tested. An asteroid is not a terrestrial warehouse: ore properties may change as excavation proceeds, machinery may jam, and transfer windows may delay replenishment. Storing water also creates problems involving thermal control, contamination, leakage, and station-keeping. A mine that has produced once is not necessarily a mine that can produce on demand five years later.

Performance bonds and test deliveries can reduce this risk, but they cannot make an immature technology bankable by themselves. The Sable Arc example assumes that commissioning has already succeeded. It says little about who finances prospecting and the first full-scale unit before capacity payments begin. Milestone contracts might fund demonstrations, or investors might accept early risk in exchange for later reservation revenue, but this bridge remains the weakest part of the model.

There is also a governance problem. A shared reserve could become a gatekeeper, favoring established spacecraft operators and imposing standards tailored to one supplier. Small missions might be unable to afford multi-year reservations even when they would benefit from occasional purchases. Auctioning a portion of surplus inventory would help, but the largest customers would still shape depot locations, purity standards, and contract terms.

Finally, the arrangement does not settle disputes about rights to extract, possess, or transfer extraterrestrial material. The reserve can standardize commercial delivery without claiming sovereignty over an asteroid, but suppliers and insurers will still require predictable rules. If several operators interfere with the same body, or if a miner’s activities create hazardous debris, a capacity contract offers no adequate remedy. Commercial coordination cannot substitute for a wider framework governing access and responsibility.

## A more credible development sequence

Despite these limits, capacity contracting suggests a more disciplined path than announcing a giant resource and searching afterward for buyers.

First, prospective users should specify delivery locations, annual quantities, acceptable variability, and the financial consequences of shortage. Second, competing suppliers should bid to provide small tranches of capacity, including terrestrial delivery where practical. Third, customers should make deposits that are large enough to distinguish firm demand from curiosity. Only then should a mining venture choose the scale of its production system.

The first contract might cover just 20 or 30 tonnes, supplied by a demonstration unit with terrestrial backup. Later contracts could remove the backup requirement as the mine accumulates operating history. Stored inventory would rise with the number of dependent missions. Usage prices would remain visible, but much of the early project’s revenue would come from availability fees.

As the market matures, the balance should reverse. Multiple mines, depots, and transport firms would make emergency supply easier to obtain. Buyers would need fewer long reservations, spot trading would become more credible, and capacity payments could shrink. A successful reserve is therefore not necessarily a permanent institution. Its long-run achievement would be to make itself less important.

## Conclusion

The first asteroid mine is unlikely to fail because humanity has no theoretical use for water in space. It is more likely to fail because theoretical demand is not synchronized, standardized, or financeable. Comparing extraction cost with Earth launch cost is necessary, but it ignores the commercial structure connecting a mine built today with missions launched years later.

Treating availability as a product addresses that mismatch. Reservation fees can support fixed infrastructure; usage charges can guide actual consumption; stored buffers can insure against disruption; and staged procurement can expose whether claimed demand is genuine. The approach cannot solve uncertain geology, immature equipment, unclear resource rules, or the financing gap before commissioning. It can, however, prevent a technically productive mine from being destroyed by the very abundance it was built to create.

In the earliest asteroid-water market, the most valuable tonne may not be the one burned as propellant. It may be the tonne left in a monitored tank, proving that a mission designer no longer has to plan as if space were empty.
