## Artefact

# CIRRUS-Vax: A Thermal-Relay Network for Reliable Vaccine Delivery Across Intermittently Connected Islands

## Extended Grant Abstract

### Background and need

Vaccine cold chains are usually designed as linked refrigerators: a central store supplies district stores, which supply clinics, which pack carriers for outreach. This model performs poorly where electricity and transport fail on different schedules. In the Veyra Archipelago, the central store has dependable solar-backed refrigeration, but the eighteen island clinics experience recurrent inverter faults, prolonged cloud cover, and irregular ferry service. Outreach teams may wait several days for a boat and then travel for hours by motorcycle or foot. Adding more refrigerators has not solved the mismatch. A refrigerator protects stock while it remains in one place; it does not preserve cooling capacity during an uncertain journey, and a failed unit can place an entire clinic inventory at risk.

The proposed CIRRUS-Vax project will test a different architecture: treat cooling as a transferable service rather than a property of fixed equipment. Reusable phase-change cartridges will be conditioned at energy-reliable hubs, moved through a network of small relay points, and exchanged during vaccine journeys before their thermal capacity is exhausted. A low-bandwidth forecasting tool will estimate each carrier’s remaining thermal reserve from its temperature history, load, ambient conditions, and expected travel time. Dispatchers will receive a simple recommendation—proceed, exchange cartridges, reroute, or hold—while retaining authority to override it. Existing refrigerators will remain in use; the intervention changes how cooling capacity is positioned and replenished between them.

The project addresses both heat and freezing. Current outreach teams in Veyra commonly use deeply frozen water packs to maximize journey duration. This practice can expose freeze-sensitive products to damaging sub-zero temperatures, especially in lightly loaded carriers. CIRRUS-Vax will instead use cartridges with a phase transition centered at 5.2°C, separated from vaccine trays by a standardized conductive sleeve. Cartridges will be physically keyed and color-coded so that they cannot be confused with frozen water packs. Product-specific transport rules will continue to govern release, and no vaccine will be treated as usable solely because an algorithm predicts that potency remains adequate.

A four-week engineering assessment conducted with the Nareth Provincial Immunization Service recorded 74 routine delivery and outreach journeys. Twenty-one percent included at least one temperature reading below 2°C or above 8°C for more than fifteen minutes, while 17% arrived too late for the planned session. The assessment was too small and too short to estimate annual performance, but it identified a recurring operational pattern: carriers often began with ample cooling capacity, lost most of it while waiting at exposed landing stages, and then passed within a short distance of a solar-powered clinic or fish-market energy kiosk capable of conditioning replacement cartridges. These underused points form the basis of the proposed relay network.

### Central hypothesis and objectives

Our central hypothesis is that a distributed thermal-relay network, coupled with conservative thermal-reserve forecasting, will increase the proportion of planned vaccine doses that reach sessions within approved transport conditions without increasing freeze exposure, staff workload, or recurrent cost per usable dose.

The project has three objectives:

1. **Determine effectiveness:** estimate the effect of CIRRUS-Vax on thermally qualified vaccine delivery, freeze and heat excursions, canceled sessions, stockouts, and avoidable wastage under routine service conditions.
2. **Explain how and where it works:** quantify the contributions of relay availability, cartridge exchange timing, route uncertainty, carrier loading, and dispatcher adherence; identify failure modes before wider adoption.
3. **Establish implementation value:** assess worker acceptability, budget impact, operational emissions, maintenance requirements, and the institutional arrangements needed for continuation by the provincial immunization service.

### Setting and participants

The study will include all eighteen public vaccine clinics in Veyra, their associated outreach routes, three provincial distribution stores, and up to twelve relay sites. The islands vary in population, topography, electricity reliability, and ferry frequency. Six clinics are reached daily by scheduled boats, seven receive two or three services per week, and five depend partly on chartered canoes. Approximately 1,260 fixed-site or outreach vaccination sessions are expected during the main evaluation period.

Eligible shipments will contain routine vaccines whose approved transport instructions can be represented in the study’s release rules. Products requiring continuously frozen storage or ultra-low temperatures will be excluded. All vaccine custodians, dispatchers, drivers, boat crews who handle carriers, and refrigeration technicians will be eligible for workflow observations. A purposive sample of approximately 54 workers will participate in interviews. Patient names will not be collected; session attendance will be obtained as aggregate counts from routine registers.

### Intervention

CIRRUS-Vax has four linked components.

**1. Reusable thermal cartridges and standardized carriers.** Each insulated carrier will contain four or six sealed phase-change cartridges, depending on route class and load. Bench testing will characterize holdover time at ambient temperatures of 20°C, 30°C, and 40°C; at three load fractions; and after 0, 100, and 250 reuse cycles. Drop, vibration, seal-integrity, and cleaning tests will precede field deployment. Cartridges that fail weight, deformation, or phase-transition checks will be withdrawn. Vaccines will sit in removable trays that prevent direct contact with the cartridges and preserve airflow around cartons.

**2. Thermal relay points.** Relay points will be located where routes already pause and where surplus solar or grid-backed cooling is available. Candidate sites include clinics, ferry depots, and three licensed energy kiosks. Relay cabinets will store conditioned and returned cartridges in separate, clearly labeled compartments. Each exchange will be recorded by scanning durable cartridge and carrier identifiers; a numbered paper seal and logbook will provide an offline fallback. Relay attendants will not handle vaccine trays or make release decisions. Sites will receive a fixed service payment rather than a fee per exchange, reducing incentives to perform unnecessary swaps.

**3. Thermal-reserve forecasting.** Two-minute temperature measurements from sensors inside the carrier and adjacent to the vaccine tray will feed a compact model that estimates the probability of remaining within the product-specific transport envelope until the next qualified destination. The model will combine recent warming rate, cartridge age, load fraction, ambient temperature, route duration, and uncertainty in the boat schedule. It will run on an inexpensive dispatch handset without continuous connectivity. Recommendations will be deliberately conservative: when data are missing or inconsistent, the tool will default to exchange or hold rather than assume adequate reserve. It will never authorize use of a shipment that has crossed a mandatory quarantine threshold.

The initial model will be derived from chamber testing and the first three months of observational journeys. It will be recalibrated once, before randomized rollout, and then locked for the primary analysis. Any later safety correction will be documented as a protocol deviation and propagated to all intervention clinics simultaneously. The project will compare predicted reserve with observed time to threshold, calibration by route class, and the frequency and reason for human overrides.

**4. Workflow redesign and training.** Clinic teams will receive scenario-based training on loading, cartridge conditioning, exchange, sensor placement, quarantine, cleaning, and paper fallback. Rather than adding a separate cold-chain worker, tasks will be assigned within existing dispatch roles. A visual departure checklist will require confirmation of vaccine identity, expiry, load plan, cartridge state, sensor status, destination, and contingency relay. Monthly learning meetings will review anonymized incidents, but clinics will not be ranked publicly. Existing emergency procedures, including transfer to another qualified store, will remain available.

### Study design

We will conduct a pragmatic, cluster-randomized stepped-wedge trial over 30 months. All clinics will contribute six months of baseline data. Clinics will then cross from current practice to CIRRUS-Vax in six randomly ordered groups of three, at three-month intervals. Restricted randomization will balance groups on remoteness, annual vaccine volume, baseline excursion frequency, and access to scheduled ferries. The sequence will be generated by a statistician who is not involved in rollout. The final six months will provide observations after all clinics have adopted the intervention.

A stepped-wedge design was selected because relay infrastructure must be introduced geographically, staff move between neighboring routes, and the provincial service intends to retain functioning equipment after the study. Calendar-month effects will be included to distinguish intervention effects from seasonal heat, storms, and vaccine campaigns. Contamination will be measured through cartridge scans and journey logs; shipments from control clinics that use intervention equipment will remain assigned to control under intention-to-treat analysis and will be flagged for sensitivity analyses.

Current practice will continue during control periods: vaccines will be dispatched in existing insulated carriers with conditioned water packs according to provincial procedures, monitored by the same study sensors but without reserve predictions or relay exchanges. Study staff will not instruct control teams to disregard dangerous temperatures. If monitoring reveals a condition requiring quarantine under existing policy, the responsible custodian will be alerted; the event will remain in the analysis.

The primary endpoint is the **dose-weighted proportion of vaccines planned 72 hours in advance that arrive at the intended session with a qualifying temperature record and before the session closes**. The denominator will be the number of doses entered in the locked dispatch plan, limiting post hoc exclusion of failed deliveries. Qualification will follow a rule set defined for each included product before enrollment, using mandatory minimum and maximum temperatures, permitted durations, and quarantine triggers. A dose arriving late, lacking a valid trace, or associated with an unresolved threshold violation will not qualify for the primary endpoint, even if it is subsequently returned to stock.

Based on routine session volumes and a baseline qualified-delivery proportion of 72%, 18 clusters and approximately 1,260 sessions provide an estimated 83% power to detect an absolute improvement of 12 percentage points, assuming an intracluster correlation of 0.08, two-sided alpha of 0.05, and 10% loss of session records. These assumptions will be checked against baseline data without examining intervention effects. Because the number of clusters is modest, randomization-based inference and small-sample corrections will accompany the main model.

Secondary endpoints are: minutes below or above each product’s transport range; proportion of shipments with any freeze exposure; session cancellation or delayed opening; clinic stockout days; unopened-vial wastage by reason; emergency transport use; doses administered per planned session; cartridge loss and failure; electricity used for conditioning; staff time; and serious handling incidents. Patient immune response and disease incidence are outside the scope of this operational trial.

Temperature classification is an informative proxy rather than a direct potency measurement. To examine its validity, 360 vials designated for destructive testing will be sampled across products, seasons, route classes, and study periods. They will include vials from qualified journeys, controlled borderline exposures, and quarantined journeys. An independent laboratory blinded to route and intervention status will assess product-appropriate antigen content, adjuvant integrity where relevant, and visible or chemical indicators of freeze damage. These results will estimate concordance between the release rules and laboratory findings; they will not be used to release sampled vials for administration.

### Analysis

The primary analysis will follow intention to treat. A generalized linear mixed model will estimate the intervention effect on dose-level qualification, with clinic random intercepts, rollout group, calendar month, product category, route class, planned dose count, and prespecified weather and campaign indicators. Dose totals will be analyzed at session level to avoid falsely treating doses carried together as independent observations. We will report absolute percentage-point differences, risk ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and sequence-adjusted randomization tests.

Prespecified heterogeneity analyses will examine remoteness, ferry regularity, baseline electricity reliability, ambient temperature, load fraction, and route duration. These analyses will be interpreted as exploratory unless supported by an interaction estimate with adequate precision. Sensitivity analyses will vary rules for missing traces, exclude periods of region-wide transport shutdown, use per-protocol exposure defined by a functioning relay within reach, and account for measured contamination. Interrupted journeys will remain outcomes rather than being removed as anomalies.

Mechanism analysis will test whether intervention effects are mediated by greater thermal reserve at departure, timely exchange, or reduced waiting at clinics. Sensor traces will be segmented into storage, waiting, and movement phases using scan times and manually verified route logs. Since the intervention bundles equipment, forecasting, and workflow changes, these analyses can identify plausible pathways but will not provide randomized estimates for the contribution of each individual component.

### Implementation, economics, and environmental assessment

Implementation will be evaluated using mixed methods. Structured observations of 120 dispatches will quantify task time, omitted checklist steps, and workarounds. Interviews at baseline, early rollout, and study close will explore trust in predictions, perceptions of responsibility, gender-neutral fit and lifting demands, maintenance burden, and the practicality of relay custody. Interview findings will be compared with override logs and observed practice rather than treated as stand-alone evidence. A provincial operations group—including clinic custodians, boat coordinators, technicians, and store managers—will review workflow changes quarterly, while endpoint definitions and the locked analytic plan remain unchanged.

Economic evaluation will take the provider perspective. It will calculate incremental cost per additional thermally qualified dose delivered and per session cancellation avoided. Costs will include cartridges, carriers, sensors, relay payments, staff time, training, maintenance, replacement, conditioning electricity, emergency transport, and wastage. Research-only sensors and laboratory assays will be reported separately. Five-year budget projections will test cartridge lifetimes from two to six years, relay-site attrition, electricity prices, and different scales of adoption.

The environmental comparison will measure electricity used for conditioning and refrigeration, fuel consumed on vaccine journeys and emergency trips, and operational waste from damaged packs and carriers. It will report greenhouse-gas-equivalent emissions per qualified dose under consistent assumptions. Manufacturing and end-of-life emissions for the specialized cartridges will not be included in the primary comparison because supplier-specific inventory data are unavailable; a scenario range based on cartridge mass and material class will be presented separately. This narrower boundary may favor a reusable system if production impacts are substantial, so cost and emissions conclusions will be kept distinct from the effectiveness result.

### Safety, governance, and data stewardship

No vaccine that fails existing quarantine rules will be administered because of this study. Suspected breaches will trigger isolation, review by the designated vaccine custodian, and disposition under provincial policy. A safety committee with cold-chain, laboratory, and service-delivery expertise will review freeze events, cartridge leaks, carrier damage, and algorithm failures every three months. A predefined pause will apply if the intervention arm shows a sustained increase in freeze exposure or if two cartridge integrity failures occur in vaccine compartments.

Sensors will be identified by shipment and clinic, not patient. Handsets will encrypt local records and synchronize when connected. Role-based access, audit logs, and quarterly completeness checks will be used. Workers will provide consent for interviews and individually attributable time observations; routine logistics records will be analyzed under service authorization. De-identified analytic data, release-rule definitions, and model specifications will be transferred to the provincial service at closeout.

### Work plan and deliverables

Months 1–6 will cover engineering qualification, route mapping, baseline measurement, release-rule approval, relay contracting, and model calibration. Randomized rollout will occur during months 7–24. Laboratory validation, economic analysis, and implementation interviews will run in parallel. Months 25–30 will complete all-intervention follow-up, reconciliation of cartridge inventories, model documentation, and preparation of an adoption plan.

Deliverables will include a field-qualified cartridge and carrier specification; an offline thermal-reserve tool with auditable rules; a relay-site operating manual; effectiveness, safety, cost, and operational-emissions estimates; a maintenance and procurement forecast; and a scale-up decision matrix. The decision matrix will distinguish settings suited to a relay network from those better served by fixed refrigeration, direct delivery, or passive carriers without exchanges.

### Expected contribution

CIRRUS-Vax will determine whether transferring cooling capacity through a route can address a failure that additional stationary refrigerators do not: the loss of thermal protection during unpredictable waiting and travel. Its contribution is not merely a new container. It is a test of a logistics architecture that coordinates thermal storage, transport uncertainty, and human release decisions while retaining conservative vaccine-safety rules.

The study is intentionally sized to detect a meaningful operational effect rather than rare clinical outcomes. Its eighteen-clinic, single-archipelago setting and stepped rollout will limit certainty about performance in dense urban systems, landlocked regions, or routes with no energy-reliable nodes. The destructive laboratory sample will strengthen interpretation of sensor-based qualification but will be too small to validate every product-by-exposure pattern. Even with these constraints, the combination of randomized implementation, high-frequency temperature records, direct workflow observation, laboratory checks, and transparent costing will provide a credible basis for deciding whether thermal relays merit broader testing or adoption.
