## Artefact

# Pause-Aware Laboratory Automation Using Chemical Sentinel Wells

## Project summary

Laboratory robots are reliable when a protocol proceeds exactly as scheduled, but their recovery from interruption is usually crude. After a power fluctuation, depleted tip rack, blocked reader, or operator pause, a system may either continue from the next command or discard the entire plate. Neither response accounts for the physical history of the material. During the interruption, some wells may evaporate, beads may settle, enzymes may remain active, and only part of a reagent addition may have occurred. The event log records commands, not necessarily the resulting chemical state.

This project will develop and test a pause-aware automation framework called **SentryPause**. It will reserve eight wells on each 96-well plate for inexpensive sentinel reactions that integrate thermal exposure, evaporation, mixing history, and addition completeness. Sentinel measurements will be combined with scheduler logs, module temperatures, pipette pressure traces, plate images, and elapsed time to estimate a compact state vector for the interrupted plate. A constrained decision policy will then choose among four validated actions: resume, mix and resume, replenish and resume, or restart affected wells from a retained intermediate.

The work will use three contrasting automated workflows: a coupled enzyme-rate assay, magnetic-bead cleanup of synthetic DNA, and cell-free fluorescent protein expression. Development experiments will deliberately introduce five common interruption classes at several protocol stages. Recovery decisions will be compared with immediate resumption, fixed timeout rules, and decisions made by experienced automation operators from the same logs. Evaluation will use unopened reagent lots, calendar-separated test runs, and a second robotic platform.

The primary outcome is usable yield: the proportion of production wells meeting predeclared workflow-specific quality limits. Secondary outcomes include calibration of failure probabilities, recovery time, reagent consumption, false restart rate, and sentinel capacity cost. The central hypothesis is that chemical sentinels reveal consequential state changes that cannot be reconstructed from timestamps alone, allowing more material to be recovered without concealing poor-quality results.

## Motivation and technical gap

Automated protocols are often represented as sequences of transfers, incubations, mixing steps, and measurements. This representation is adequate for execution but incomplete for recovery. A command such as “incubate for 20 minutes” does not distinguish 20 minutes on a controlled heater from 12 minutes on the heater followed by eight minutes on an unsealed ambient deck. Similarly, a logged transfer can conceal partial aspiration, dispensing into only the first portion of a plate, or liquid retained in a tip. When execution is interrupted, the scheduler knows where it stopped but has only indirect evidence of what happened to the sample.

Most practical recovery rules therefore rely on elapsed time and the last acknowledged command. Such rules are transparent and safe in narrow settings, but they discard material unnecessarily near conservative time limits and may accept compromised material just inside those limits. Full digital twins offer a more ambitious alternative, yet they require kinetic and transport models for every reagent combination. Those models are expensive to construct and become brittle when lots, ambient conditions, or plate geometries change.

SentryPause takes a middle path. It does not attempt to model every molecular species. Instead, it measures a few dimensions of plate history that recur across workflows:

- **Thermal dose:** accumulated time-temperature exposure, rather than current temperature alone.
- **Solvent loss:** evaporation during an open or poorly sealed hold.
- **Dispense completion:** whether a scheduled addition reached all intended regions.
- **Settling or separation:** loss of suspension homogeneity during inactivity.

The proposed sentinels are sacrificial wells containing low-cost reporting mixtures rather than valuable samples. A slow fluorogenic reaction will act as a thermal-dose integrator. A nonreactive dual-absorbance tracer will report concentration caused by solvent loss. A colored addition marker will reveal plate regions reached before a partial-batch fault. A bead-containing sentinel will provide an image-derived settling index. The first two sentinels will be distributed across plate corners and central positions so that the system can distinguish plate-wide changes from spatial gradients.

Chemical sentinels cannot reproduce every production reaction. Their role is narrower: to provide direct evidence about shared physical exposures. The project will test whether this evidence adds enough predictive information to justify consuming eight wells and adding approximately 70 seconds of reading time per plate.

## Research questions and hypotheses

The project addresses four questions:

1. Can sentinel wells estimate interruption-related physical state more accurately than execution logs and module sensors alone?
2. Does a recovery policy based on that state increase usable yield compared with standard resume-or-restart rules?
3. Does the framework transfer across workflows and robotic platforms without full retraining?
4. When do the capacity and time costs of sentinels exceed their recovery benefit?

The predeclared hypotheses are:

- **H1:** Adding sentinel measurements will reduce the Brier score for predicting well-level quality failure by at least 20% relative to a model using logs, time, and hardware sensors alone.
- **H2:** SentryPause will increase usable yield after interruption by at least 15 percentage points relative to immediate resumption, while increasing median reagent cost per attempted plate by no more than 10%.
- **H3:** After 12 calibration plates per workflow, a model transferred to a second automation platform will retain at least 70% of the usable-yield improvement observed on the development platform.
- **H4:** Sentinels will have positive net value when interruption prevalence exceeds 3% of plate runs, under the project’s operational cost model.

H1 and H2 are the primary scientific hypotheses. H3 and H4 are intended to establish practical boundaries rather than universal thresholds.

## System architecture

SentryPause will contain three layers.

### 1. Event and sensor layer

A common event schema will record planned commands, acknowledgements, timestamps, tip identifiers, liquid classes, module states, and operator interventions. Hardware evidence will include pipette pressure summaries, heater and cooler temperatures, balance readings from the plate hotel, and overhead plate images. Raw video will not be retained; the system will store timestamped still images and derived meniscus, color, and bead-distribution features.

A small uninterruptible recorder will continue logging during simulated controller or deck power loss. This is important because a recovery system should not depend on the component that has failed. It will not power heaters or motion systems.

### 2. State-estimation layer

The estimator will produce four interpretable latent quantities: thermal dose, fractional solvent loss, dispense completion by plate region, and settling index. Calibration experiments will provide approximate physical labels for these quantities. For example, plate mass change will label solvent loss, and images acquired at known stationary intervals will label bead settling.

The main predictive model will use these state estimates, workflow stage, reagent lot, and sensor features to predict the probability that each candidate action will produce an acceptable well. A monotonic gradient-boosted model will be used initially because several relationships have known directions: greater solvent loss should not reduce estimated evaporation risk, and a longer unsealed pause should not imply less thermal exposure when temperature is held constant. A hierarchical logistic model will serve as a more interpretable baseline. Calibration curves and out-of-lot performance, rather than training accuracy, will determine model selection.

### 3. Constrained recovery layer

The decision layer will select only from actions validated in advance for the current protocol stage:

1. **Resume:** continue at the next confirmed command.
2. **Mix and resume:** perform a stage-specific mixing cycle before continuing.
3. **Replenish and resume:** add a bounded volume of solvent or a reagent known to have been missed, then mix.
4. **Selective restart:** discard affected wells and rebuild them from a retained intermediate.

The policy will minimize expected quality failure plus configured penalties for time, reagent use, and unnecessary restart. It will not invent pipetting steps, alter reagent identities, or exceed volume limits. If all action-specific failure probabilities exceed a workflow threshold, the plate will be quarantined for review.

## Experimental workflows

Experiments will be performed in the invented Lattice Bay Automation Laboratory on a gantry-style liquid handler with an integrated shaker, plate reader, heater, magnetic module, camera, and plate hotel. A second compact robotic workcell with different scheduling software and pipetting geometry will be used for transfer testing.

Three 96-well workflows were selected to expose the recovery system to different failure mechanisms:

### Workflow A: Coupled enzyme-rate assay

A nonhazardous fluorogenic substrate will be combined with a purified enzyme and coupling reagent. The reported result will be the initial fluorescence slope over 12 minutes. This workflow is sensitive to premature reagent contact, thermal exposure, and evaporation. A well will be acceptable if its slope is within 8% of its uninterrupted same-day reference and the local replicate coefficient of variation is below 10%.

### Workflow B: Magnetic-bead cleanup

Synthetic DNA fragments will be bound to magnetic beads, washed twice, dried briefly, and eluted. Outcomes will be DNA recovery, residual wash-marker carryover, and a fluorescence-based fragment-integrity proxy. Interruptions may cause bead settling, overdrying, incomplete aspiration, or inconsistent resuspension. A well will be acceptable if recovery is 75–115% of the reference, wash-marker carryover is below the predeclared limit, and the integrity proxy differs by no more than 12% from the reference.

### Workflow C: Cell-free reporter expression

A commercially prepared, nonliving transcription-translation mixture will express an invented fluorescent reporter, **MicaGreen**, from a synthetic template. Measurements will be lag time and fluorescence at 120 minutes. This workflow introduces greater lot variability and time-dependent biology without requiring cell culture. A well will be acceptable if endpoint signal is within 15% of the same-lot reference and lag time differs by less than 12 minutes.

Each plate will contain 64 production wells, eight sentinels, eight uninterrupted or process controls, and 16 wells used for action branching during development. Plate positions will be rotated through a Latinized layout to reduce consistent edge and action-location effects.

## Aim 1: Build a dataset linking interruptions, sentinel responses, and recovery outcomes

Five interruption classes will be induced:

- scheduler pause while temperature modules continue operating;
- loss of power to active deck modules;
- delayed replacement of a plate seal;
- partial-batch dispense caused by simulated tip depletion;
- missed or delayed mixing, including extended magnetic settling.

Interruptions will occur at early, middle, and late stages selected separately for each workflow. Time-based pauses will last 3, 12, 30, or 60 minutes. Partial-dispense and missed-mix faults will instead be parameterized by completion fraction or settling time. Conditions that could damage equipment, overflow wells, or cause uncontrolled aerosol generation will not be attempted.

The development dataset will contain 60 interrupted plates per workflow, for 180 plates in total. Thirty-six distinct fault-stage-severity combinations will be sampled in each workflow; 24 core conditions will be repeated across reagent lots and days. Ten uninterrupted reference plates per workflow will establish day and lot effects. A balanced incomplete design is necessary because a full factorial experiment would require several hundred plates per workflow.

Within development plates, production wells will be divided among eligible recovery actions. Action placement will rotate across plate regions. This creates approximate counterfactual evidence: wells exposed to the same interruption can receive different recovery treatments. For actions that necessarily affect a whole plate, such as resealing before a long incubation, matched plates will be run consecutively and their order randomized.

Ground-truth physical measurements will be collected on a subset of 36 plates per workflow. These measurements will include plate mass before and after interruption, manual imaging of menisci, direct temperature probes in sacrificial wells, and gravimetric verification of partial dispenses. They will calibrate the sentinel-derived state variables but will not be available to the deployed recovery policy.

All faults will be initiated by a separate fault-injection controller. The automation scheduler will receive only the evidence it would have during an ordinary interruption. This prevents the fault label or intended duration from leaking directly into the model.

The resulting dataset, **PauseSet-441**, will include command events, sensor summaries, sentinel readings, action assignments, and final quality outcomes. Reagent lots and repeated wells will be assigned group identifiers so that they cannot be split casually between model training and testing.

## Aim 2: Train and prospectively test the recovery policy

Model development will use only the 180 interrupted development plates and their reference runs. Hyperparameters and state transformations will be selected using grouped cross-validation in which entire dates and reagent lots are held out. Performance will be compared against four baselines:

- immediate resumption at the next confirmed command;
- a fixed rule that resumes after pauses of 10 minutes or less and restarts after longer pauses;
- a workflow-specific lookup table written before outcome analysis;
- recommendations from six automation operators shown the same logs, images, and module readings available to the model.

The operator comparison will use 24 anonymized interruption cases and will measure recommendation agreement, decision time, and expected outcome based on experimentally observed action branches. It is a secondary comparison because six operators cannot represent the full range of laboratory practice.

Prospective evaluation will use 30 new interrupted plates per workflow. Interruption conditions will be sampled before the runs and concealed from the recovery software. Two unopened reagent lots will be reserved for this phase. For actions that can be localized, alternating plate blocks will receive either the learned action or the fixed-rule action, with assignment reversed across runs. Plate-wide actions will be evaluated with matched plate pairs. Immediate-resume outcomes will remain available from designated branch wells where technically possible.

The primary endpoint will be the difference in usable yield between SentryPause and the fixed rule. A result will count as a successful recovery only if it passes the assay-specific limits; completion of a protocol is not itself success. Secondary endpoints will include:

- proportion of plates sent to quarantine;
- false recovery, defined as acceptance of a plate that later fails quality limits;
- false restart, defined as discarding wells that would have passed after immediate resumption;
- minutes from fault clearance to completed output;
- reagent and disposable cost per acceptable well;
- calibration error of predicted failure probabilities;
- additional failures caused by the recovery action itself.

Sentinel ablation will be performed computationally by retraining the selected model without sentinel features. On 18 evaluation plates, recovery decisions will also be generated before and after sentinel reading to measure whether the sentinels change the chosen action rather than merely improve retrospective prediction.

## Aim 3: Test platform transfer and estimate operational value

The selected model will be ported to the second robotic workcell through the common event schema. No development plates from the second platform will be used. For each workflow, 12 calibration plates will map platform-specific pressure, timing, and image features onto the existing state variables. Eighteen additional interrupted plates per workflow will then test transferred performance.

Three transfer approaches will be compared: direct use of the original model, recalibration of only the final probability layer, and limited fine-tuning of platform-specific feature mappings. The transfer endpoint will be the fraction of the original usable-yield improvement retained relative to the fixed rule. This design tests whether physical state variables are more portable than raw event features, although both workcells will be housed in the same laboratory and will use the same plate format.

An operational model will combine measured interruption prevalence, sentinel capacity loss, recovery time, reagent cost, and false-restart cost. Scenarios will range from one interruption per 100 plates to one per five plates. The model will report a break-even frequency rather than assuming that sentinels are worthwhile for every laboratory. Capacity loss will be valued explicitly: eight sentinel wells consume 8.3% of a nominal 96-well plate, even when no interruption occurs.

## Statistical analysis

The principal analysis of usable yield will use a mixed-effects binomial model with recovery method, workflow, interruption class, severity, and protocol stage as fixed effects. Plate, run date, and reagent lot will be modeled as grouped effects. Standard errors will be clustered by plate because wells exposed to the same fault are not independent. The primary contrast is SentryPause versus the fixed rule over all three workflows; workflow-specific contrasts will be reported with confidence intervals.

For continuous outcomes such as enzyme slope or DNA recovery, normalized error relative to same-day controls will be analyzed with robust mixed models. Prediction quality will be measured using Brier score, area under the precision-recall curve, and calibration intercept and slope. The Brier score is primary for H1 because recovery decisions depend on calibrated probabilities rather than ranking alone.

With 30 prospective plates per workflow and repeated action blocks within most plates, simulations based on a beta-binomial distribution indicate approximately 80% power to detect a 15-percentage-point increase from a baseline usable yield of 60%, assuming an intraplate correlation of 0.12. The calculation is less favorable for plate-wide faults: only about 70% power is expected for the same effect because those comparisons rely on matched plates. Higher-order interactions among workflow, stage, fault class, and severity will be treated as exploratory.

H1 and H2 will each be tested at a two-sided 0.025 level. H3 and H4 will be reported with 95% uncertainty intervals but will not be used to claim broad platform generality. Missing sensor values will be represented explicitly and stress-tested by masking additional values during evaluation. Runs aborted for equipment safety will be reported but excluded from chemical recovery analysis under a rule written before fault injection begins.

## Success criteria and decision points

At month 10, the project will continue to policy testing only if at least three sentinel channels show repeatable associations with their intended physical quantities and the combined sentinel read requires less than two minutes. A channel with a day-to-day calibration coefficient of variation above 15% will be removed or reformulated.

At month 20, platform transfer will proceed if the prospective policy improves usable yield over the fixed rule in at least two workflows and does not increase false recovery. Failure in the cell-free workflow alone will not terminate transfer testing, but the final framework will label unsupported workflow classes rather than extrapolating silently.

## Risks, limitations, and mitigation

**Sentinel-production mismatch:** Sentinel wells experience similar temperature and atmosphere but do not reproduce the full production chemistry. This is particularly important for cell-free expression, where reagent degradation may not track a simple thermal integrator. The model will therefore be allowed to conclude that sentinels add little for a particular workflow. Same-lot controls and workflow identity will remain necessary inputs.

**Sparse coverage of fault combinations:** The balanced incomplete design cannot estimate every interaction, and rare sequences of multiple faults are excluded. Core conditions will be repeated, while high-order effects will be labeled exploratory. The deployed policy will quarantine states outside the calibrated feature range.

**Recovery-induced contamination or dilution:** Replenishment and remixing add robotic operations at a time when plate state is uncertain. Recovery steps will use capped volume limits, fresh tips, spatially separated action blocks, and negative controls. A recovery action that increases control-well contamination above 1% will be removed from the whitelist.

**Artificial interruption behavior:** Deliberate pauses are repeatable but may not reproduce the disorder of actual outages, including operator handling or several simultaneous faults. Naturally occurring interruptions during the project will be logged as a small observational cohort, but they will not be pooled with controlled experiments for the primary analysis.

**Platform dependence:** The two systems differ in scheduler and pipetting geometry, but they share a building, operators, consumable format, and environmental conditions. Successful transfer would support portability of the state representation, not generality across all laboratory robots or plate scales.

**Capacity overhead:** Eight sentinels may be unacceptable for low-interruption or high-value screening campaigns. The operational analysis will compare eight-, four-, and zero-sentinel layouts. The four-well option will sacrifice spatial resolution and will be recommended only if its prediction loss is small.

The experiments use purified reagents, synthetic DNA, and nonliving cell-free mixtures. No pathogenic material, human samples, autonomous protocol generation, or unbounded robotic motion is involved. Motion interlocks and existing deck safety controls will remain active during all fault injections.

## Reproducibility and outputs

PauseSet-441 will be released with a data dictionary, event-schema specification, interruption scripts, plate maps, model code, and a simulator that replays logged states without moving hardware. Raw proprietary controller packets will be converted into the common schema before release. Reagent identities that affect interpretation will be described by function and concentration, although exact formulations of purchased mixtures may not be available.

The prospective analysis plan, QC thresholds, exclusion rules, and model-selection procedure will be frozen before the reserved reagent lots are opened. Model cards will list supported workflow stages, calibrated fault ranges, sentinel requirements, and conditions that trigger quarantine.

## Expected contribution

SentryPause will test a practical proposition: an automation system can recover more intelligently if it measures what happened to the material rather than relying only on where the command sequence stopped. The project will contribute a structured interruption dataset, a restricted and auditable recovery policy, and evidence about when sentinel wells are worth their capacity cost. Even if a universal recovery model proves unrealistic, the experiments should identify which physical state signals are reusable and which failures still require workflow-specific restart rules.
