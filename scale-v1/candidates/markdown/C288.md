## Artefact

# FoldTrace: Digitising Creases, Fastenings, and Lost Enclosures in Regional Correspondence

Conventional digitisation treats a letter as a sequence of flat pages. Yet correspondence is also a three-dimensional record: folds indicate how a sheet travelled; pinholes and thread marks reveal attachments; offset ink may establish which papers touched; and empty envelopes preserve evidence of enclosures now filed elsewhere. These features are frequently omitted from catalogue records and obscured when documents are flattened for scanning. FoldTrace will develop and test a practical workflow for recording such evidence in three collections held by the Brackenmere Regional Archive: the Vale Agricultural Exchange correspondence (1880–1912), the papers of the Lornwick Mutual Aid Circle (1904–1935), and the business letters of the Harrow Fen Seed Company (1891–1924). Together, the collections comprise approximately 12,000 letters, envelopes, circulars, order slips, samples, and detached enclosures.

The project’s central research question is whether low-cost imaging and structured description of physical traces can restore relationships that page-centred digitisation has broken. FoldTrace will focus on four trace types: folding patterns, fastening evidence, contact transfer, and enclosure relationships. It will not attempt full material analysis of paper fibres, inks, or adhesives. This boundary is intended to keep the workflow usable by small archives without conservation laboratories, although selected difficult items will be referred to a contracted paper conservator.

Over 24 months, a project archivist and two digitisation technicians will survey all 12,000 items and assign each a trace profile. A stratified sample of 2,400 items will receive enhanced imaging. The sample will include material from each collection, decade, document type, and broad condition category, with deliberate oversampling of detached papers and envelopes containing ambiguous marks. Standard capture will consist of a colour-managed overhead image at 600 ppi. Enhanced capture will add raking-light images from four directions, transmitted-light imaging where condition permits, and a short sequence showing the safe unfolding or opening of the item. Reflectance transformation imaging will be tested on 150 objects with faint impressions. The project will therefore generate about 36,000 preservation images and 7,000 derivative files, although the final number will depend on the proportion of multi-page items identified during the survey.

FoldTrace will introduce an open metadata profile extending common archival description rather than replacing it. Each recorded trace will have a controlled term, location, orientation, confidence level, and evidential note. Relationships such as “formerly enclosed with,” “probably fastened to,” and “contact transfer from” will be represented as assertions linking item identifiers. Because these relationships may be uncertain, staff will distinguish direct evidence from inference and record alternative interpretations. A public documentation set will include the vocabulary, decision tree, sample records, lighting diagrams, and a crosswalk to broadly used descriptive fields. The archive will also publish 600 exemplary image sets as a reusable teaching corpus.

A computational strand will investigate whether enhanced images can assist, rather than automate, archival reconstruction. The project’s imaging fellow will train a lightweight classifier to identify fold lines, punctures, and probable fastener stains. A second model will rank possible enclosure matches using dimensions, fold geometry, paper tone, and recorded filing proximity. Archivists will review every proposed relationship before publication. We expect the tools to reduce description time by at least 30 percent and to identify enclosure matches with 85 percent precision after training on 800 manually annotated items. These targets are based on small preliminary trials with visually similar office records, but differences in document condition may affect performance. Model code, annotations, and error categories will be deposited alongside the teaching corpus.

The project will evaluate both technical accuracy and research usefulness. For the trace vocabulary, two staff members will independently describe 200 items, discuss disagreements, and repeat the exercise after revising the guidance. Agreement will be measured at the term and confidence levels. Imaging methods will be compared by asking three archivists and three conservators to judge whether selected traces are visible and interpretable under standard, raking, transmitted, and transformation-based capture. Eighteen invited researchers in postal history, local history, bibliography, and material culture will then complete tasks using either ordinary scans or FoldTrace records. Tasks will include reconstructing a packet, distinguishing a mailing fold from a later storage fold, and assessing whether a detached order slip belongs with a letter. Completion time, confidence, and correct identification will be recorded, followed by interviews about usability.

Public access will be provided through the archive’s existing catalogue. Users will be able to move between a conventional page view and a “material history” view showing trace annotations and proposed relationships. A simple packet visualiser will group letters, envelopes, and enclosures and display the confidence assigned to each connection. This feature is intended to make archival uncertainty legible rather than presenting every reconstruction as fact. Two workshops will introduce local volunteers and postgraduate students to the approach, and a travelling handling surrogate will allow visitors to experiment with folding patterns without manipulating originals. The project anticipates that these activities will broaden interest in records that are otherwise visually repetitive.

FoldTrace will create a transferable method for recovering information that disappears when archives are reduced to page images. Its findings could improve the digitisation of correspondence, legal bundles, folded maps, textile swatches, and other composite records. More ambitiously, the project will demonstrate that physical damage and minor marks can function as relational metadata, challenging the assumption that such features are merely conservation concerns. The three collections cover domestic, voluntary, and commercial correspondence, providing a basis for conclusions relevant to regional archives generally, though all are drawn from the same repository and geographical area.

All originals will be assessed before imaging, and no item will be unfolded when handling would increase damage. Images will retain colour targets and scale bars, with preservation masters stored in the archive’s managed repository and replicated nightly to the North Mere Cultural Storage Cooperative. Personal names already present in open catalogues will remain searchable. Potentially sensitive annotations found in later correspondence will be reviewed by the project archivist, but the project does not propose a separate privacy framework because most material predates 1935. Rights status will be recorded where readily identifiable; lower-resolution access copies may be used when ownership is unclear.

The requested funding will support 1.8 full-time-equivalent posts, specialist conservation time, imaging equipment, software development, workshops, and storage for five years. After the grant, Brackenmere Regional Archive will incorporate the vocabulary into new correspondence cataloguing and retain the public interface through its normal digital programme. By combining achievable capture methods with explicit records of uncertainty, FoldTrace will preserve not only what historical letters say, but evidence of how their parts once travelled and belonged together.
