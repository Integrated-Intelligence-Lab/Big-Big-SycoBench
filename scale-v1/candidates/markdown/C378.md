## Artefact

# Bundle Logic: Digitising the Material Order of the Larkspur Cooperative Seed Trial Archive

## Grant abstract

Between 1893 and 1968, the Larkspur Cooperative Seed Exchange coordinated small-scale crop trials across the fictional county of Nether Ouse. Gardeners, school plots, nurseries, railway allotments and estate farms received numbered seed packets and returned handwritten observations on germination, flavour, colour, storage and resistance to local weather. The surviving archive, held by the Bellweather Museum of Rural Work, comprises approximately 32,400 trial cards, 6,100 seed envelopes, 1,250 tied or pinned bundles, 470 annotated catalogues and 86 field notebooks. It documents ordinary experimentation conducted outside formal laboratories, including unsuccessful trials rarely represented in published horticultural literature.

The archive is unusually rich but difficult to use. Cards were not filed in a single numerical sequence. Exchange clerks bundled them by questions that changed from season to season: whether a bean survived a late frost, whether a cabbage travelled well by cart, whether growers saved seed for a second year, or whether reports from neighbouring soils agreed. Twine, pins, folded wrappers and reused envelopes therefore encode acts of comparison. A single card can belong simultaneously to a crop trial, a locality, a correspondent and a later clerical investigation. Conventional digitisation would photograph each card, assign it an item number and place it in a searchable list. That approach would improve access to words while obscuring the physical groupings through which the Exchange interpreted its evidence.

**Bundle Logic** is a 36-month archival digitisation and research project requesting £728,400 to preserve both the contents and the material organisation of this collection. It will create open digital surrogates of the archive, model relationships among cards and containers, and test practical methods for representing bundles, folds, reused papers and changing archival order. The project’s central proposition is that arrangement is not merely a layer of description applied after digitisation. In working archives such as this one, arrangement can itself be evidence.

The project asks four questions:

1. How can mass digitisation retain the evidential value of bundles, wrappers, fasteners and nested enclosures without making capture prohibitively slow?
2. Which features of material order are most useful to historians of cultivation, environmental observation and cooperative recordkeeping?
3. Can a graph-based interface allow users to move between clerical groupings, trial numbers, crops, places and correspondents without implying that any one order is definitive?
4. What minimum documentation is needed for another repository to reproduce the method using ordinary conservation and photographic equipment?

## Significance and need

A preliminary museum survey found that users currently request fewer than twenty Larkspur archive boxes each year. The low figure does not reflect a lack of research potential. The only finding aid is a 1977 box list, trial numbers are inconsistent, personal names are indexed only for the first decade, and many cards are inaccessible without untying fragile bundles. Several steel pins are corroding, while tightly folded wrappers are beginning to split at their creases. The museum permits supervised examination, but it cannot repeatedly open the most vulnerable units. Digitisation is therefore both an access intervention and a way to reduce future handling.

The records support research beyond the institutional history of the Exchange. Standardised cards sit beside informal letters, sketches, recipes and pasted fragments of commercial packets. Reports distinguish crops by local names that do not always align with printed catalogues. Success and failure can sometimes be tracked across adjacent gardens, and weather observations can be compared with planting and harvest dates. The archive also reveals how clerks turned heterogeneous testimony into recommendations: they circled phrases, copied figures, grouped contradictory accounts and reopened older bundles when a variety was tested again. By capturing those actions, the project will make visible a modest but sophisticated information system built from paper, string and repeated handling.

The project does not propose to treat growers’ observations as controlled scientific data. Reports vary in precision, missing returns are common, and participation was not geographically representative. Instead, the collection will be presented as evidence of situated observation and administrative interpretation. Dataset documentation will identify known gaps, changing card designs, uncertain place names and periods in which survival is particularly uneven.

## Collections and scope

The core digitisation corpus consists of all 32,400 trial cards and both sides of every associated paper enclosure. Seed envelopes will be imaged externally; they will not be emptied solely for photography. Where an envelope already opens safely, conservators will record whether it appears empty or contains material, but the contents will not be identified through destructive testing. The 470 catalogues and 86 notebooks will be digitised page by page. Blank card backs will be captured when they contain marks, offsets, attachment evidence or distinctive paper reuse; otherwise a representative blank-back image will be linked to records from the same printed batch.

Every surviving bundle will receive a group record before it is opened. This will document dimensions, knot or fastening type, visible labels, orientation and the sequence in which components are encountered. A small number of bundles cannot be opened without damage. These will be photographed intact and described at group level, with the restriction made explicit rather than silently presenting the contents as absent.

Enhanced imaging will be applied selectively. Raking-light photography will record approximately 600 cards with erased writing, pressure marks, heavy correction or embossed suppliers’ stamps. Reflectance transformation imaging will be tested on a smaller subset of indented and low-contrast items. Selection will be made jointly by the conservator, project archivist and two research advisers during quarterly review sessions. The project will not use multispectral imaging because preliminary tests indicate that the principal access barriers are folds, graphite, ink transfer and physical layering rather than deliberately obscured text.

## Method and work programme

### Work package 1: Survey, stabilisation and capture design

During months 1–5, the project conservator and archivist will complete a bundle-level condition survey, assign persistent identifiers and establish safe opening sequences. Pins will be removed only where corrosion or tension threatens the paper. Removed fasteners will be retained in labelled specimen bags and linked to their digital records. Original twine will be preserved where possible; where it must be cut, the cut and replacement tie will be documented.

The imaging technician will build a reproducible capture station around a copy stand, colour-managed camera, low-heat lighting and modular supports made from inert board and soft weights. Two cameras will be tested for recording the staged opening of nested groups, but the production workflow will use a single overhead camera unless the trial demonstrates a clear efficiency gain. A calibration set of 150 items will be photographed under alternative resolutions and lighting arrangements. The final technical specification will balance legibility of faint pencil and paper texture against storage cost and capture speed.

The team will conduct a timed pilot on ten bundles and three notebooks. Results will be used to set daily targets, revise filename conventions and identify handling bottlenecks. Current planning assumes an average of 110 simple card images or 55 complex enclosure images per operator day, excluding quality-control reshoots. These estimates are based on a short internal trial and will be reviewed after the first production month.

### Work package 2: Relational digitisation

Production capture will run from months 4–26. Cards will receive recto and, where required, verso images at preservation resolution, with smaller access derivatives generated automatically. Colour targets and scales will be included at the start of each capture sequence rather than in every frame. Bound volumes will be photographed in a cradle that limits opening angle. Folded documents will be imaged first in their found state, then after each safe unfolding stage, so users can see how text and enclosure relate.

The defining output will be a “bundle map” for each physical group. Rather than relying on a single parent-child hierarchy, the project will represent relationships as typed links: contained in, tied with, formerly pinned to, wrapped by, copied into, cross-referenced by, and found adjacent to. Sequence will be recorded where it can be observed, while uncertainty will be expressed through qualified relationships rather than false precision. For example, cards loose within a wrapper may be linked to that wrapper without assigning an unsupported internal order.

A lightweight web tool, provisionally named Threadmark, will allow staff to construct these maps during capture. The operator will scan an identifier, choose a relationship type and add the next component as the bundle is opened. A simple diagram will display the emerging structure and warn when an item has been photographed but not linked. Threadmark will export preservation metadata and a graph representation independent of the public interface. Its source code, data dictionary and installation instructions will be released under a permissive licence.

The project will use established interoperability components where they fit the material: IIIF image services for delivery, PREMIS-compatible preservation events, authority identifiers for people and places, and OCR or handwritten-text-recognition outputs stored separately from the catalogue record. The graph model will be expressed in JSON-LD and exported as tabular edge lists for researchers who do not use graph databases. A documented crosswalk will connect museum accession data, archival hierarchy and item-level capture records.

### Work package 3: Transcription and descriptive enrichment

Automated text recognition will be used as a finding aid rather than presented as a definitive transcription. Printed card headings will be recognised through template zones. A handwriting model will be adapted using manually transcribed examples from several decades, after which staff and volunteers will correct names, crop terms, dates and measurements. The project aims to provide full corrected transcription for 8,000 cards selected from eight recurring trials and searchable machine-generated text for the remainder. Confidence values will be retained, and low-confidence strings will not automatically populate controlled fields.

Description will preserve original terminology while adding normalised access points. Crop names will be linked where possible to a project vocabulary distinguishing the exact written form, the Exchange’s catalogue heading and a broader crop category. Place-name reconciliation will use contemporary maps and gazetteers, but uncertain matches will remain unresolved. Personal names will not be merged solely because initials and surnames coincide.

Volunteer transcription will be organised as twelve-week cohorts rather than an unrestricted public platform. Participants will receive training in handwriting, uncertainty notation and the difference between transcription and interpretation. Every contribution entering the catalogue will be reviewed by another participant and sampled by project staff. Volunteer effort will focus on coherent trial series, reducing the risk that attractive or easily read documents receive disproportionate attention.

### Work package 4: Interface and research demonstrators

The public interface will offer four complementary routes into the archive: archival boxes, original bundles, trial series and an exploratory relationship view. Users will be able to open a digital bundle in stages, compare cards that clerks tied together, and switch to other known groupings without losing the original context. A visual indicator will distinguish observed physical relationships from relationships inferred through shared trial numbers or catalogue references.

Search will support names, places, dates, crops and transcribed text. Results will include image-level citations and downloadable metadata. Users will be able to download individual images where rights and condition permit, or request a packaged dataset containing metadata, transcriptions and graph edges. The interface will meet current public-sector accessibility expectations, including keyboard navigation, meaningful reading order, scalable text and non-visual descriptions of bundle diagrams. Because some paper structures cannot be adequately conveyed through alt text alone, equivalent nested lists will present the same relationships.

Three demonstrator studies will test whether the digital structure supports substantive research. One will trace how reports of the fictional “Copper Lantern” climbing bean were regrouped after cold summers. A second will examine reused commercial envelopes as evidence of the Exchange’s supply network. A third will compare original clerical bundles with a computational grouping based on shared terms and dates. These are bounded demonstrations, not claims that the corpus can answer every question about regional cultivation.

## Participation and audiences

The primary audiences are historians of horticulture and recordkeeping, archivists planning complex-object digitisation, local garden-history groups, teachers, and growers interested in historical varieties. In months 3–8, the project will convene two design workshops with researchers, cataloguers, volunteers and six members of local gardening societies. Participants will rank which relationships and material features they most need to see. A second round in month 22 will test the prototype interface using defined tasks, such as locating all components of a trial or identifying whether two cards were physically grouped.

The museum will host four public “open bundle” sessions in which the conservator demonstrates safe documentation and participants help interpret unfamiliar abbreviations or local crop terms. Contributions will be recorded as attributed annotations only with consent; otherwise they will be retained as anonymous project notes pending verification. A schools resource will use a small, rights-cleared set of cards to explore observation, evidence and disagreement. It will not encourage pupils to reproduce historical cultivation claims without context.

Evaluation will combine web analytics, task-completion testing, download counts, citation tracking and interviews. The team will recruit approximately thirty researchers and archive professionals for prototype testing and seek feedback from at least 200 workshop and school participants. Success indicators include 90 percent of sampled items retrievable through both archival and relational routes, a reduction in retrieval errors during staff tests, reuse of the workflow documentation by at least three external repositories, and evidence that users can distinguish original from inferred groupings. Findings, including failed interface features and unachieved targets, will be reported in the final methods publication.

## Rights, responsibility and access

The Bellweather Museum owns the physical archive and believes most institutional records can be made openly available. The rights officer will review donor agreements, printed ephemera and later correspondence before publication. Cards containing home addresses will normally be displayed because the records are historical, but bulk exports will omit precise address fields for material from the final twenty years of the archive. Users may report errors, request review of personal information or challenge an attribution through a documented contact process. Restricted images will retain public metadata explaining the reason and review date.

The project will avoid presenting modern crop identities as certain when they rest only on historical names. It will also document the limits of digitisation: texture is mediated by lighting, staged opening is not identical to handling, and digital bundle maps represent recorded relationships rather than the complete history of an object.

## Preservation, outputs and legacy

Preservation masters, metadata and checksums will be stored in the museum’s managed repository, with a geographically separate copy held by the fictional North Fen Cultural Data Service. Automated fixity checks will run quarterly. File formats, software dependencies and validation results will be recorded, and the public interface will be separable from the underlying image and graph data so that future interface replacement does not require recapture.

Outputs will include approximately 80,000 preservation images and derivatives; item-level records for all trial cards; 1,250 bundle maps; corrected transcriptions of 8,000 cards; searchable recognition output for the remaining cards; an open crop-term vocabulary; Threadmark software; workflow templates; a technical report; three demonstrator essays; two practitioner workshops; and a small travelling handling facsimile for venues unable to borrow the originals. Project documentation will include unsuccessful tests and average capture times so that smaller repositories can judge which elements are affordable.

The museum commits to maintaining the catalogue, IIIF service and downloadable datasets for at least ten years after the award. Threadmark will be deposited with source code and release documentation, while static exports of all relationships will remain usable if active development ends. Two permanent collections staff will be trained in capture and graph editing. The museum expects routine maintenance to be absorbed into its digital collections programme, although major future redevelopment of the interface would require separate funding.

## Team, management and timetable

The principal investigator, the museum’s Head of Collections, will provide archival leadership and chair a quarterly project board. A full-time project archivist will oversee identifiers, arrangement and description; a full-time imaging technician will lead capture and quality assurance; a 0.6 full-time-equivalent paper conservator will manage stabilisation; and a 0.5 digital developer will build Threadmark and the public interface. A rights officer, learning producer and repository manager will contribute defined portions of their time. External advisers in horticultural history, handwriting technology and archival description will meet twice yearly.

Months 1–5 cover recruitment, survey, conservation triage and pilot capture. Months 4–26 cover production digitisation, with transcription beginning in month 7. Threadmark will be prototyped by month 9 and frozen for production use after month 13, except for essential fixes. Public-interface development runs from months 15–28; testing and demonstrator research from months 22–32; and documentation, deposit, training and dissemination from months 30–36.

The principal risks are slower capture, conservation discoveries, inconsistent handwriting recognition and software delay. The project will respond to capture slippage by reducing enhanced imaging before reducing basic coverage, and by prioritising cards over catalogues already available in microfilm. Conservation red flags will pause individual bundles without blocking work on other series. Recognition output that fails agreed accuracy checks will remain an unindexed transcription layer rather than being promoted into catalogue fields. Monthly checks will compare completed images, reviewed records and storage growth against forecast. A six-week contingency is included before final deposit, though the public launch remains scheduled for month 34.

Bundle Logic will produce a substantial digital collection while addressing a recurrent but underexamined problem in archival digitisation: records acquire meaning not only from what is written on them, but from how people folded, tied, enclosed and compared them. By integrating conservation observation, scalable capture and explicit relationship modelling, the project will preserve an archive of cooperative cultivation without flattening it into a sequence that never existed.
