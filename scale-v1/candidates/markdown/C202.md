## Artefact

# The Most Valuable Wires in a Chiplet Package May Be the Ones That Diagnose It

Advanced packaging roadmaps usually celebrate the same achievements: finer bond pitch, more vertical tiers, wider die-to-die interfaces, and greater bandwidth per millimetre of package edge. These are meaningful gains, but they encourage a narrow definition of progress. A package that connects more transistors is not necessarily a better product if manufacturers cannot efficiently identify which bond, lane, voltage domain, or thermal interface caused it to fail. For high-value chiplet systems, post-bond observability should therefore be treated as a primary architectural resource, not as test circuitry added after useful wiring has claimed the available space.

The case begins with a change in what constitutes a “component.” In a monolithic device, wafer probing can reject many bad dies before packaging, and the remaining uncertainty is concentrated in a comparatively mature assembly process. A heterogeneous package may combine compute tiles, cache dies, an I/O hub, memory stacks, passive bridges, and a silicon or organic interconnect substrate. Each element can pass its own test while the completed system still fails because of marginal micro-bumps, hybrid-bond voids, substrate warpage, local power droop, or interactions that appear only under simultaneous load.

The familiar remedy—use known-good dies—is necessary but incomplete. It establishes that each tile worked under its test conditions; it does not establish that every assembled interface works across voltage, temperature, and ageing. Nor does it tell an engineer whether an intermittent error originates in a transmitting die, a receiving die, the physical bond between them, or a shared power rail. Without package-level observability, expensive systems are divided into two crude categories: working and scrap. That is an avoidable loss of information as well as material.

Consider a stylised accelerator called Morrowglass, assembled from eight compute tiles, one control die, and four memory stacks. If each component has a 99.5% probability of passing incoming inspection, their combined probability of all being good is about 94%. If four critical assembly stages each yield 99%, the nominal final yield falls to roughly 90% before accounting for correlated failures. These figures are illustrative, but they expose the multiplication problem: respectable local yields can still leave a costly fraction of completed packages unusable. More importantly, a binary final test cannot distinguish an irreparable package from one with a single defective lane that could be isolated.

Designers should reserve an explicit “observability budget” alongside power, area, and bandwidth budgets. It would fund three capabilities. First, die-to-die links should support loopback, lane-level error counters, and fault injection so that failures can be localised rather than merely detected. Second, packages should include distributed thermal and voltage sensors near interfaces, not only on the largest compute die. A package-wide average can conceal the local expansion or droop that destabilises one bond cluster. Third, high-bandwidth interfaces should include spare lanes or alternate routes that allow a package to operate at a certified lower grade when a small region is defective.

This proposal is not simply a demand for more sensors. The important feature is continuity of evidence across the product’s life. Wafer-sort results, bond-tool process data, post-assembly link maps, burn-in behaviour, and field error counters should refer to the same physical interface identifiers. A lane labelled C4-L17 during design should remain identifiable at final test and in returned-product analysis. Today, data often stops at organisational boundaries: the die team records logical ports, the assembly house records tool coordinates, and system firmware records corrected errors. A shared package map would let those observations be compared rather than manually reconstructed after a failure.

The economic benefit can exceed the direct recovery of marginal units. Suppose Morrowglass sells for $1,200 and observability features add $18 in silicon area, test time, and package complexity. If lane remapping and evidence-based binning recover only two additional percentage points of production, the expected gross value recovered is $24 per attempted unit, already greater than the assumed cost. The same mechanisms can shorten failure analysis, identify a drifting assembly tool before it produces a large bad lot, and support differentiated grades: full bandwidth, reduced bandwidth, or non-critical deployment. The exact break-even point will differ by product, but expensive modules do not need dramatic salvage rates for diagnosis to pay.

There are real objections. Diagnostic wires consume shoreline, sensors require calibration, and spare lanes add capacitance and routing congestion. At the finest pitches, a “small” addition can disturb power delivery or force a larger die. Observability must therefore be hierarchical. Most raw monitors can remain local, while compact summaries travel over an existing management link. Test structures can reuse functional transmitters and receivers rather than duplicate them. Redundancy should be concentrated where failures are independently isolatable; adding a spare lane is pointless if a shared clock failure disables the entire interface.

A second concern is that detailed telemetry may expose implementation information or create a control surface that hostile software could misuse. The answer is not to eliminate evidence but to separate collection from authority. Field software may receive rate-limited health summaries, while intrusive loopback and fault-injection modes remain available only during authenticated manufacturing or service states. This division will not remove every security risk, but opaque hardware is not inherently secure; it is merely harder for both attackers and maintainers to understand.

Finally, observability is not equally valuable in every market. A low-cost consumer package with two mature dies may rationally favour minimum area and simple pass-or-fail test. The stronger requirement applies to large accelerators, network processors, and other heterogeneous modules in which the assembled package embodies substantial sunk cost. Roadmaps and procurement criteria should reflect that distinction rather than impose one universal overhead.

The advanced-packaging industry has learned to measure what it wants to improve: bond pitch, interconnect density, energy per bit, and thermal resistance. It should add fault-localisation coverage and recoverable-interface fraction to that list. The next constraint on chiplet systems may not be the ability to place another thousand connections. It may be the ability to determine, quickly and precisely, which of the existing connections can still be trusted.
