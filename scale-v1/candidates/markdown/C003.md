## Artefact

# Designing the Sound of a Prosthesis

Noise is often treated as a defect in prosthetic design. Motors whine, joints click, and body-powered cables scrape inside their housings. Designers commonly try to suppress these sounds, but complete silence is not always the most useful goal. A prosthesis can also produce **acoustic feedback**: sound that helps its user understand what the device is doing.

Consider an electric hand. Its motor pitch rises under greater load, while a repeated click may indicate that a grip has reached its mechanical limit. If these cues are consistent, a user can sometimes identify a stalled finger or slipping object without looking. Sound may therefore complement visual attention and socket-based vibration, particularly during tasks such as carrying a bag or gripping an object behind the body.

Useful sound differs from accidental noise. It should correspond reliably to a device state, remain quiet enough not to dominate conversation, and avoid tones likely to be mistaken for alarms. In an illustrative prototype called the Kestrel hand, a low mechanical pulse could signal secure grip, while a higher pulse could indicate rising motor resistance. Designers might tune such cues through gear geometry, damping materials, or a small speaker. Mechanical cues consume little additional power, although they are harder to alter after manufacture.

There are important trade-offs. Audible feedback can expose prosthesis use when a wearer would prefer discretion. It may also disappear in a workshop or crowded station, and it offers limited value to users with hearing loss. Silencing every sound, however, can conceal early signs of wear. A practical design process should therefore let users compare quiet, mechanically informative, and electronically adjustable modes.

Acoustic testing deserves a place beside strength, comfort, and battery testing. Recording loudness alone is insufficient: designers should also ask whether users can correctly interpret each sound during realistic tasks and whether bystanders find it intrusive.
