## Artefact

# Safe Closure, Fair Access: An Avalanche System for the Greycap Night Corridor

**To:** Board, Talren Mountain Mobility Authority  
**From:** Office of Risk and Resilience  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Adopting an equitable avalanche-closure standard for the Greycap Pass corridor

## Decision requested

Approve a two-winter pilot of the **Safe Closure Standard** on Route M-8 through Greycap Pass, with a first-year budget ceiling of **1.45 million crowns**. The standard would combine improved avalanche detection and road controls with guaranteed transport, shelter, and employment protections for people affected by closures.

## Executive summary

The current avalanche safety system protects vehicles from entering known danger zones, but it does not protect all road users equally from the consequences of closure. Route M-8 connects the towns of Larkspur and Vey to three high-elevation resorts and the North Basin service district. When the pass closes, salaried visitors can often change plans or purchase lodging. Night-shift cleaners, kitchen staff, carers, and maintenance contractors are more likely to wait in cars, attempt informal detours, or lose pay.

This disparity is also a safety defect. A closure regime that predictably encourages gate bypasses or travel on unmonitored forestry roads is not functioning as a complete risk-control system.

The Authority should retain conservative closure powers while adding four elements: redundant detection, staffed gate control during high-risk periods, a closure mobility guarantee, and a limited worker non-retaliation rule. The pilot should be assessed against safety, access, and reliability metrics rather than closure duration alone.

## The problem

Greycap Pass crosses seven mapped avalanche paths over 31 kilometres. The present system relies on two weather stations, morning field observations, forecasts issued at six-hour intervals, and remotely operated gates at each end of the exposed segment. When conditions permit, specialist teams conduct preventive release work before reopening the road.

The system has two weaknesses. First, its sensing network has blind spots. Wind loading on the eastern start zones can change faster than the forecast cycle, while one weather station loses telemetry during severe icing. Operators therefore compensate—appropriately—with wide safety margins. That reduces exposure to avalanches but increases precautionary closure time.

Second, the system treats the closed gate as the end of the Authority’s responsibility. A 27-day observation exercise last winter recorded 126 people waiting more than two hours at the southern gate. Thirty-eight remained in vehicles after midnight, including nine travelling with children. Authority staff also documented 17 attempted diversions onto Quarry Spur, an unmaintained road beneath two uncontrolled slopes. No one was injured, but the pattern creates foreseeable exposure.

An accompanying questionnaire received 184 responses. Among hourly workers, 43% reported losing pay after a closure and 18% said they had been warned informally about future attendance. The questionnaire was voluntary and does not establish the prevalence of these outcomes across all corridor users, but it identifies a credible mechanism by which economic pressure undermines compliance.

## Proposed Safe Closure Standard

### 1. Build redundancy into detection

Install a compact Doppler avalanche radar covering the three paths above the eastern approach, add two wind-and-snow stations, and provide a radio backup for gate commands. Radar should confirm movement and support reopening decisions; it should not automatically open or close the road. The duty forecaster must retain authority because radar cannot reliably identify every slow release, cornice failure, or developing slab.

The Authority should publish the factors used in closure decisions—forecast danger, observed loading, visibility, control-work status, and sensor confidence—without publishing a mechanical threshold that could be mistaken for a promise of safety. Each decision should be logged for later review.

### 2. Replace unattended closures with controlled closure points

During forecast high-risk windows, both gates should have trained attendants, portable lighting, toilets, water, and a heated waiting cabin. Attendants would prevent unsafe diversions, provide multilingual updates, identify medical or safeguarding concerns, and direct travellers to transport or shelter.

This is not a proposal to allow exceptions through the avalanche zone. Emergency passage should occur only under the existing incident-command process. Gate staff must not be put in the position of bargaining over whose journey is important enough.

### 3. Establish a closure mobility guarantee

After 90 minutes of an unplanned closure, the Authority should provide free shuttle transport back to the nearest town. After four hours, any stranded person unable to return home should be eligible for basic overnight accommodation. People with mobility needs and adults travelling with young children should receive priority, but eligibility should not depend on employment status or proof of income.

The guarantee should be delivered through standby contracts with two local bus operators and participating inns. Universal eligibility is simpler to administer at a cold roadside gate and avoids forcing workers to disclose income or immigration information. To control costs, the Authority may cap reimbursement at the published basic-room rate and use public buildings as overflow shelter during prolonged events.

### 4. Prevent closures from becoming a wage-discipline tool

As a condition of resort access licences and service contracts, large corridor employers should be prohibited from penalising a worker for lateness or absence directly caused by an Authority closure. Employers would not be required to pay for hours not worked, but they would have to permit shift swaps or use of accrued leave without attendance points.

The Authority should issue digitally signed closure receipts through its existing alert service, with paper receipts available at gates. Receipts should confirm only the affected route and time window. They should not include location histories or identify which shuttle or shelter a person used.

This limited rule recognises that the Authority cannot credibly ask people to obey closures while licensed businesses threaten their continued employment for doing so. It also avoids creating a broader wage mandate outside the scope of this pilot.

## Options considered

**Maintain the current system.** This has the lowest direct cost and preserves operational discretion, but leaves known sensor failures and diversion behaviour unaddressed.

**Invest only in detection and automated gates.** This could shorten some closures and is attractive as a capital project. However, automation does little for travellers already at the gate and may create false confidence if a single threshold replaces professional judgement.

**Require all corridor users to carry transceivers.** This option should be rejected. A transceiver is valuable only when companions can conduct an immediate rescue and when users are trained. It does not make travel beneath an active road avalanche path acceptable. A mandate would also shift cost and responsibility onto workers while offering little protection to occupants buried inside vehicles.

The Safe Closure Standard best addresses both avalanche exposure and the behaviour induced by closures.

## Delivery and cost

The first winter should cover procurement, gate facilities, operator training, and baseline data collection. The second should test the full mobility and employment provisions. Estimated first-year costs are 720,000 crowns for sensors and communications, 310,000 for temporary gate facilities and staffing, 240,000 for transport and lodging contingencies, and 180,000 for training, evaluation, and reserve.

Funding should come from the Authority’s hazard-modernisation allocation, supplemented by a temporary 0.35-crown passenger levy on commercial resort shuttles. No charge should be collected from individual workers at the gate.

## Evaluation and safeguards

The Board should receive monthly winter reports covering gate bypass attempts, time from hazard clearance to reopening, sensor downtime, people waiting more than two hours, shuttle and shelter use, reported employment penalties, and near misses. Success should not be defined as fewer closure hours if shorter closures increase exposure.

An independent review after the second winter should compare outcomes with the prior three winters and with the unmodified South Talren corridor. Because snowfall and traffic vary, the review should report conditions alongside raw counts. The Board should then decide whether to make the standard permanent, revise it, or end it.
