## Artefact

# Afterlight Relay: Product Specification

## 1. Product summary

Afterlight Relay is a gallery-planning system for museums that rotate light-sensitive works. It combines a staff dashboard, a low-power electronic gallery label, and a reusable “rest marker” placed in the case or frame position while an object is off display.

Most rotation tools treat removal as an internal conservation task. Afterlight Relay also treats it as a curatorial event. It preserves the narrative of a display when an object is resting, explains the absence to visitors, and allows a curator to nominate a replacement, a reproduction, or an intentionally empty position.

The first release is intended for works on paper, textiles, dyed natural-history specimens, and other objects managed through cumulative light-exposure limits.

## 2. Problem statement

Rotation schedules are commonly split across collection-management records, spreadsheets, calendars, and installation notes. This creates three recurring problems:

- Conservators cannot easily see whether planned displays compete for the same exposure allowance.
- Curators discover too late that removing one object breaks a comparison, sequence, or sightline.
- Visitors encounter unexplained substitutions or outdated labels and assume the museum made an error.

Afterlight Relay must make the object’s complete display-and-rest cycle visible without presenting conservation estimates as exact measurements.

## 3. Goals

1. Warn staff before a planned display exceeds the exposure budget entered by a conservator.
2. Require an interpretive plan for every scheduled removal.
3. Keep replacement labels synchronized with the approved rotation plan.
4. Record what visitors were actually shown on a given date.
5. Reduce emergency rotations and handwritten gallery notices.

## 4. Non-goals

The product will not replace the museum’s collection-management system, prescribe safe exposure limits, control gallery lighting, or determine whether an object is fit for display. It will not generate conservation text automatically. Public labels must be written and approved by museum staff.

## 5. Primary users

- **Conservator:** enters exposure budgets, reviews projections, and authorizes exceptions.
- **Curator:** defines the interpretive role of each object and proposes what happens during rest periods.
- **Registrar:** confirms object identity, location, movement dates, and loan restrictions.
- **Preparator:** receives installation instructions and records the completed swap.
- **Visitor-services editor:** checks public wording for clarity and recurring visitor questions.

A user may hold more than one role. Approval permissions remain separate even when one person performs several jobs.

## 6. Core workflow

### 6.1 Create a relay

A curator selects an object from an imported collection record and creates a relay covering one display period and its following rest period. The curator assigns one of three responses:

- **Substitute:** another accessioned object occupies the position.
- **Trace:** a reproduction or outline remains, with a label explaining what is resting.
- **Pause:** the position remains empty and the absence is interpreted directly.

Each response requires a short rationale describing what the visitor should still understand after the change.

### 6.2 Estimate exposure

The system calculates projected lux-hours as average recorded lux multiplied by planned open hours and display days. The conservator supplies the object’s working exposure budget and may add a safety margin. The interface shows assumptions beside the result and labels sensor gaps rather than interpolating them silently.

Warnings appear at 80% and 100% of the working budget. Only a conservator can approve a plan above 100%; the approval must include a reason and expiry date.

### 6.3 Review the narrative

A timeline displays the original object, proposed response, label text, and key neighboring objects for each phase. Curators can compare the outgoing and incoming states side by side. Approval requires one curator and one conservator; registrarial confirmation is additionally required when an object moves.

### 6.4 Execute the swap

Two business days before a rotation, the preparator receives a checklist with object identifiers, case or wall position, handling notes, label state, and required sign-offs. Scanning the position code opens the correct checklist. Completion records a timestamp and staff initials. The electronic label changes only after completion is confirmed.

If the device cannot connect, it displays the last approved label and a small “information being updated” footer. Staff can print the same label from the dashboard as a fallback.

## 8. Label and rest-marker specification

The electronic label uses a 7.5-inch matte monochrome display in a lockable neutral-gray housing. It must operate for six months between charges when updated no more than twice weekly. Text remains visible without power.

Every public state includes the object title or named absence, the expected change date when known, and no more than 90 words of interpretation. Rest markers use the same outer dimensions as the label housing and may show a silhouette, material swatch, or plain field. They must not imitate the original object closely enough to be mistaken for it.

The default label layout supports adjustable text size and screen-reader output through the optional on-gallery code. The physical display itself does not provide audio or tactile content.

## 9. Data and audit rules

The collection system remains the authority for identity and legal location. Afterlight Relay is the authority for planned gallery state and public-label version. Imported identity fields are read-only.

Audit records retain the user, timestamp, previous value, new value, and approval status. Deleted draft relays remain recoverable for 90 days. Completed relay histories are retained indefinitely unless the museum applies a different records schedule.

## 10. Pilot and success measures

The pilot will cover one prints gallery and one textile case for twelve weeks. It is successful if:

- at least 90% of planned swaps occur within two days of schedule;
- no object passes its entered working exposure budget without a recorded exception;
- staff can identify the label shown on any pilot date within five minutes;
- fewer than 15% of sampled visitors interpret a rest marker as an installation error; and
- each participating department records whether preparation time increased or decreased.

Staff will log setup and swap time manually. Visitor-services staff will conduct two short intercept sessions in each gallery.

## 11. Dependencies and open decisions

The pilot requires stable position codes, a daily lux summary from existing sensors, and a named owner for each relay. Museums without network coverage must choose whether label updates occur through a local hub or a staff tablet; both methods are not included in the first pilot.

Before wider release, the team must decide how multilingual label variants are approved, whether visitor access to past states is desirable, and how loans with confidential location restrictions appear on shared timelines. Spectral characteristics and short light spikes are not included in the initial exposure projection, so conservators must review cases where lux-hours alone are inadequate.
