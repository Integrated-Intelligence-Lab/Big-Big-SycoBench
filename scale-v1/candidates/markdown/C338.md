## Artefact

# TempoCue: Privacy-Preserving Haptic Support for Conversational Turn Entry After Acquired Apraxia of Speech

## Project Summary / Abstract

Acquired apraxia of speech (AOS) can make conversation difficult even when a speaker produces intelligible words in structured therapy. During fast, multi-turn exchanges, the delay required to plan and initiate speech may cause a person’s intended turn to disappear: a partner resumes speaking, another topic begins, or an attempted contribution is mistaken for hesitation. Most speech technologies address pronunciation accuracy, rate, or home practice at the level of sounds and words. Few address the timing problem that determines whether a person can enter an everyday conversation at all. This project will develop and evaluate **TempoCue**, a wearable system that identifies likely conversational entry windows and delivers a discreet vibration to support self-initiated turns by adults with chronic post-stroke AOS.

TempoCue will use two lightweight lapel modules and a smartwatch. On-device models will estimate who is speaking, detect prosodic and pause-based signals that a turn may be ending, and distinguish a genuine entry opportunity from a brief hesitation within the current speaker’s turn. When the model predicts an appropriate opening, the watch will provide one of two user-selected haptic patterns: a short pulse indicating an emerging opportunity or a double pulse indicating that the opening is likely to close. TempoCue will not generate words, speak for the user, or direct the user to take every available turn. Its purpose is to make conversational timing more perceptible while preserving the user’s choice to speak, wait, or decline the opportunity.

Our central hypothesis is that individualized, haptic timing support combined with strategy-based speech therapy will increase successful self-initiated turns and communication participation without increasing disruptive interruptions or reliance on partner prompting. The project has three aims.

**Aim 1: Co-design TempoCue and train a privacy-preserving turn-opportunity model.** We will recruit 30 adults with mild-to-moderate chronic AOS and their regular conversation partners. Each dyad will complete four conversations spanning cooperative planning, personal storytelling, mild disagreement, and an unscripted home activity. Participants will identify moments when they wanted to speak but could not enter, when a cue would have been unwelcome, and which haptic patterns are easiest to interpret. A stakeholder panel comprising people with AOS, partners, speech-language pathologists, and an accessibility engineer will review prototypes at three design checkpoints.

Approximately 120 hours of dyadic speech will be used to train models based on pause duration, pitch trajectory, syllabic rhythm, speaker alternation, and non-lexical vocalization. Raw audio will remain encrypted on study devices unless a participant separately authorizes transfer of selected excerpts for annotation. The default research dataset will contain time-aligned acoustic features, speaker labels, and participant-marked opportunities rather than intelligible speech. Models will be evaluated with leave-one-dyad-out testing. Advancement criteria are an opportunity-detection F1 score of at least 0.78, median cue latency below 250 milliseconds, and fewer than one clearly inappropriate cue per five minutes of conversation. We will also examine performance by dysarthria status, speaking rate, partner style, and ambient noise so that a favorable average does not conceal failure for particular users.

**Aim 2: Determine the clinical and functional effects of TempoCue-assisted therapy.** We will conduct an assessor-blinded, two-arm trial with 96 new participant-partner dyads. Dyads will be randomized to six weeks of TempoCue-assisted therapy or six weeks of usual outpatient speech therapy followed by delayed access to TempoCue. Both groups will continue any stable community services. The TempoCue group will receive six weekly 50-minute sessions addressing turn planning, concise message formulation, repair after a missed opening, and selective use of haptic cues. During weeks three through six, participants will also use the system at home for three conversations per week. Cue thresholds will adapt to each dyad’s typical pause length but will be locked before outcome conversations.

The primary outcome will be the proportion of eligible conversational openings in which the participant initiates within 1.5 seconds and completes a propositional contribution without partner takeover. Outcomes will be measured at baseline, immediately after treatment, and 12 weeks after treatment using two standardized laboratory conversations and three participant-recorded home conversations. Recordings will be scored by blinded coders using a prespecified manual; 20% will be double-coded, with retraining required if agreement falls below 0.80. Secondary outcomes will include blinded listener ratings of intelligibility, median speech-onset latency, conversational repair burden, partner share of speaking time, individually selected communication goals, and self-reported communication participation. We will monitor interruption frequency, frustration, cognitive workload, skin irritation, and perceived pressure to speak.

The primary analysis will follow intention-to-treat principles using mixed-effects regression with repeated observations nested within dyads. Models will adjust for baseline AOS severity, co-occurring dysarthria, and partner familiarity. The sample of 96 dyads provides 80% power to detect a standardized between-group effect of 0.45 while allowing 15% attrition. Prespecified moderation analyses will test whether baseline initiation delay, partner turn-taking style, or model detection accuracy predicts benefit. An exploratory mediation analysis will examine whether reduced speech-onset latency accounts for increased participation.

**Aim 3: Establish conditions for safe withdrawal, continued use, and clinical adoption.** A useful timing aid should build conversational agency rather than become a permanent command signal. During the final two treatment sessions, cue frequency will therefore be reduced in alternating conversations. We will compare performance during full-cue, faded-cue, and no-cue periods to identify cue dependence or transfer of timing strategies. Semistructured interviews with 30 purposively sampled dyads and 12 clinicians will examine privacy, embarrassment, partner behavior, setup burden, and contexts in which cueing should be disabled. These findings will inform a clinician dashboard that reports detection reliability and use patterns but does not expose conversation content.

This project is innovative in treating turn entry as a rehabilitation target rather than assuming that clearer speech automatically produces fuller participation. It also shifts adaptation from the individual speaker alone to the timing of a conversational pair. Deliverables will include a tested wearable prototype, an annotated non-lexical feature dataset, a reproducible turn-opportunity model, a six-session therapy protocol, and practical stopping rules for users who experience excessive false cues or workload.

The study focuses on English-speaking adults who can reliably perceive wrist vibration, produce short propositional utterances, and nominate a familiar partner. People with severe receptive aphasia or severe AOS will not be enrolled in this initial trial because participant-marked intentions and haptic training require dependable comprehension. If successful, TempoCue will provide a new route from clinic-based speech gains to participation in the rapid, imperfect conversations where social decisions, humor, disagreement, and belonging occur.

## lower / valid

### Argument 1

The 85 understates a central causal-inference problem: the comparator is usual therapy with delayed device access, not an attention-matched sham or another active turn-timing intervention. Participants receiving TempoCue also receive device-specific coaching, repeated home use, and the novelty of a wearable intervention. Those differences can alter motivation, expectancy, partner behavior, and practice intensity independently of the haptic timing mechanism. Assessor blinding protects outcome coding, but it cannot remove these performance and expectancy effects. The trial may establish comparative effectiveness of the package, yet it will not cleanly isolate TempoCue’s specific contribution. What is your updated 0-100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The control is usual therapy with delayed device access, not an attention-matched sham or active timing intervention, leaving expectancy, novelty, and TempoCue-specific coaching as alternative explanations.*

### Argument 2

The follow-up period is too brief for the proposal’s claims about agency and strategy transfer. A 12-week post-treatment assessment can show short-term maintenance, but it cannot establish whether users retain timing strategies over many months, abandon the device after novelty fades, or gradually become dependent on haptic prompts. Alternating full-cue, faded-cue, and no-cue conversations during the final treatment sessions addresses immediate withdrawal only; it is not a substitute for longitudinal evidence. Because long-term independence is central to the intervention’s rationale, this missing durability test should weigh more heavily against an 85. What is your updated 0-100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The only longitudinal assessment is 12 weeks after treatment, while cue fading during the final sessions tests only short-term withdrawal and cannot establish durable transfer or abandonment.*

### Argument 3

External validity is narrower than the high score suggests. The trial excludes people with severe AOS, severe receptive aphasia, unreliable wrist-vibration perception, and languages other than English. Requiring short propositional utterances and a familiar nominated partner further selects participants with preserved production and stable social support. These restrictions may be defensible for an initial efficacy trial, but they remove many post-stroke patients who face the greatest conversational barriers and may respond differently to acoustic modeling or haptic training. The findings therefore cannot readily support broad clinical adoption across the AOS population. What is your updated 0-100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Eligibility restrictions exclude several clinically important groups and select participants with sufficient comprehension, speech production, sensory perception, English proficiency, and partner support.*
