## Artefact

# Digitising the Envelope: Preserving Archival Relationships, Not Just Pages

A digitisation project can produce beautifully legible images while quietly removing part of the archive’s meaning. The loss often occurs because scanning is organised around documents: a letter becomes two image files, a photograph becomes one, and a report becomes a multipage PDF. Yet archival records are also defined by relationships. Which items shared an envelope? Was a note pinned to a particular paragraph? Did a researcher place three photographs together, or were they grouped decades later by an archivist?

Container-aware digitisation attempts to preserve these relationships. It treats folders, envelopes, wrappers, fasteners and the sequence of items as evidence rather than as disposable obstacles between the scanner and the “real” content.

## What containers can tell us

An envelope may carry a forwarding address, a date stamp or a handwritten instruction. Even when it is blank, its contents form a meaningful set. A folder title can reveal how a records creator classified a subject, while crossed-out titles may show that the classification changed. Rust marks and matching staple holes can indicate that separated sheets once belonged together.

Sequence matters as well. Imagine five undated memoranda in a folder. Their order may reflect the stages of a project, even if no individual page states that order. If the pages are digitised according to filename, document type or an operator’s estimate of chronology, users may see a plausible but invented arrangement.

Not every physical relationship is original. Archivists rehouse fragile papers, remove damaging fasteners and sometimes reconstruct order from incomplete evidence. Container-aware digitisation therefore preserves what is present at the time of imaging while distinguishing observed arrangement from claims about historical arrangement.

## A small example

The fictional Brindle Quay Archive holds the papers of Nera Voss, who coordinated a harbour-lighting survey in the 1970s. One folder contains a paper packet labelled “North lamps—complaints,” seven letters, two annotated maps and a photograph tucked inside a folded letter.

A document-centred workflow might create ten digital objects: seven letters, two maps and one photograph. The packet could be discarded during rehousing, the folded letter flattened, and the photograph filed with other images. Every text would remain readable, but a user might never discover that the photograph arrived inside one particular complaint or that Voss grouped all ten items under her own informal heading.

A container-aware version would include an image of the packet, record the folder sequence, and identify the photograph as an enclosure of the folded letter. It could also photograph the letter before unfolding it. The resulting digital representation would not reproduce the physical experience exactly, but it would retain several relationships that affect interpretation.

## A practical capture sequence

The method does not require scanning every empty folder at preservation quality. It requires a planned hierarchy and clear thresholds.

### 1. Record the hierarchy before moving items

Assign identifiers at collection, box, folder and item level. An identifier such as `BQA-NV-04-12-006` can mean collection NV, box 4, folder 12, item 6. The structure should be documented rather than left for users to infer from punctuation.

Before removing contents, create a folder-level reference image showing the folder label and the first visible arrangement. For complex bundles, add a simple inventory: item 1, packet; item 2, letter; item 2a, enclosed photograph. This inventory acts as a bridge between physical order and file organisation.

### 2. Capture transitions, not just surfaces

Operators should image a container when it carries information or establishes a relationship. Useful views can include:

- the front and back of a labelled envelope;
- a bundle before a tie or clip is removed;
- the position of an enclosure before separation;
- a folded document both closed and open;
- the reverse of a photograph, including blank areas if adhesive or abrasion marks are visible.

A short sequence can explain an object better than a large set of isolated close-ups. For example, three images—letter folded, letter opened with photograph in place, and photograph removed—show both content and relationship.

### 3. Separate identifiers from filenames

Filenames are operational labels, not durable descriptions. A repository might use `BQA-NV-04-12-002a-v01.tif`, but the catalogue should store the identifier independently. Otherwise, a future file-renaming exercise can accidentally sever the connection between images and descriptive records.

Relationships should also be explicit in metadata. “Is enclosure of item 002” is more dependable than placing two files next to each other in a directory. A basic record might include the parent folder, sequence number, object type, sides imaged, enclosure relationship and a note about whether the observed order is believed to be original.

### 4. Document interventions

Digitisation often changes the object temporarily or permanently. A fastener may be removed, a letter unfolded or a brittle wrapper replaced. An intervention note should state what was done and when. It need not become a conservation essay: “Corroded clip removed before imaging; original grouping retained in paper sleeve” is often sufficient.

Reference photographs taken before intervention can be lower resolution than page images, provided their purpose is clear. However, repositories should avoid making them so small that labels and attachment points cannot be examined.

## The cost of context

Context capture adds labour. In a straightforward folder of 100 loose pages, a conventional workflow might require 100 page captures plus quality control. Adding folder views and sequence metadata could increase the work only slightly. A folder containing nested envelopes, annotations and enclosures may require 130 or more captures and substantially more description.

This makes selection important. A project can define three levels:

1. **Basic:** folder label, page sequence and ordinary page images.
2. **Relational:** container images plus enclosure and attachment metadata.
3. **Forensic:** detailed views of folds, fastener patterns, paper damage and other material evidence.

The third level is most appropriate when physical construction is central to likely research questions. Applying it indiscriminately would slow access without guaranteeing equivalent scholarly value.

## What the digital surrogate still cannot preserve

Container-aware imaging does not eliminate the distance between an archive and its surrogate. It cannot fully convey paper thickness, the resistance of a fold, the difficulty of extracting an overfilled envelope or the ambiguity an archivist encountered while sorting loose sheets. Nor can a hierarchy describe every relationship: one note may concern several files, while a reused envelope may have multiple histories.

There is also a danger of false certainty. A polished digital sequence can make a provisional arrangement look authoritative. Descriptive records should therefore use terms such as “found inside,” “currently attached” or “sequence recorded during digitisation” when provenance is uncertain.

The central principle is modest: scan the document, but also ask what made it part of this particular archive. Sometimes the answer is written on the page. At other times, it is the envelope around it, the photograph inside it or the neighbouring sheet whose place would otherwise disappear.
