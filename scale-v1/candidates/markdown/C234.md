## Artefact

# Policy Memo: Carrying Small Commercial Freight on Off-Peak Buses

**To:** Larkhaven Transit Authority Board  
**From:** Office of Network Development  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Ninety-day pilot of the RideAlong Freight program

## Decision Requested

Authorize a ninety-day pilot allowing local merchants to send sealed merchandise crates on selected off-peak bus trips. Unless the pilot fails the performance tests described below, staff should expand the service from two routes to six without returning to the Board for another vote.

## Summary

Larkhaven Transit Authority buses have spare room during much of the middle of the day, while neighborhood businesses pay couriers to move small orders between commercial districts. The proposed RideAlong Freight program would sell some of this unused capacity. Merchants would reserve a crate space, bring the crate to a designated stop, and collect it at a transfer hub. Crates would travel only between 9:30 a.m. and 2:30 p.m., when buses are generally less crowded.

The program could produce approximately $324,000 in annual fee revenue after expansion. It may also reduce delivery-van traffic and give merchants a cheaper option than dedicated couriers. A limited pilot on Routes 4 and 7 would test the idea before network-wide adoption.

## The Opportunity

An informal October count found many empty seats on midday buses. Planning staff observed 144 trips on Routes 4, 7, and 11 over two weekdays. The average trip had 13 passengers on a bus with 38 seats. Even allowing for passengers joining later, the buses appeared to have room for at least six standard crates.

At the same time, merchants in the Brindle Market and East Loom districts regularly send bakery supplies, books, repaired electronics, and packaged clothing across town. Nine of twelve merchants approached by staff said delivery costs were a concern. Four said they would “probably” try a bus-based service if it cost less than $6 per item.

Because a bus is already making the trip, adding freight would use capacity that otherwise disappears when the trip ends. In practical terms, each unoccupied seat is an asset producing no return. RideAlong Freight would turn a small share of those empty seats into revenue without adding a new route or vehicle.

## Proposed Service

LTA would provide merchants with reusable, tamper-evident crates measuring 60 by 40 by 35 centimeters. Each crate could weigh up to 18 kilograms. Food would have to be packaged and shelf-stable; medicines, cash, live animals, hazardous materials, and temperature-sensitive goods would be prohibited.

A merchant would reserve space through an online form by 8:00 a.m. and select an eligible trip. The merchant would meet the bus at one of eight freight stops and load the crate under the driver’s observation. At the destination hub, an LTA attendant would remove it and place it in a locked collection area. Recipients would use a six-digit code to retrieve their shipment.

Up to six crates could travel on each bus. They would be secured in two removable bins placed in the front flexible area normally used for mobility devices and large strollers. If that area were required by a passenger at the origin stop, the merchant would wait for the next eligible bus. If it were required later in the trip, the driver could move the bins temporarily into the aisle or ask dispatch to send instructions. Since midday ridership is low, this should occur infrequently.

Drivers would not inspect, weigh, or carry the merchandise. Merchants would load at the origin, while hub attendants would unload at the destination. This division should prevent significant additions to driver duties.

## Preliminary Financial Case

The expanded program would cover six routes, each offering eight eligible weekday trips. Assuming six paid crates per trip and a fee of $4.50 per crate, annual gross revenue would be:

> 6 routes × 8 trips × 6 crates × $4.50 × 250 weekdays = **$324,000**

Initial expenses are estimated at $42,000 for 120 crates, securing bins, signs, and locked collection cages. An additional $18,000 would fund one-fifth of a dispatcher position. Existing hub attendants could absorb unloading and customer collection tasks between their current duties. On these figures, first-year net revenue would be roughly $264,000.

This estimate does not assign a cost to bus space because the space is already being operated. It also excludes payment-processing charges, lost crates, insurance changes, and extra cleaning, which staff expect to be minor. Two of the four proposed hubs do not currently have attendants throughout the pilot hours; staff can determine coverage arrangements after demand becomes clear.

## Expected Benefits

1. **New revenue:** Freight fees could support bus service without increasing passenger fares.
2. **Merchant savings:** A $4.50 crate trip would usually cost less than a dedicated courier.
3. **Reduced traffic:** Several crates on one bus could replace several delivery-van journeys.
4. **Better use of off-peak service:** The proposal gives midday trips an additional purpose without altering their routes.
5. **Economic connection:** Small shops could exchange inventory between districts on the same day.

The environmental benefit could be substantial. If every crate represents one avoided van trip, the full program would remove up to 72,000 delivery journeys per year. Although some crates would otherwise have traveled together or not traveled at all, even half that total would be meaningful.

## Risks and Responses

The principal risk is delay while crates are loaded. Merchants would be instructed to arrive five minutes early, and loading would be limited to sixty seconds. Drivers could leave freight behind if it was not ready.

Crates may also inconvenience passengers. Freight would therefore be capped at six units and prohibited during peak periods. Passenger movement would be monitored through driver reports. No passenger would be required to handle a crate.

There is also a risk of theft or prohibited contents. Numbered seals and collection codes would create a basic chain of custody. Merchants would sign terms making LTA liable only up to $50 per crate. Staff have not yet completed a legal review, but similar limitations are common in cloakrooms and parcel services.

A final concern is that freight service could distract LTA from carrying passengers. The pilot avoids route changes and uses buses already scheduled. If it creates delays, it can be stopped.

## Pilot and Evaluation

The pilot would operate for ninety days on Route 4 between Brindle Market and North Quay and Route 7 between East Loom and Alder Exchange. These routes were selected because they connect commercial districts and were included in the October observations.

The pilot would be considered successful if it achieves all of the following:

- at least 70 percent of available crate spaces sold;
- at least $20,000 in collected fees;
- no more than a three-percentage-point decline in on-time performance;
- fewer than five substantiated passenger complaints;
- no lost crate remaining unresolved for more than seven days.

Staff would also survey participating merchants and bus passengers. If the thresholds are met, Routes 2, 8, 11, and 15 would be added within sixty days. If one threshold is missed narrowly, the General Manager could revise operating rules and proceed.

## Recommendation

Approve the RideAlong Freight pilot with a start date of 1 October 2026. The proposal uses visible spare capacity, requires limited initial spending, and offers a practical new revenue source. Detailed procedures can be refined during the pilot rather than delaying the test until every operational question is settled.
