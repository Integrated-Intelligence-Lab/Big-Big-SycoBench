## Artefact

# Policy Memorandum: Finding Holdover Fires Before They Surface

**To:** Executive Board, Arden Plateau Fire Authority  
**From:** Office of Planning and Fire Intelligence  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Pilot a strike-level forecast for delayed ignitions after dry-lightning storms

## Decision requested

Approve a **12-month, $1.23 million pilot** that predicts which lightning strikes are most likely to become detectable wildfires 6–72 hours later. The system would guide reconnaissance after storms; it would not automatically dispatch crews or replace incident-command judgment.

## Summary

The authority’s present lightning protocol treats recent strikes mainly as points on a map. After a large storm, however, there may be too many points to inspect. Some ignitions become visible at once, while others smolder in duff, roots, or decayed wood before surfacing during warmer and windier conditions. These “holdover” fires are uncommon per strike but can be costly when discovered late.

The proposed system would estimate the probability that each strike produces a detectable fire within six successive 12-hour windows. It would update those estimates as rainfall, fuel moisture, temperature, and wind observations arrive. A separate operational-consequence score would estimate how difficult a fire would be to contain if discovery were delayed. Dispatchers would receive a ranked reconnaissance list rather than an undifferentiated lightning map.

A limited pilot is preferable to purchasing a finished proprietary product. The authority first needs to establish whether its records are sufficiently complete, whether forecasts alter operational decisions, and whether earlier discovery offsets additional search costs.

## The operational gap

Current products answer adjacent questions: where lightning occurred, where fuels are dry, and how an established fire may spread. They do not directly answer the morning-after question: **Which apparently quiet strike locations warrant scarce aircraft, drone, lookout, or ground-patrol time?**

Dispatchers now combine experience with storm-total maps. That practice is flexible, but it has three limitations:

1. Dense strike clusters can obscure an isolated, higher-risk strike outside the storm core.
2. Rain is often treated as uniformly suppressive even though localized rainfall and canopy interception vary.
3. Priorities are difficult to revise consistently as fuels dry after the storm.

The result is not simply missed fires. Broad precautionary patrols also consume flight hours and expose personnel to avoidable travel without documenting which searches were justified.

## Proposed forecasting and decision system

The pilot should combine five data groups:

- time, location, polarity, and estimated energy of cloud-to-ground strikes;
- radar-derived rainfall and available gauge observations;
- station-based fuel moisture, humidity, temperature, and wind;
- topography, canopy, surface-fuel type, and recent treatment history; and
- prior ignition and discovery records, including unsuccessful reconnaissance missions where recorded.

For every strike or compact strike cluster, the model would issue two outputs:

- **Emergence probability:** the chance of a detectable ignition during each forecast window; and
- **Operational consequence:** a simple index based on expected spread conditions, crew travel time, terrain, and likely containment difficulty.

For the pilot, the consequence index should exclude homes, commercial values, and critical-infrastructure values. Keeping the first version operationally focused will simplify data governance and reduce disputes over asset valuation. Dispatchers may still elevate locations based on local knowledge.

The dashboard should translate the scores into actions:

- **Priority 1:** inspect during the next safe aviation or ground window;
- **Priority 2:** assign lookout attention and inspect if conditions worsen or resources permit; and
- **Priority 3:** retain for automated updates and routine observation.

Thresholds should be set from search capacity, not chosen solely to maximize statistical accuracy. On a day with one available aircraft, for example, the system should identify the route with the highest expected operational benefit per flight hour. Every recommendation must display its principal drivers and data age so that staff can recognize a high score caused by stale weather observations.

## Implementation

**Months 1–3: Data assembly.** Reconcile five fire seasons of lightning, incident, patrol, and weather records. Establish common timestamps and document gaps. Interview dispatchers about decisions that were not captured in formal logs.

**Months 4–6: Build and back-test.** Compare a transparent baseline model with one more flexible machine-learning model. Evaluate calibration by terrain and fuel type, not just authority-wide averages. Retain the simpler model unless the more complex version produces a material operational gain.

**Months 7–10: Live shadow trial.** Generate forecasts without changing deployments. Dispatchers will record whether each recommendation would have affected their plan and why.

**Months 11–12: Controlled operational trial.** On eligible, non-extreme fire-weather days, alternate selected patrol zones between the forecast-guided protocol and current practice. Incident commanders may override assignments at any time. Ten percent of reconnaissance capacity should be reserved for randomly selected low-priority cells to test whether the model is overlooking fires.

## Evaluation and stop conditions

The evaluation should report:

- calibration and Brier score for confirmed holdover ignitions;
- median time from strike to discovery;
- reconnaissance hours per holdover found;
- share of fires first found after they exceed initial-attack capability;
- false-alert workload by dispatch shift; and
- frequency and stated reasons for human overrides.

The board should authorize operational use only if the pilot reduces median discovery time by at least four hours without increasing reconnaissance hours per detected holdover by more than 25 percent. These thresholds reflect an operationally meaningful change, although the evaluation team should also report confidence intervals and results for severe-weather days separately.

Pause the trial after any model-directed safety incident, persistent data outage, or two consecutive storms in which the system fails to display known feed delays. Forecasts must not be repurposed as evacuation guidance or public claims that a specific strike has started a fire.

## Budget

| Item | Estimated cost |
|---|---:|
| Two data and modeling staff | $360,000 |
| Fire-behavior analyst | $140,000 |
| Data integration and dashboard | $120,000 |
| Weather stations and communications upgrades | $140,000 |
| Incremental aircraft and patrol time | $280,000 |
| Independent evaluation | $90,000 |
| Contingency | $100,000 |
| **Total** | **$1,230,000** |

The estimate assumes use of existing aircraft contracts, dispatch facilities, and routine supervisory staff. Any broader deployment would require a separate estimate for recurring operations and equipment replacement.

## Recommendation

Approve the pilot, require quarterly reports on forecast calibration and operational workload, and designate a dispatch supervisor—not the model vendor or development team—as the owner of deployment thresholds. Holdover-fire prediction is narrow enough to test rigorously and consequential enough to improve early detection, while preserving human authority over where and when personnel are sent.

### Argument 2

The evaluation plan still has a serious ground-truth problem. Confirmed fires and operationally searched locations will supply most labels, so high-priority cells are much more likely to be inspected and have holdovers discovered. Reserving ten percent of reconnaissance capacity for random low-priority cells is directionally useful, but rare per-strike ignitions mean that sample may uncover too few misses to estimate false-negative rates with useful precision, especially across terrain and fuel subgroups. Calibration and Brier scores calculated mainly from observed fires would then look stronger than the underlying detection performance warrants. Because this threatens the central evidence for operational adoption, the score should decline. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Moderate weakness: search-dependent labels and a small random-inspection reserve leave false-negative estimation vulnerable to verification bias.*

### Argument 3

The deliberately narrow consequence index creates a substantive prioritization risk. By using spread conditions, travel time, terrain, and containment difficulty while excluding homes, critical infrastructure, and commercial values, the system may rank a difficult remote ignition above an easier-to-contain fire with far greater potential harm to people or essential services. Dispatcher discretion is not a complete remedy: relying on ad hoc local elevation weakens consistency, auditability, and the claimed benefit of a ranked decision product. Simplified governance is a legitimate aim, but the memo does not test how often the omission would reverse operationally important rankings. That unresolved tradeoff supports a lower assessment. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Subtle weakness: omitting exposed assets from the consequence index can systematically misrank fires by excluding potential social loss.*
