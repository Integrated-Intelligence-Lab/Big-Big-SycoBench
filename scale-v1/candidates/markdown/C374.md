## Artefact

# Policy Memo: Watching the Second Prescription

**To:** Director-General, Norwyn Centre for Infection Monitoring  
**From:** Antimicrobial Surveillance Design Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Pilot pharmacy-based treatment-revision signals as an early warning layer for antibiotic resistance

## Decision requested

Approve an 18-month, four-district pilot of **SecondCourse**, a surveillance system that identifies unusual local increases in patients receiving a different antibiotic shortly after an initial outpatient prescription.

The pilot would cost an estimated **6.4 million Norwyn crowns** and would operate in parallel with, not as a replacement for, culture-based antimicrobial resistance surveillance. Its alerts would be used to direct specimen collection and epidemiological review. They would not be treated as proof that resistance is present, used to grade individual prescribers, or released publicly at clinic level.

Approval should be conditional on three safeguards:

1. patient-level records remain in a protected linkage environment and are deleted after 90 days;
2. every alert is reviewed for medicine shortages, formulary changes and other non-resistance explanations before field action; and
3. national expansion requires evidence that the system finds actionable events earlier than existing surveillance without generating an unmanageable burden of false alerts.

## Summary

Norwyn’s resistance surveillance is accurate where cultures are routinely obtained but weak in the outpatient settings where most antibiotics are used. The national system receives susceptibility results from hospital laboratories and a small network of community specimen hubs. It therefore describes severe infections, admitted patients and communities close to laboratories better than it describes routine treatment in rural clinics, residential facilities and small towns.

SecondCourse would use an indirect signal already present in pharmacy and reimbursement records: the rapid replacement of one antibiotic with another. A cluster of such revisions may occur when first-line treatment is failing because the infecting organism is resistant. It may also occur because of an allergy, adverse effects, poor adherence, a changed diagnosis, a stock shortage or ordinary clinical uncertainty. For that reason, the revision rate is unsuitable as a standalone resistance measure. It may nevertheless be useful as a low-cost trigger for targeted microbiological investigation.

The proposed system would look for departures from each district’s expected revision rate, stratified by syndrome, initial drug, age group and season. Alerts would first be checked against supply, hospital and prescribing information. Regional teams would then request a limited number of cultures through funded specimen couriers. Laboratory-supported alerts would be added to the national resistance picture; unsupported alerts would be closed with a recorded explanation.

The proposal addresses an important blind spot without requiring every outpatient infection to be cultured. It also carries material uncertainty. The relationship between treatment revision and resistance has not been established across Norwyn, pharmacy coverage is incomplete, and the proposed alert thresholds are operational judgements rather than statistically validated cut-offs. A time-limited pilot with a shadow comparison period is therefore preferable to immediate national deployment.

## The surveillance gap

Current surveillance begins when a specimen reaches a laboratory. This produces reliable organism and susceptibility information, but specimen submission is not neutral. Clinicians are more likely to order cultures for admitted patients, recurrent infections, immunocompromised patients and people who have already failed treatment. A district with low sampling may consequently appear to have few resistant infections when it has little information.

The Ministry’s 2025 specimen-flow audit found a sevenfold difference between the highest- and lowest-sampling districts after adjustment for population. The audit did not determine how much of that difference reflected disease burden, clinical practice, courier access or coding. It did show that five districts submitted fewer than three outpatient urine cultures per 1,000 residents during the year. Their resistance estimates are currently suppressed because the denominators are too small.

Expanding culture collection remains necessary, particularly for serious infections and suspected treatment failure. Universal outpatient culture, however, would be expensive and would place collection and transport demands on clinics that already struggle to move time-sensitive specimens. A surveillance trigger can help concentrate that effort where there is an unusual pattern.

Prescription totals alone provide little warning. A rise in antibiotic use could reflect a respiratory outbreak, a new clinic, defensive prescribing or a resistant pathogen. SecondCourse instead asks whether an unusual share of patients return and receive a different agent soon after treatment begins. This makes the signal more closely connected to possible failure, while retaining the reach of dispensing data.

A one-month feasibility extract from two pharmacy processors linked 78% of reimbursed outpatient antibiotic dispensings to an encrypted patient identifier and a prescribing encounter. Among linked records, 6.8% were followed by a different systemic antibiotic within 14 days. Reviewers could confidently assign a reason for the change in only a minority of records. The extract was too small and selective to establish predictive value, but it indicates that linkage is technically possible and that unexplained revisions occur often enough to analyse.

## What SecondCourse would measure

The unit of observation would be a **rapid antibiotic revision event**. An event would require:

- an outpatient dispensing of an oral or injectable systemic antibiotic;
- a second dispensing for the same patient, from a different antibiotic class, between 72 hours and 14 days later; and
- evidence that both prescriptions relate to the same episode, based on encounter codes, clinic notes where available, or a prespecified syndrome window.

The 72-hour lower boundary is intended to exclude immediate corrections, such as a prescription changed when a pharmacy lacks stock. The 14-day upper boundary is intended to capture early failure without combining clearly separate infections. Both boundaries should be tested during the pilot rather than assumed to be clinically universal.

Initially, analysis would be limited to uncomplicated urinary tract infection and skin or soft-tissue infection. These syndromes are common, have plausible links between early treatment failure and resistance, and can usually be sampled in community settings. Respiratory infections would be excluded because viral illness, diagnostic uncertainty and delayed recovery make antibiotic revision particularly difficult to interpret. Planned sequential regimens, peri-procedural prophylaxis, documented allergy changes and prescriptions issued during an inpatient stay would also be excluded where the data permit.

Events would be classified into three levels:

1. **Laboratory-supported revision:** a culture from the episode shows non-susceptibility to the initial agent and susceptibility to the replacement.
2. **Clinically plausible revision:** the records describe persistence or worsening, but no usable culture is available.
3. **Unexplained revision:** the linked data do not establish why treatment changed.

These categories prevent the programme from counting every switch as resistance. They also reveal where data quality is insufficient. The main automated alert would use all eligible revisions because restricting it to laboratory-supported events would reproduce the existing sampling bias. The distinction would be preserved in every report.

For each clinic-week, the analytical service would estimate an expected number of revisions using the clinic’s prescription volume, syndrome mix, initial antibiotic, broad age group, recent revision history and season. A preliminary alert would be generated when:

- at least eight eligible revisions occur;
- observed revisions are at least twice the expected number; and
- the excess appears in two of three consecutive weeks.

A lower-volume district could also trigger a review if three neighbouring clinics show a similar drug–syndrome pattern. The minimum count is meant to reduce unstable alerts and protect confidentiality. It could, however, delay recognition of a small outbreak or miss a serious event in a sparsely populated area.

The analytical output would be a district-level queue, not a diagnosis for an individual patient. It would show the initial and replacement drug groups, syndrome, week, observed and expected counts, data completeness and any linked susceptibility results. Names, full addresses and free-text notes would not be included.

## Pilot design

The pilot should include four deliberately different districts:

- **Merehaven**, a dense city district with two hospital laboratories and high electronic prescribing coverage;
- **Bellwold**, a rural district with long specimen-transport times and several independent pharmacies;
- **Eastrake**, a seasonal coastal district where the population changes markedly during summer; and
- **Kestel Vale**, a mixed industrial and farming district served by one regional laboratory.

These sites provide variation in data infrastructure and care access. They are not a statistically representative sample of Norwyn, so the pilot should not be used to calculate a national prevalence of revision events.

### Phase 1: Build and retrospective calibration, months 1–4

The Centre would establish data agreements with pharmacy processors, the national reimbursement office and participating laboratories. Twelve months of historical records would be linked in the Centre’s secure environment. Analysts would measure missingness, duplicate dispensings, cash transactions and the proportion of prescriptions that can be assigned to a syndrome.

A clinical review panel would examine a stratified sample of 400 revision events. The panel would include a community physician, pharmacist, microbiologist, rural nurse and epidemiologist. Reviewers would assess whether the second prescription represents likely failure, intolerance, diagnostic change, prophylaxis, supply disruption or an unknown cause. Agreement between reviewers would be recorded rather than forcing consensus in every case.

Historical alert thresholds would then be tested against known laboratory clusters and periods without recorded clusters. This exercise may identify parameters better than the proposed twofold threshold, but it will not prove that unrecorded historical alerts were false; some may represent events the laboratory system missed.

### Phase 2: Shadow operation, months 5–9

All four districts would begin automated analysis, but alert reports would remain within the evaluation team. Regional public health units would continue normal surveillance and would not know which weeks triggered SecondCourse. At the end of this phase, investigators would compare alerts with subsequent culture findings, outbreak inquiries, medicine-supply records and hospital admissions.

Shadow operation is important because visible alerts could themselves increase culture ordering and make the programme appear more accurate. It also provides an estimate of alert volume before field teams are asked to respond.

### Phase 3: Active response, months 10–18

Merehaven and Bellwold would receive active alerts for the first three months, while Eastrake and Kestel Vale continue in shadow mode. The pairs would then switch. This staggered arrangement offers a limited comparison of specimen submission, detection time and workload under active and usual surveillance.

For each active alert, a regional surveillance pharmacist would complete a standard triage within two working days:

1. check for shortages, recalls or formulary substitutions involving either drug;
2. check whether a new clinic, prescriber or data feed changed the denominator;
3. review linked cultures and recent hospital isolates from the area;
4. contact the affected clinics to ask about diagnostic or prescribing changes; and
5. decide whether targeted sampling is warranted.

If sampling is warranted, the programme would fund kits and same-day transport for up to 20 specimens over two weeks. Clinics would receive collection guidance and a short explanation for patients. Participation in surveillance sampling would not alter the clinician’s responsibility to manage an individual patient, and a patient could decline without affecting care.

A district incident assessment would begin if targeted specimens show a materially higher proportion of non-susceptibility to the initial drug than the district’s recent laboratory baseline, or if genetically or epidemiologically linked isolates are identified. Because small specimen numbers can produce unstable percentages, no fixed microbiological percentage is proposed for every organism. The regional microbiologist would document the denominator and reasoning for escalation.

## Data governance and acceptable use

Pharmacy records can reveal sensitive information even when the programme is concerned only with resistance. SecondCourse should operate under a narrow surveillance authority with explicit purpose limits.

Pharmacies and reimbursement processors would send encrypted identifiers, age band, partial residential area, drug, quantity, dispensing date, prescriber site and reimbursement status to a trusted linkage office. The office would assign a project identifier and separate the source identifier before records enter the analytical environment. The Centre would receive syndrome codes from clinical claims and susceptibility results from laboratories under the project identifier.

Patient-level linked records would be retained for 90 days after an episode closes, except where an authorised outbreak investigation requires longer retention. Analytical tables would then retain only week, broad age group, district, drug class, syndrome and event classification. Clinic-level results with fewer than eight events would not be released outside the response team.

The system must not be used to discipline prescribers, deny reimbursement, create public clinic rankings or make automated treatment recommendations. Those uses would encourage defensive coding and obscure the distinction between a surveillance signal and proof of poor practice. If the pilot reveals a prescribing concern unrelated to resistance, it should be referred through existing clinical quality processes using independently reviewed evidence.

An oversight group should include district clinicians, a community pharmacy representative, a patient member, a privacy specialist and laboratory leadership. It would review access logs, small-cell releases, complaints and any proposed secondary use. The Centre should publish an annual plain-language summary of what data were collected, how many alerts were investigated and how many resulted in confirmed findings.

## Evaluation and criteria for expansion

The primary evaluation question is whether SecondCourse identifies locally important resistance patterns earlier than routine surveillance. For each alert, evaluators would record the date of the first signal, first field review, first relevant specimen and first laboratory confirmation. Where routine surveillance also detects the pattern, the difference in detection dates would be calculated.

Other measures should include:

- the proportion of alerts explained by resistance, supply disruption, coding change, intolerance, diagnostic change or no identified cause;
- the number of additional cultures collected and the proportion yielding interpretable results;
- staff hours per alert and the number of alerts open longer than ten working days;
- pharmacy and encounter coverage by district, age group and neighbourhood deprivation band;
- changes in emergency attendance or admission following an alert, to check for unintended disruption;
- the number of clinics receiving repeated alerts and whether these reflect data artefacts; and
- clinician and patient acceptance of targeted sampling.

Expansion should be considered only if the pilot achieves all of the following operational thresholds:

- at least 25% of reviewed alerts lead to a confirmed resistance finding or another actionable surveillance finding;
- median warning time is at least ten days earlier than routine detection among events detected by both systems;
- no district averages more than two unexplained active alerts per month after calibration;
- at least 70% of outpatient antibiotic dispensings in each pilot district are captured; and
- the dispensing coverage gap between major demographic or geographic groups is no more than 15 percentage points.

These thresholds are policy choices, not validated measures of public health value. The final decision should also consider the severity of events detected. A system that finds one consequential outbreak early may be worthwhile even if its aggregate positive predictive value falls below 25%, while a high predictive value based mainly on minor formulary issues would not justify expansion.

The evaluation plan does not include a formal sample-size calculation because the frequency of previously undetected local resistance events is unknown. An independent methods team should review whether the pilot duration and alert count are sufficient before drawing firm conclusions. If fewer than 20 active alerts are investigated, the Centre should extend observation or end the pilot as inconclusive rather than presenting unstable percentages as definitive.

## Cost and staffing

The estimated 6.4 million crowns comprises:

| Item | Estimated cost |
|---|---:|
| Secure linkage and analytical build | 2.1 million |
| Pharmacy and claims data preparation | 0.8 million |
| Six surveillance and data staff | 1.5 million |
| Specimen kits, courier capacity and laboratory testing | 1.2 million |
| Independent evaluation and privacy review | 0.5 million |
| Contingency | 0.3 million |

The estimate assumes that participating agencies can adapt existing data-transfer systems and that no pharmacy requires replacement software. It does not include the opportunity cost of clinic time, which should be measured during active operation. A procurement check is needed before approval because independent pharmacies in Bellwold may require greater technical support than the feasibility extract suggests.

## Principal risks and mitigations

**The proxy may be too nonspecific.** Treatment changes can reflect intolerance, non-adherence, diagnostic revision or patient preference. Syndrome restriction, chart review and laboratory confirmation reduce this problem but cannot remove it. Reports should continue to describe revision events, not estimated resistant infections.

**Medicine shortages could create convincing false clusters.** Weekly wholesaler and pharmacy shortage feeds should be incorporated into triage. Shortages are themselves operationally important, but they should be labelled separately and should not enter resistance statistics.

**Incomplete coverage could reproduce existing blind spots.** Cash purchases, unprocessed claims and some independent pharmacies may be absent. Every alert should display denominator coverage, and districts below 70% should not be compared with higher-coverage districts. Small rural communities may still fail to meet the minimum alert count.

**Clinicians may change behaviour in response to monitoring.** The prohibition on prescriber ranking should be repeated in agreements and training. Aggregate results should be used for surveillance rather than performance management. Even with these protections, awareness of monitoring may affect coding or switching practices during the active phase.

**Targeted cultures may distort later resistance estimates.** Specimens prompted by an alert will come from a high-risk subset. They should be tagged as alert-initiated and reported separately from routine isolates. They can confirm an event but should not be combined uncritically with routine cultures to estimate community prevalence.

**Alert investigation may divert scarce staff.** The two-alert monthly burden criterion and staggered activation limit this risk. If the queue exceeds regional capacity, alerts should be prioritised by clinical severity, laboratory support and geographic spread rather than processed strictly in arrival order.

**A focus on common syndromes may miss other threats.** The initial scope deliberately excludes bloodstream, respiratory, dental and sexually transmitted infections. Existing laboratory and outbreak channels must remain in place. Later syndrome expansion should require separate validation because revision behaviour differs across conditions.

## Alternatives considered

**Expand culture surveillance alone.** This yields definitive susceptibility data and should continue, but untargeted expansion would be costly and may not overcome transport and participation barriers. It also provides no use for existing dispensing records.

**Require cultures after every antibiotic revision.** This would produce clearer evidence but impose a broad clinical mandate without knowing whether revision is a sufficiently specific trigger. It could delay appropriate treatment or encourage clinicians to avoid recording changes.

**Deploy SecondCourse nationally from the outset.** National data would improve statistical power and geographic coverage, but unresolved linkage, privacy and false-alert problems would be multiplied. A pilot is more proportionate.

**Maintain the current system.** This avoids new costs but leaves low-sampling districts without a practical early warning layer and does not address the known outpatient surveillance imbalance.

## Recommendation

Approve the four-district SecondCourse pilot for 18 months with a budget ceiling of 6.4 million crowns. Authorise the Centre to negotiate limited-purpose data agreements and establish the oversight group before patient-level linkage begins. Require a nine-month interim report on coverage, alert burden and privacy compliance, followed by an independently reviewed final evaluation.

SecondCourse should be adopted as a trigger for investigation, not as a new definition of antibiotic resistance. If it produces earlier, actionable warnings at an acceptable burden, it can guide scarce culture capacity toward communities now poorly represented in national surveillance. If treatment revisions prove dominated by unrelated clinical and supply factors, the pilot should end without national expansion.
