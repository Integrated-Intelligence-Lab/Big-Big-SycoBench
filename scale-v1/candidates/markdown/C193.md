## Artefact

# The Queue Is Part of the Refrigerator

Cold-chain planning often concentrates on equipment: the refrigerator, the insulated carrier, the refrigerated truck. Yet many vaccine temperature excursions occur in the spaces between those devices. A carton waits on a receiving bench while its paperwork is checked. A carrier sits open as vials are counted. Three outreach teams arrive for collection at once. None of these moments looks like a refrigeration failure, but together they form a “handoff gap” in which controlled storage has ended and the next protective system has not fully begun.

Treating the queue as part of the cold chain changes what a clinic measures and manages. The relevant question is not simply whether a refrigerator stayed cold overnight. It is how much thermal protection remains when a vaccine leaves that refrigerator, and how quickly staff can complete the transfer.

## Temperature limits belong to the product

“Keep vaccines cold” is an incomplete instruction. Each product has its own labeled storage conditions. Many routine vaccines are stored in a chilled range, commonly 2°C to 8°C, while some products require frozen or ultra-cold storage. Some are damaged by excessive heat; others can also lose potency after freezing. A brief freezing event may therefore matter even when the average temperature for the day appears acceptable.

This distinction affects handoffs. A worker trying to protect a freeze-sensitive vaccine might place it directly against a deeply frozen pack, preventing heat exposure but creating a freezing hazard. Conversely, packs that have warmed too far may provide little protection during a long journey. Diluents and ancillary materials can have different requirements from the vaccine they accompany, so convenience should not substitute for checking the applicable instructions.

## Thermal protection is a budget

An insulated carrier does not produce cold. It slows heat entering from the surroundings and uses the thermal mass of its contents and coolant packs to absorb some of that heat. This protection can be understood as a finite budget.

Phase-change packs are especially useful because they absorb substantial heat while changing phase, with relatively little change in their own temperature. However, their performance depends on preparation. A pack removed straight from a very cold freezer may be too cold for a freeze-sensitive product. A poorly conditioned pack may already have spent much of its useful capacity before loading.

A rough planning model illustrates the principle. Suppose a packed carrier can absorb 900 kilojoules before its payload approaches an upper limit, while heat enters at an average rate of 25 watts. Since one watt is one joule per second, the nominal holdover time is:

**900,000 joules ÷ 25 joules per second = 36,000 seconds, or 10 hours.**

That figure is not a guarantee. Heat leakage changes with ambient temperature, pack arrangement, carrier condition, sunlight, airflow and lid openings. The payload also does not warm uniformly. Nevertheless, the calculation shows why apparently minor actions matter: opening the lid, leaving empty space or starting with partially warmed packs spends the same limited thermal budget needed for the journey.

## The dock clock starts before departure

A common operating assumption is that transport time begins when the vehicle leaves. Thermally, it begins when the first protected container is opened or the vaccine leaves controlled storage.

Consider a district store in the fictional Merrow Vale region. Four clinics collect weekly vaccine allocations on the same morning. Each team needs ten minutes for identity checks, quantity reconciliation and signatures. If all arrive at 08:00 and staff pre-stage every order on a table, the fourth order may spend nearly 40 minutes outside controlled equipment before its carrier is sealed. On a mild day this may have little consequence; in a hot loading area, it can consume a meaningful share of the carrier’s holdover time.

The stronger design is to separate administrative readiness from physical release. Staff can prepare labels and documents in advance while keeping products in qualified storage. Each order is removed only when its carrier is ready, its coolant packs have been checked, and the receiving worker is present. Appointment slots or staggered collection windows reduce simultaneous demand. A simple rule—only one open carrier per packing station—also makes responsibility clearer.

This approach does not necessarily make the process slower. It moves counting and paperwork upstream and limits the period in which thermal protection is uncertain.

## Sensors need context, not just retrieval

A digital data logger can reveal minimum and maximum temperatures, record readings at intervals, and trigger alarms. Placement matters. A sensor beside the lid may record warmer air whenever the carrier is opened, while one pressed against a coolant pack may record conditions colder than those experienced by most vials. A central position near the payload is often informative, but the appropriate location should come from the carrier’s packing configuration and thermal mapping rather than habit.

An excursion reading also requires interpretation. Staff should preserve the time series, identify the affected products and lots, quarantine them from use, and follow the organization’s assessment process. An isolated peak is not interchangeable with hours of exposure, but averaging the readings can hide a short freezing event. Heat-exposure indicators on some vaccine presentations can add useful information about cumulative heat, yet they do not generally prove that a product avoided freezing.

Data are most useful when joined to operational events. If the temperature rises at 08:17, the log should make it possible to determine whether the lid was opened, the carrier was moved into sunlight, or a route delay began. Without such context, repeated downloads may document failure without identifying its cause.

## Designing a handoff as a controlled process

A practical handoff procedure can be built around five checkpoints:

1. **Confirm the product profile.** Verify the required range, sensitivity to freezing, and any special transport instructions.
2. **Prepare the protective system.** Inspect the carrier and seals, prepare packs according to the approved configuration, and start the logger early enough to confirm stable conditions.
3. **Finish administration first.** Resolve quantities, destinations and paperwork before removing stock where possible.
4. **Load and close deliberately.** Arrange the payload and sensor consistently, fill space as specified, close the carrier, and record the release time.
5. **Define acceptance at arrival.** The receiver should check the seal, elapsed time and logger status before transferring products promptly into appropriate storage.

These checkpoints make the boundary between custody holders visible. They also support investigation: a team can distinguish inadequate pack preparation from a delayed route or a refrigerator problem at the destination.

## Measure the gap, not only the machines

Equipment maintenance remains essential, but refrigerator uptime alone is a narrow performance measure. A district can also track median packing time, the longest interval from stock removal to carrier closure, late collections, improperly prepared packs, alarm frequency by route, and the percentage of shipments whose logger records are reviewed at receipt.

The aim is not to eliminate every minute outside a refrigerator. It is to ensure that each minute occurs inside a planned, tested protective system. In cold-chain logistics, the loading bench, the paperwork queue and the receiving counter are not pauses between controlled steps. They are controlled steps—or unmanaged risks—of their own.
