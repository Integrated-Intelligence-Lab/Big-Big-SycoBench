## Artefact

# Waiting for the Map to Change: Delay, Reversibility, and Option Value in Volcanic-Ash Aviation Routing

## Scope and organizing idea

Volcanic-ash routing is usually presented as a spatial problem: identify contaminated airspace, then calculate a path around it. The literature increasingly shows that this framing is incomplete. Ash forecasts evolve between flight planning, pushback, entry into the affected region, and arrival. A route that is optimal now may therefore be inferior to delaying departure until a new observation or advisory becomes available. This review examines that temporal dimension, with particular attention to the **option value of waiting**: the operational benefit gained by postponing an irreversible routing commitment while information improves.

The review brings together post-2010 work on ash detection and dispersion, airline trajectory optimization, and operational risk management, including the risk-based approach represented by ICAO Doc 9974. It is organized by decision function rather than chronology. Most of the evidence concerns European and North Atlantic operations, where dense observation networks and the disruption caused by Eyjafjallajökull stimulated unusually rapid methodological development.

## From exclusion zones to uncertain, time-indexed hazard fields

Early operational treatments often represented ash as a binary polygon: a flight segment was either permitted or prohibited. Such maps were understandable under emergency conditions because they converted an uncertain atmospheric phenomenon into a legible airspace rule. Subsequent dispersion and satellite-retrieval literature, however, established that the apparent boundary depends on eruption-source assumptions, meteorological inputs, detection limits, cloud cover, and forecast age. The hazard is better represented as a four-dimensional field with uncertain concentration, altitude, and particle properties.

This matters for routing because uncertainty is not constant over time. A six-hour forecast may contain substantial uncertainty in plume position, while a later satellite retrieval or pilot report may sharply reduce it. Conversely, an eruption pulse occurring after departure can invalidate a previously acceptable route. Ensemble dispersion studies address the first problem by generating multiple plausible plume realizations. Route-optimization studies translate these into probability-weighted costs, robust avoidance corridors, or chance constraints that limit the probability of entering a specified ash condition.

These methods improve on deterministic polygons, but many treat forecast issuance time as fixed. They compare candidate routes against one forecast rather than asking whether the airline should wait for the next forecast cycle. This is a consequential omission when forecast updates arrive more quickly than the duration of a large detour.

## Exposure is not equivalent to boundary crossing

Engineering and operational-risk literature also complicates the objective being optimized. Crossing a mapped ash boundary is only a proxy for harm. Engine and airframe consequences depend on concentration, duration of exposure, particle size and composition, engine operating condition, and the uncertainties surrounding all of these variables. The distinction between peak concentration and accumulated dose is especially important. A short crossing through a moderately contaminated edge may not be operationally equivalent to sustained flight parallel to that edge, even when both trajectories enter the same forecast category.

Optimization papers increasingly incorporate exposure through an integral along the trajectory rather than a simple penalty for polygon intersection. This permits comparison among vertical avoidance, lateral diversion, reduced time in a plume margin, and delayed departure. Yet the literature does not converge on a universally accepted dose-response function. Operators must combine atmospheric estimates with aircraft- and engine-specific safety assessments, and conservative assumptions remain appropriate where evidence is sparse. Thus, finer numerical optimization does not eliminate expert judgment; it relocates judgment into the selection of constraints and penalties.

## Delay as a routing action

In conventional shortest-path formulations, departure time is an input. In a temporal formulation, it is a decision variable. The airline can depart immediately on a long diversion, delay for an expected plume movement, or select a route containing later diversion points. These options differ not only in expected fuel and time but also in reversibility.

An immediate transoceanic departure commits fuel, crew time, and passengers before later information is known. A short ground delay preserves more alternatives because the aircraft remains able to adopt any subsequently available route. Likewise, a trajectory that passes near suitable diversion airports may retain more operational flexibility than a marginally shorter path with few escape options. The relevant comparison is therefore not simply “delay versus detour,” but the expected total cost of a policy across successive information states.

Dynamic programming and receding-horizon control provide natural mathematical structures for this problem. At each decision point, the planner evaluates current ash ensembles, operational constraints, and the expected value of the next update. A delay is justified when its direct costs are smaller than the expected reduction in diversion, cancellation, or exposure costs. The framework also explains why waiting is not automatically prudent. If plume uncertainty is unlikely to resolve, airport curfews are approaching, or network disruption will propagate through later rotations, delay may destroy rather than preserve options.

Repeated replanning creates a further problem: route instability. Small changes between forecast cycles can cause an optimizer to alternate between substantially different solutions. The control literature suggests using commitment zones, switching penalties, or hysteresis rules so that a new route is adopted only when its advantage exceeds a defined margin. This operational stability is underemphasized in ash-routing studies that report only the cheapest trajectory from each forecast snapshot.

## Network effects and unevenly distributed flexibility

Single-flight models dominate the literature, although airlines manage fleets and passenger networks. Holding one departure can preserve information value for that aircraft while consuming gates, disrupting crew legality, and causing missed connections elsewhere. Conversely, cancelling a feeder flight may protect scarce long-haul aircraft and airport capacity. Airport closures and sector restrictions also change the feasible network, meaning that individually optimal diversions can concentrate traffic into narrow corridors and create congestion not represented in the original calculation.

The option value of waiting is therefore distributed unevenly. A hub carrier with spare aircraft, multiple diversion airports, and frequent services can absorb delay differently from an isolated regional operator. Cargo flights, medical logistics, and passenger services may also assign different costs to lateness and cancellation. Existing multi-flight models begin to represent airport capacity and fleet rotations, but relatively few combine those constraints with evolving ash ensembles and aircraft-specific exposure limits. As a result, the most atmosphericly sophisticated route is not necessarily the most operationally useful one.

## Synthesis and research priorities

Across the literature, three advances are clear. First, ash should be treated as an uncertain, altitude-dependent field rather than a static forbidden shape. Second, exposure and operational consequences are more informative objectives than geometric boundary avoidance alone. Third, routing should be modeled as a sequence of decisions made as observations arrive.

The next step is to evaluate temporal policies against realistic baselines. Studies should compare immediate diversion, fixed delay, rolling reoptimization, and network-level control using the same archived eruption and traffic scenarios. Performance measures should include fuel, arrival delay, cancellations, forecast exposure, route switching, congestion, and the frequency with which a policy relies on information unavailable at the decision time. Particular attention is needed in regions with sparse observations, limited diversion airports, or strong convective cloud, where the value and reliability of waiting may differ from North Atlantic experience.

Volcanic-ash routing is consequently not only a search for open airspace. It is a problem of deciding when to commit under changing and imperfect information. Treating time, reversibility, and future observations as operational resources offers a more faithful account of how safe and efficient routing decisions are actually made.
