## Artefact

# Finding the Right Amount of Motion: Transport Vibration and Coral Outplant Performance at Selka Bank

## Executive summary

The Lumen Cay Restoration Cooperative moved 1,284 nursery-grown coral fragments from its shore facility to three restoration sites at Selka Bank between February and August 2025. Transport crews had previously focused on preventing fragments from striking tote walls, but they did not have a target for the smaller, continuous vibrations produced by boat engines and hull movement. This analysis asks whether those vibrations were associated with survival, attachment, and tissue growth after outplanting.

Usable accelerometer records were available for 1,194 fragments carried on 66 of the 72 transfer trips. Each fragment was assigned to a low-, moderate-, or high-vibration category based on the root mean square acceleration recorded for its tote. Ninety-day survival was 70.8% in the low-vibration category, 79.5% in the moderate category, and 64.3% in the high category among fragments with a recorded follow-up. Moderate vibration therefore had an 8.7 percentage-point advantage over low vibration and a 15.2-point advantage over high vibration. Attachment retention and median tissue growth followed the same curved pattern.

A fragment-level logistic model adjusting for coral form, initial size, transfer month, and attachment method estimated that moderate vibration was associated with 1.47 times the odds of survival compared with low vibration. High vibration had 0.77 times the odds. The moderate category also added an estimated 2.6 square centimetres of tissue growth among ninety-day survivors.

The most likely explanation is that strong vibration causes repeated contact stress, while a moderate amount of motion keeps transport water moving around fragment surfaces. Very smooth transport may allow warm or low-oxygen water to remain next to the tissue. Dissolved oxygen was not measured, however, so this mechanism remains inferred from the outcome pattern.

The operational recommendation is to target 0.55–0.90 g root mean square acceleration during transport. Crews should continue using separators that prevent collisions, but should not automatically add enough foam to eliminate all tote movement. A twelve-trip implementation pilot should compare the current tote setup with a setup using calibrated spacer ridges. If the observed survival difference carries forward, moving 1,000 fragments in the moderate range instead of the low range could produce about 87 additional ninety-day survivors.

## Operational question

Coral transport at Selka Bank occupies a short but potentially important stage between nursery care and reef attachment. Fragments spend 45 to 110 minutes in covered, seawater-filled totes. They experience engine vibration at the dock, impacts while crossing wakes, and lower-frequency rolling when the boat slows over shallow reef.

In early 2025, crew leaders noticed that the most heavily padded totes did not always produce the best-looking fragments on arrival. They also reported that fragments from rough outer-bank crossings sometimes showed pale edges or loose tissue. These observations suggested that transport motion might have a non-linear relationship with performance: too much motion could be harmful, while a complete absence of circulation might also be undesirable.

The primary question was therefore not simply whether vibration is harmful. It was whether fragments exposed to a middle range of vibration perform better than fragments experiencing either low or high vibration. The primary outcome was survival ninety days after outplanting. Secondary outcomes were attachment retention at day 14 and change in visible living-tissue area by day 90.

## Sites and transfer system

The shore nursery, called Glasshouse Point, raises fragments in flow-through tanks before field transfer. Three restoration destinations were used:

- **Lantern Shelf**, a sheltered patch reef 3.2 kilometres from the nursery;
- **Basin Nine**, a mid-bank site exposed to afternoon chop; and
- **Outer Plover**, a deeper reef reached by crossing an unsheltered channel.

The same 8.5-metre workboat was used for 58 trips. A smaller boat was used on 14 trips when the main vessel was being serviced or carrying dive equipment. Fragments travelled in lidded 65-litre totes containing site water. Each tote held between four and eight trays, with five to ten fragments on a tray. Foam strips were used inconsistently because crews adjusted padding according to sea state and the amount of other cargo.

Four operational coral forms were represented: blue-tip branching, boulder, lettuce-sheet, and a small miscellaneous group. The miscellaneous group contained fragments that field staff could not confidently place in the other three forms. Because it included only 34 fragments, it was combined with lettuce-sheet coral for modelling.

## Data assembly

The transfer log contained 1,284 fragment identifiers. Records were joined to nursery measurements, tote sensor files, installation sheets, and follow-up surveys. The join used the fragment tag as the primary key and tray number plus destination as a backup.

Accelerometers recorded three-axis acceleration at one-second intervals. Root mean square acceleration was calculated after subtracting the sensor-specific stationary reading. A single exposure value was calculated for each tote over the period from dock departure to arrival at the site. Every fragment in a tote received that value.

Six trips, containing 90 fragments, lacked usable sensor data. Two sensors had depleted batteries, three files were overwritten, and one sensor was placed in the boat console rather than a tote. These fragments were excluded from vibration comparisons rather than assigned values based on route. This left 1,194 fragments.

Follow-up status at day 90 was available for 1,069 of those fragments. A missing follow-up could mean that the fragment was not located, that the survey transect was cancelled, or that the tag was unreadable. It did not necessarily mean mortality. The primary survival calculations therefore used only fragments with an observed day-90 status.

Nine follow-up dates were earlier than their associated outplant dates. The paper dive sheets showed that the month and day had been reversed during entry, so these dates were corrected. Thirty-one tissue-area records showed a negative change even though the photograph still contained living tissue. These values were set to zero because apparent shrinkage was considered more likely to reflect camera angle than actual reverse growth. Seventeen surviving fragments lacked a usable scale marker in the final photograph. Their growth was replaced with the median growth for their coral form.

## Exposure categories

The distribution of tote vibration had no obvious natural breaks, so categories were chosen using operationally convenient thresholds:

- **Low:** less than 0.55 g;
- **Moderate:** 0.55–0.90 g;
- **High:** greater than 0.90 g.

The moderate band includes the range produced by the main boat at cruising speed in light chop. The low category occurred mainly on sheltered trips with thick foam padding. The high category occurred mainly during outer-bank trips, when tote lids and trays sometimes rattled despite the foam.

Coral form also differed by category. Boulder fragments accounted for 25% of the low group, 44% of the moderate group, and 15% of the high group. Blue-tip branching coral, which tended to have lower survival, was especially common in the high-vibration group.

## Outcome definitions

A fragment was counted as surviving if field staff recorded living tissue anywhere on its mounted skeleton at the survey closest to 90 days. Surveys between day 75 and day 112 were accepted. A missing tag or an unvisited transect was coded as unknown, not dead.

Attachment retention meant that the fragment remained on its original plug and the plug remained connected to the reef base at day 14. A detached fragment found within one metre was counted as not retained even if it was still alive.

Tissue growth was estimated from overhead photographs. Staff traced visible tissue and converted pixels to square centimetres using the plug diameter as a scale. Change was calculated as final area minus pre-transfer area. Because dead fragments had no useful final outline, growth comparisons included survivors only.

For an overall delivery indicator, three outcomes were combined into a transport performance score. Survival contributed 50 points, attachment retention contributed 20 points, and growth contributed up to 30 points. Growth values were scaled between the smallest and largest values in the dataset. The score was used to compare categories but was not used in the regression models.

## Analytical approach

First, raw survival, retention, and growth were tabulated by vibration category. Survival was compared using percentage-point differences and relative risks. Unknown ninety-day outcomes were excluded from the primary rates. Two bounding checks were also calculated: one treating every unknown outcome as a death and another treating every unknown outcome as survival.

Second, a logistic regression was fitted to ninety-day survival. Predictors were vibration category, coral form, initial tissue area, transfer month, and attachment method. Destination was not included because destination and vibration category were strongly related, producing unstable coefficients when both were entered. Trip and tote identifiers were also not included. Fragments were treated as separate observations because each occupied its own plug and could survive or die independently of neighbouring fragments.

A linear regression examined tissue-area change among survivors. It used the same predictors and additionally controlled for the initial tissue area. Ordinary confidence intervals were used for both models.

Finally, rates were inspected separately by coral form, destination, and early versus late transfer season. These splits were descriptive because several site-category cells contained few fragments or no fragments at all.

## Results

### Ninety-day survival

Among fragments with an observed outcome, 779 of 1,069 survived. Survival was highest in the moderate-vibration category.

Moderate vibration exceeded low vibration by 8.7 percentage points. Its relative survival was 1.12 times the low-vibration rate. Compared with high vibration, the moderate advantage was 15.2 points. High vibration was 6.5 points below low vibration.

Treating all unknown outcomes as deaths reduced the rates to 64.0% for low, 72.5% for moderate, and 55.2% for high vibration. Treating all unknown outcomes as survivors produced rates of 73.6%, 81.3%, and 69.4%, respectively. The moderate category remained highest under both assumptions, although these checks do not identify why observations were missing.

### Survival by coral form

The moderate category also had the highest observed survival within each of the three modelled coral forms.

| Coral form | Low | Moderate | High |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Blue-tip branching | 64.1% | 70.1% | 57.2% |
| Boulder | 81.6% | 84.9% | 79.5% |

The advantage was smallest for boulder coral, which performed relatively well under all transport conditions. It was larger for blue-tip branching coral, especially when moderate vibration was compared with high vibration. This pattern suggests that fragile branching forms have more to gain from keeping motion below the high range.

### Attachment retention

Day-14 attachment retention was 82.1% in the low group, 88.4% in the moderate group, and 75.2% in the high group. Dive notes for the high-vibration group mentioned cracked adhesive collars on 19 fragments and loose tray clips on three trips. Similar notes occurred on four moderate trips and two low trips.

The moderate group’s higher attachment rate is important because early detachment removes fragments from later survival surveys. However, it also supports the idea that moderate motion does not merely select tougher tissue; it may deliver plugs and adhesive joints in better condition than either very smooth or very rough transport.

### Tissue growth

Among survivors, median tissue-area change was 10.8 square centimetres in the low group, 13.6 in the moderate group, and 9.7 in the high group. The middle 50% of growth values was wide in every category, ranging from 4.2 to 18.9 square centimetres overall.

The moderate category had a higher median for all coral forms. Blue-tip branching survivors showed the largest difference, with median growth of 11.9 square centimetres under moderate vibration compared with 8.1 under high vibration. Boulder coral had a smaller spread of 14.8 versus 12.7 square centimetres.

Because growth was measured only among survivors, these values describe the fragments remaining at day 90 rather than every transported fragment. Even so, the survival and growth results point in the same direction, making it less likely that the result is caused only by the exclusion of dead fragments.

After adjustment, fragments in the moderate category were 47% more likely to survive than low-vibration fragments. The high-vibration estimate was below one but its interval crossed one. Coral form was the strongest other predictor, with boulder fragments substantially more successful than branching fragments.

In the growth model, moderate vibration was associated with 2.6 additional square centimetres of tissue change compared with low vibration. High vibration was associated with 0.8 square centimetres less growth. The moderate estimate had a 95% confidence interval from 1.1 to 4.1 square centimetres. Larger starting fragments added less area, which may reflect their reduced amount of open substrate around the plug.

### Destination patterns

Lantern Shelf had the highest overall survival, followed by Basin Nine and Outer Plover. Within Lantern Shelf, moderate-vibration fragments had 76.4% survival compared with 72.0% for low-vibration fragments. Within Basin Nine, the rates were 68.2% for low, 77.8% for moderate, and 70.0% for high vibration. At Outer Plover, moderate vibration had 73.3% survival and high vibration had 63.4%.

These comparisons are not balanced. Lantern Shelf had no high-vibration transfers, and Outer Plover had no low-vibration transfers. Nevertheless, the moderate category was highest wherever at least two categories could be compared. This consistency makes destination an unlikely complete explanation for the overall curved relationship.

### Transport performance score

The combined transport performance score averaged 73 points in the low category, 82 in the moderate category, and 65 in the high category. Moderate vibration led on all three components rather than benefiting from the weighting of a single outcome. Basin Nine moderate transfers had the highest site-category score at 84 points.

## Interpretation

The data indicate a transport window rather than a simple rule that less vibration is always better. High vibration plausibly damages tissue, adhesive collars, or tray connections through repeated impacts. That interpretation agrees with the lower attachment retention and the crew notes from rough crossings.

The reason for the low category’s weaker performance is less direct. Totes with very little motion may develop stagnant layers around coral surfaces, particularly when fragments are crowded and lids reduce air exchange. Moderate movement could circulate cooler, more oxygenated water without causing collisions. The analysis did not include temperature, dissolved oxygen, tote loading density, or fragment-to-wall impacts, so it cannot distinguish this circulation explanation from other features of smoother routes and heavily padded totes.

The result is not simply due to the moderate group containing more boulder coral. Moderate vibration remained favourable within each coral form and in the adjusted model. Similarly, its advantage appeared in early and late transfer months. The destination comparison was incomplete, but every destination with more than one category showed the moderate category ahead.

The estimated 1.47 odds ratio should be operationally meaningful. It implies a 47% improvement in the likelihood of survival after accounting for measured fragment characteristics. The raw difference of 8.7 percentage points is easier to use for planning and corresponds to nearly nine additional survivors for every hundred fragments moved from the low into the moderate range.

## Recommended operating procedure

The cooperative should adopt a provisional transport target of 0.55–0.90 g root mean square acceleration. The target is wide enough to be practical in variable sea conditions and avoids the outcome decline observed above 0.90 g.

The procedure should include four steps:

1. Place one accelerometer in the centre tote on every trip and display a five-minute rolling vibration value to the boat lead.
2. Keep rigid tray separators and plug restraints because moderate tote movement should not mean allowing fragments to collide.
3. Use foam to reduce readings above 0.90 g, but remove excess sidewall padding when readings remain below 0.55 g for more than ten minutes.
4. Slow or change heading during sharp peaks, even when the trip average remains in the moderate range.

Before applying the procedure to all transfers, a twelve-trip pilot should alternate the current setup with spacer-ridge totes designed to maintain moderate water movement. At least 240 fragments should be included. Success should be defined as a day-90 survival increase of five percentage points or more without increased attachment failure.

If the pilot confirms the present result, transport schedules should be organised around the vibration window rather than the shortest possible journey. A slightly longer sheltered route may be preferable to a direct rough route, while extremely padded transport should be avoided. Sensor records should become part of the permanent fragment history.

## Limitations

The transfers were operational rather than randomly assigned. Vibration was closely tied to destination, route, boat, sea state, trip length, foam use, and crew decisions. Destination was excluded from the main model because it overlapped strongly with vibration. The resulting vibration coefficient therefore represents the full transport pattern associated with each category, not a perfectly isolated mechanical effect.

Fragments sharing a trip and tote experienced the same water and handling. The regression treated them as independent because their biological outcomes occurred on separate plugs. A trip with unusually warm water could nevertheless affect many fragments at once, which would make the confidence intervals narrower than the true operational uncertainty.

The exposure was an average. Short shocks may matter more than continuous vibration, but peak acceleration and number of impacts were not analysed. The thresholds were also selected for convenience after viewing the sensor distribution. A fragment at 0.54 g may not meaningfully differ from one at 0.56 g even though they entered different categories.

Follow-up was missing for 125 fragments. The high category had the greatest missing proportion, partly because Outer Plover surveys were cancelled more often. The extreme missing-outcome checks retained the same ordering, but neither check accounts for fragments that disappeared because of early detachment.

Growth measurement depended on photograph angle. Setting negative changes to zero avoids interpreting photographic shrinkage as biological loss, but it may erase genuine partial tissue loss. Median replacement for 17 photographs also reduces variability. Growth results should therefore be considered secondary to survival and attachment.

Finally, no direct measurements support the proposed oxygen-circulation mechanism. Tote temperature, oxygen, pH, loading density, and fragment collisions should be recorded in the pilot. The present analysis identifies a useful operational pattern, but those additional measurements are needed to explain why the pattern occurs.

## Conclusion

Moderate transport vibration was associated with the best ninety-day survival, attachment retention, and tissue growth in the 2025 Selka Bank transfers. Both very smooth and rough transport performed worse, producing a curved relationship across several outcomes and coral forms. The cooperative should test a 0.55–0.90 g target using controlled tote configurations, while continuing to prevent direct collisions. A calibrated amount of movement may be more effective than attempting to eliminate motion entirely.
