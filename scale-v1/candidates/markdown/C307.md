## Artefact

# The Island with No One on It

On Nera Vale’s screen, the Orison Islands breathed.

Every morning the circles brightened and dimmed as the model drew people from bedrooms to ferries, from ferries to workshops, from workshops to kitchens. Infection travelled with them as a red haze, faint at first, then darkening wherever Kestrel fever found enough susceptible lungs.

All except Cinder Cay. Cinder was a black dot between the two largest islands, its permanent population recorded as zero. It had an automated lighthouse, a disused customs house and no circle in Nera’s model. Nothing breathed there.

At 08:17, a message arrived from the regional laboratory.

**Three positive swabs. Collection site: Cinder Cay.**

Nera read it twice before calling the lab.

“Labels get switched,” said Tovan Rusk, the outbreak coordinator, when she told him. He was already chairing the morning briefing, his voice squeezed through her laptop speaker. “Especially with the new courier.”

“The tube numbers match the manifest.”

“Then someone entered the wrong site.”

“The manifest was signed on Cinder.”

A pause followed. Behind Tovan, the briefing room’s projection showed Nera’s seven-day forecast: forty-one new infections, most on Marrow Island, none on Cinder.

“Cinder has no population,” he said.

Nera looked at the black dot. “The virus disagrees.”

Her model was a metapopulation system: one set of susceptible, exposed, infectious and recovered compartments for each island, joined by ferry-ticket data. It allowed for unreported cases, uncertain generation intervals and the fact that people often became infectious before symptoms. During its first month, it had predicted hospital admissions within tolerable bounds. Tovan had called it their second-best instrument after the thermometer.

Now three swabs had made it ridiculous.

By noon, Nera was on a maintenance launch with Seli Anwar, the laboratory courier. Rain clicked against the cabin windows. Cinder Cay appeared as a low grey tooth, then vanished behind spray, then appeared again.

“I collected them myself,” Seli said. “One from a woman in an orange coat. Two men. They’d been coughing all night.”

“What were they doing there?”

“Waiting.”

“For what?”

Seli shrugged. “For the weather to become something else.”

The launch could not use Cinder’s broken jetty, so they went ashore in a rubber boat. Inside the customs house, six iron bunks lined the walls. A kettle sat on a camping stove. Someone had hung wet socks from an old brass scale. On the table was a paper tide chart marked with names, arrows and small additions in pencil.

Nera counted twenty-three names from the previous nine days.

“The ferry records show no passengers landing here,” she said.

“They didn’t land,” Seli replied. “Not officially.”

Cinder lay where three shipping channels narrowed around a reef. Mussel boats waited there when the tide ran against them. Maintenance crews slept in the customs house if fog closed the northern harbour. Ferry captains sometimes put ashore passengers during engine checks, then collected them on the return passage. None of these movements appeared in the ticket data because nobody bought a ticket to Cinder.

Nera photographed the tide chart. One pencilled name, Iven Vale, tightened something beneath her ribs.

Her father had maintained the lighthouse before it was automated. He had died six years ago, but she remembered him describing Cinder as a waiting room with the sea for a clock. When she built the model, she had entered its population as zero without thinking to ask what zero meant.

Back at the office, she laid the tide chart beside her keyboard.

The easy repair would have been to add twenty-three people to Cinder. It would also have been wrong. They were not residents. They arrived in bursts, mixed intensely in one room, and dispersed to several islands before symptoms appeared. Cinder was less a population than a lung: emptying, filling, emptying again.

Nera replaced the black dot with a transient node. Instead of assigning it a fixed population, she estimated occupancy-hours from tide height, wind direction and maintenance schedules. She gave each arrival a home island and allowed infections acquired in the customs house to emerge elsewhere after a sampled incubation period.

At two in the morning, the revised model found a chain the old one had hidden. A mechanic from Marrow had probably reached Cinder infectious but without symptoms. The orange-coated woman had returned to Pelican Island two days later. One of the men had joined a survey vessel bound for Glass Harbour.

There were other plausible chains. The data could not identify a single patient as the source, and Nera refused the temptation to make it do so. But across most runs, Cinder changed the same thing: Glass Harbour’s risk was not low. It was merely delayed.

She checked the old model’s fit. It had absorbed the missing movements by stretching the reporting delay and raising the estimated transmission rate on Marrow. Its forecasts had looked accurate because two errors had leaned against each other like tired sailors.

At the morning briefing, Tovan frowned at the wider bands around Nera’s new forecast.

“Yesterday you gave me forty-one cases,” he said. “Today you’re giving me thirty to seventy-eight.”

“Yesterday I was more certain than the evidence allowed.”

“And now you want treatment packs placed on an island with no residents.”

“I want a small cache where infected people actually gather. I also want swabs on the maintenance launches and a shelter register that records arrival and departure times.”

Tovan turned toward the projection. The islands still brightened and dimmed, but Cinder now flickered between them. At certain tides it became the most connected place in the archipelago.

“How did we miss this?” he asked.

Nera could have said that the ferry authority collected journeys, not interruptions; that census tables counted beds owned, not bunks borrowed; that every dataset was an answer to somebody else’s question. Instead she said, “We treated nobody as if nobody was there.”

By afternoon, a launch carried twelve treatment packs, a box of swabs and a battery-powered radio to the customs house. The first person to use the radio was not ill. She was a deckhand from Glass Harbour, stranded with four others when fog folded over the reef.

Nera watched their arrival through the lighthouse maintenance feed. One by one they crossed the threshold and disappeared from the camera’s view.

On her other screen, Cinder’s circle brightened.

It did not mean the model knew their names. It did not mean anyone was infected. It meant only that, for the first time, their presence had somewhere to go.
