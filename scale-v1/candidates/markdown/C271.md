## Artefact

# The Mishearing Cabinet: Using Smart-Speaker Corrections to Preserve Kelmiri

## Project summary

Kelmiri is a minority language spoken in Elset Quay and three nearby villages in the Brackwater Reach. The Kelmiri Cultural Room estimates that about 430 people can hold a conversation in the language, but fewer than 50 speakers are under 25. Existing preservation work has concentrated on recording elders telling stories. These recordings are valuable, yet young residents rarely listen to files longer than a few minutes.

This six-month project will investigate a different source of language material: the phrases people repeat when a smart speaker misunderstands them. Eighteen households will receive a tabletop speaker running a basic Kelmiri command system. When the system fails, users will be encouraged to correct it in Kelmiri. The resulting exchanges will form a community-owned “Mishearing Cabinet” containing commands, corrections, pronunciation variants, and short explanations. The project assumes that correcting a machine is entertaining and may therefore encourage younger household members to speak more Kelmiri.

## Research question and objectives

The main question is: **Can failed interactions with a household voice device generate useful Kelmiri language records and increase everyday use of the language?**

The objectives are to:

1. collect at least 600 short Kelmiri speech clips;
2. identify words and grammatical forms that cause repeated recognition failures;
3. produce a searchable archive and a printed “dictionary of mishearings”;
4. increase the number of Kelmiri phrases spoken by participating households; and
5. demonstrate that imperfect technology can support language preservation better than passive recording.

Success will be defined as collecting 600 clips and observing a 25% increase in the number of distinct Kelmiri words used with the device between the first and final weeks.

## Rationale

Archives usually favour polished speech: prepared stories, ceremonial songs, or carefully pronounced word lists. Repair speech is different. A person who has been misunderstood may slow down, substitute a synonym, divide a word into parts, or explain its meaning. These small moments could reveal variation that a conventional interview misses. They may also capture vocabulary associated with ordinary domestic routines, such as closing shutters, finding keys, reheating soup, or reminding a child to bring boots.

The project focuses on smart-speaker interactions because machines are neutral conversation partners. A teenager embarrassed to make a mistake in front of an elder may be willing to repeat a phrase to a device. Once a speaker successfully recognises several Kelmiri commands, families will probably continue using the language in other situations. This effect is expected even though the project will not examine conversations that occur away from the device.

## Participants and recruitment

Eighteen households will be recruited through notices at the Kelmiri Cultural Room. Six will include an elder fluent speaker, six will include at least one learner aged 12–20, and six will be any other interested households. These categories may overlap if recruitment is slow. Participants will receive the device for free and keep it after the study, which should help retention.

The sample size was chosen because 18 devices can be purchased within the project budget. A comparison group will not be used, since households without devices would receive no immediate benefit. Although volunteers may already be unusually enthusiastic about Kelmiri, their results should indicate what could happen across Elset Quay if more devices were distributed.

## Method

During a two-week baseline period, each household will receive a paper tally sheet and record every occasion on which someone speaks Kelmiri at home. No audio will be collected during this stage because installing the devices earlier might alter baseline behaviour.

The speakers will then operate for eight weeks. They will recognise 40 commands, including “set a timer,” “play the local weather bulletin,” “add to the shopping list,” and “tell me today’s Kelmiri word.” Users may also press a blue correction button whenever the device responds incorrectly. Pressing it will save the previous 20 seconds of audio and the next correction. Household members will write the intended meaning on a companion card or select it from a menu.

Every two weeks, a project assistant will copy the clips to an encrypted laptop. Two fluent speakers will label the words used, broad pronunciation features, speaker age group, and type of repair. Disagreements will be settled by the project coordinator. The assistant will also add frequently corrected words to the recognition system. It is expected that 600 recordings will provide enough examples to improve recognition substantially, although no preliminary technical test has been conducted.

In the final week, households will repeat the paper tally exercise. They will also complete a five-question survey rating whether the device was enjoyable, accurate, and likely to make them speak Kelmiri more often. Three households judged especially active will be invited to a group discussion.

## Analysis

The project will compare the baseline and final tally sheets, calculating the percentage change in reported Kelmiri use. It will also count unique Kelmiri words addressed to the speaker. A rise in either measure will be treated as evidence of improved language vitality.

Recordings will be grouped into four repair types: repetition, slower pronunciation, substitution, and explanation. The most common forms will be described with examples. Results from households with young participants will be compared with results from other households, but statistical testing is not planned because the patterns should be visible in a table.

Device accuracy will be calculated in the first and final weeks. If accuracy improves while language use also rises, this will suggest that the archive and recognition system reinforce each other.

## Community archive and outputs

Participants will review their own clips at a Saturday “sorting table” before publication. They may remove any recording or mark it for access only inside the Cultural Room. Approved material will be stored on two hard drives owned by the Cultural Room rather than uploaded to a commercial service.

The main public output will be the *Kelmiri Dictionary of Useful Mishearings*. Each entry will include the intended phrase, common variants, what the device thought it heard, and a short usage note. A set of 30 audio buttons will also be installed in a wooden cabinet at the Cultural Room. Pressing one button will play an error followed by the speaker’s correction.

## Ethics and data management

The household lead will sign a consent form on behalf of the home. Other residents will receive an information sheet, and a sticker beside the device will warn visitors that pressing the blue button saves audio. Names will be replaced with household numbers. Because the buffer may capture background conversation, the assistant will delete obviously private material while transferring files. Participants can request deletion until the printed dictionary is completed.

Children may contribute recordings if the household lead agrees. Their ages will be stored only as ranges. The Cultural Room will decide whether future researchers may access restricted clips.

## Schedule and budget

Months one and two will cover recruitment, command translation, and device setup. Data collection will take place in months three and four. Month five will be used for labelling and software improvement, and month six for analysis, the sorting table, and printing.

The requested budget is 9,800 Brackwater crowns: 3,600 for devices, 2,400 for a half-time project assistant, 1,000 for fluent-speaker honoraria, 1,200 for printing and cabinet construction, and 600 for storage drives. The remaining 1,000 will cover refreshments, batteries, and unexpected software needs.

## Expected significance

The project will preserve forms of Kelmiri that are usually edited out of formal archives, including hesitation, rephrasing, and household vocabulary. It will also give families a practical reason to speak the language each day. If the number of distinct words rises, the approach could be expanded to every household in the Brackwater Reach and adapted for other minority languages without major changes.
