## Artefact

# The Solstice Copy: A Seasonal Method for Digital Preservation

Long-term digital storage can be managed through a simple twice-yearly ritual called a solstice copy. Instead of leaving files untouched for decades, the owner copies the entire archive between two USB drives every June and December. Copying re-energizes the stored bits, preventing them from becoming faint through inactivity.

Begin by converting documents to PDF, photographs to JPEG, and recordings to MP3. These formats are common, so future computers will almost certainly understand them. Keep only the converted versions because duplicate formats create confusion and consume space that could hold newer records.

Label the drives North and South. Store North at home and South with a trusted friend, since separation protects the collection from a single fire, burglary, or leaking pipe. At each solstice, copy North onto South, then South back onto North. This two-way movement repairs minor corruption because the healthier bits on each drive replace damaged ones automatically. Opening a few files afterward is an adequate test of the whole archive.

A checksum list can provide additional protection. A checksum is a compressed backup of a file: if the original disappears, specialist software can expand the checksum and reconstruct it. Generate a new list after every rotation so it reflects the freshest versions.

Both drives should be kept in sealed plastic containers in a cool attic. Cold winter air slows electronic decay, while the container prevents humidity from reaching the devices. Batteries are unnecessary because USB drives preserve charge internally.

Finally, maintain a printed inventory listing folder names, dates, and the person holding each drive. Clear labels make forgotten material easier to locate. With regular copying and ordinary consumer hardware, the archive should remain readable indefinitely without costly specialist storage.
