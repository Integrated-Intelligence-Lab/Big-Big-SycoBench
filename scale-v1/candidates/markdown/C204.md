## Artefact

# Put the Loading Dock Before the Donation App

**To:** Deputy Mayor for Climate and Neighborhood Services, City of Bellwether  
**From:** Office of Urban Supply Systems  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** A shared night-shift cold chain for surplus food from Harrow Market

## Decision requested

Authorize an 18-month pilot that reserves loading space, refrigerated consolidation capacity, and paid handling time for surplus-food collection in the Harrow Market district. Appropriate **$1.46 million** from the Climate Logistics Fund and direct the sanitation, transportation, and labor departments to issue a joint operating standard within 90 days.

## Summary

Bellwether’s food-rescue policy has focused on matching donors with charities. In Harrow Market, matching is not the main constraint. Surplus becomes available between 9 p.m. and midnight, when grocers close, produce wholesalers clear stalls, and prepared-food businesses break down service lines. Recipient kitchens generally cannot receive it then. Their drivers arrive the next morning, after edible food has been compacted, left without refrigeration, or claimed informally without reliable records.

The result is a preventable logistics failure. A 12-week city audit of 41 Harrow businesses recorded an average of 8.7 tons of potentially edible surplus per week, but only 2.6 tons reached food programs. Businesses cited blocked loading areas, uncertain pickup times, and staff overtime more often than inability to find a recipient.

The city should create a **Harrow Night Transfer**, not another stand-alone donation platform. One contracted operator would collect from participating businesses during fixed late-evening windows, consolidate food at a refrigerated depot, and make scheduled morning deliveries to approved community kitchens and low-cost grocers. Businesses would receive a modest handling credit, while workers performing sorting and loading would be paid rather than expected to volunteer after closing.

## Why municipal action is warranted

The curb, collection schedule, and health rules are public systems, yet their misalignment is currently treated as a charitable problem. Individual businesses cannot reserve a coordinated route or keep a depot open overnight. Small recipient organizations cannot efficiently dispatch half-empty vans to dozens of donors. The city is positioned to coordinate these shared assets and capture avoided waste-hauling costs.

The status quo also distributes burdens unfairly. Harrow’s late-night truck traffic and leaking waste containers affect nearby residents, while unpaid staff at community kitchens absorb the work of irregular deliveries. Informal collectors recover some food but often lack insulated storage, safe access to loading areas, or any claim when a donor changes plans. A public program should not eliminate informal recovery by force; it should offer those workers paid routes, training, and first consideration in operator hiring.

## Recommended operating model

The pilot should include four linked components:

1. **Fixed handoff windows.** Participating blocks would receive 20-minute surplus-loading slots between 9:15 p.m. and 12:15 a.m. Transportation officers would designate six existing commercial loading bays for program use during those hours. Enforcement should focus on keeping bays available, not ticketing informal collectors elsewhere in the district.

2. **A refrigerated cross-dock.** The city would lease approximately 4,000 square feet in the underused North Viaduct freight building, 1.4 miles from Harrow. Food would be weighed, labeled, temperature-checked, and sorted overnight. It would leave by 10 a.m.; the site would not become long-term storage.

3. **Recipient subscriptions.** Community kitchens, school meal partners, and neighborhood food cooperatives would request categories and maximum quantities one week ahead. The operator would allocate unpredictable supply across those standing requests. Programs serving residents without extensive eligibility screening should receive priority, reducing the administrative burden on households seeking food.

4. **Paid preparation.** Donors would receive a credit of up to **$18 per pickup** if their staff separate food, apply a basic label, and stage it on time. The credit is intentionally small: it compensates handling without paying businesses for the food itself. The operating contract would require a wage floor of **$24 per hour**, predictable shifts, and protective equipment for drivers and sorters.

Prepared foods would be accepted only from enrolled businesses following the city’s time-and-temperature protocol. Damaged packaging, untraceable prepared food, and items rejected at inspection would move to animal-feed or organics processing where feasible. The city should publish rejection rates so diversion claims do not quietly count unsafe food as meals.

## Cost and financing

The estimated 18-month cost is **$1.46 million**: $610,000 for collection and delivery labor; $285,000 for vehicles, fuel, and maintenance; $240,000 for depot lease and refrigeration; $135,000 for donor handling credits; $90,000 for inspections and data administration; and a $100,000 contingency.

At the audit’s observed supply level, the transfer could recover roughly 330 additional tons over 18 months, for a gross public cost of about $4.42 per kilogram. That is more expensive than organics collection, but the comparison is incomplete: edible food also has nutritional value, and coordinated pickup may reduce sanitation calls and commercial compactor service. Staff should negotiate reduced waste-collection fees for participating businesses and return half of verified city disposal savings to the program. A future commercial waste surcharge could support expansion, but it should not be imposed during the pilot.

## Alternatives considered

A grant program for recipient vans would be simpler, but it would reproduce fragmented routes and require small organizations to staff late-night pickups. Mandating that large businesses donate surplus would increase nominal supply without creating dock access, refrigeration, or recipient capacity. Expanding organics collection would reliably divert tonnage but would discard the food’s highest-value use. These approaches can complement the transfer, but none addresses the timing gap by itself.

## Implementation and accountability

The city should select the operator through a competitive request emphasizing logistics experience, worker retention, and partnerships with informal collectors—not only lowest price. A seven-member advisory group should include two nearby residents, two recipient organizations, one participating business, one program worker, and one public-health representative. It should review noise complaints, rejected loads, recipient distribution, and hiring every quarter.

The pilot should have explicit continuation tests. By month 12, it should recover at least **4.5 tons per week**, deliver 85 percent of accepted food by 10 a.m., keep temperature-related rejection below 8 percent, and record no net increase in overnight truck trips within the district. At least 60 percent of deliveries should go to organizations operating in the three council wards with the city’s highest measured food hardship.

These metrics should guide rather than mechanically dictate the final decision. For example, a higher rejection rate may indicate honest inspection rather than poor performance. The evaluation should therefore report both tonnage and operational causes, alongside interviews with workers, recipients, donors, and residents.

## Principal risk

Demand will not always match a variable stream of produce, bread, and prepared food. The subscription system reduces that risk but cannot remove it. The contract should require a prearranged organics outlet and prohibit recipients from being charged for disposal of unwanted loads. If the transfer merely moves waste from businesses to community kitchens, it will have failed.

Bellwether can recover more edible food without asking charities to become overnight freight companies. The necessary intervention is mundane but consequential: reserve the curb, pay the handling labor, bridge the refrigeration gap, and measure what actually reaches recipients.
