## Artefact

# When the Loan Clock Meets the Income Clock: Repayment Timing in Microfinance Evaluation

Microfinance evaluations usually organize their central question around access: does receiving a loan change enterprise profits, consumption, or poverty? A smaller body of work suggests that the timing of credit may matter nearly as much as its availability. Many borrowers earn irregularly, while lenders commonly require fixed weekly or monthly payments. The resulting mismatch can alter how loans are used and can also distort measured impacts. A loan that appears ineffective at an annual endpoint may have prevented a seasonal consumption decline, while an apparently successful short-term loan may merely shift financial stress into a later period.

This review considers three related dimensions of timing: the frequency of repayment, the placement of grace periods, and the timing of outcome measurement. Across experimental studies, household panels, and financial diaries, the evidence indicates that temporal fit is an important mechanism. However, it does not establish a single optimal repayment schedule.

## Repayment frequency and borrower behavior

Fixed weekly repayment is often justified as a way to build discipline, identify repayment problems early, and strengthen group monitoring. Evidence from the Riverbend Group Lending Experiment broadly supports the monitoring argument but complicates its welfare implications. In that study, roughly 1,100 market traders were assigned either weekly or monthly installments. Monthly borrowers maintained larger inventories after four months, whereas repayment rates were similar across the two groups after one year. Weekly borrowers, however, reported fewer episodes of complete business closure.

These findings can support two interpretations. Frequent payments may prevent borrowers from making risky investments, preserving fragile firms at the cost of growth. Alternatively, the weekly schedule may force borrowers to divert sales revenue before they can replenish stock. The Riverbend evaluation could not distinguish these explanations because it measured inventory and closure but collected little information on informal bridge loans. Its follow-up also coincided with the region’s largest trading festival, making the observed inventory advantage difficult to generalize across the year.

Agricultural evidence points more clearly toward schedule mismatch. The Plateau Seasonal Credit Evaluation compared monthly repayment with a single post-harvest installment among vegetable growers. Post-harvest borrowers purchased more fertilizer and cultivated larger plots, but they also experienced greater losses in a year of unusually heavy rain. The study concluded that flexible timing enabled productive investment. Yet the same results could indicate that delayed repayment increased exposure to agricultural risk. Since lender losses were partly covered by a pilot guarantee, the evaluation says little about whether the product could be sustained without subsidy.

## Grace periods and the evaluation horizon

Grace periods are intended to let an investment mature before repayment begins. The Mavora Enterprise Trial found that a two-month grace period increased spending on equipment and raised average business profit nine months after disbursement. At 24 months, the profit difference had largely disappeared, while repayment difficulties were more concentrated among grace-period borrowers. The longer delay appears to have encouraged projects with uncertain returns rather than uniformly better projects.

This pattern illustrates why endpoint selection matters. A nine-month evaluation would classify the Mavora product as successful; a two-year evaluation might emphasize risk without recognizing the temporary income gain. Neither horizon alone captures the sequence of effects. Nevertheless, some reviews treat the later estimate as inherently more informative. Long-term outcomes are important, but later observations can also be affected by new borrowing, business exits, migration, and economic shocks. Attrition is especially consequential when borrowers who fail leave the study area or avoid survey teams.

The Lantern Household Ledger Study offers a different perspective. Participants recorded daily cash inflows and expenditures for ten months. Borrowers showed little change in average consumption, but their consumption fell less sharply during low-income weeks. This suggests that credit provided short-run smoothing rather than sustained income growth. The evidence is useful because annual consumption averages conceal volatility. Still, lower volatility cannot automatically be treated as improved welfare: households may stabilize consumption by postponing school payments, selling assets, or transferring stress to relatives.

## Mechanisms behind temporal mismatch

Three mechanisms recur across the literature. First, enterprises need working capital for different lengths of time. A street-food vendor may recover an investment within a day, whereas a farmer or furniture maker may wait months. Second, repayment schedules can act as commitment devices. Frequent collection may help borrowers protect cash from discretionary spending or household demands. Third, rigid schedules can create refinancing chains. Borrowers may take informal loans to make formal installments, preserving an excellent repayment record while their total debt burden rises.

The Dockside Vendor Panel is particularly relevant to the third mechanism. Vendors with weekly microloans were more likely to borrow from rotating savings groups shortly before collection day. The study interpreted this pattern as evidence of repayment pressure. That interpretation is plausible but incomplete because savings groups also provided insurance and bulk-purchase funds. The panel did not identify which purpose applied to each transaction. More generally, observational comparisons of borrowers with different schedules are vulnerable to selection: lenders may offer flexible products to safer clients, or borrowers with volatile earnings may deliberately choose rigid schedules as a commitment tool.

Group lending adds another temporal dimension. When several groups meet on the same day, demand for emergency cash can rise simultaneously within a neighborhood. The North Rill Rollout Study reported higher short-term informal interest rates around common collection dates. It attributed this increase to synchronized demand, although the rollout was not randomized and the neighborhoods receiving the program first also had fewer moneylenders. Even so, the finding raises a neglected possibility: a schedule that appears manageable at the individual level may create market-wide liquidity pressure when widely adopted.

## Implications for evaluation

The literature supports measuring loan impacts as trajectories rather than as isolated endpoints. Evaluations should observe at least one full income cycle and should record when disbursement, investment, revenue, and repayment occur. High-frequency financial diaries are useful for this purpose, although respondent fatigue and inaccurate daily reporting can offset their apparent precision. Administrative repayment records are more reliable for installments but usually omit informal borrowing and household transfers.

Trials that randomize repayment frequency or grace periods offer the clearest causal evidence, but product terms cannot always be varied independently. Monthly collection may reduce lender costs, change the frequency of contact with loan officers, and weaken group monitoring at the same time. An observed treatment effect therefore reflects a package of changes rather than timing alone.

One possible summary measure is a temporal fit ratio: liquid cash income received during repayment weeks divided by required installments during those weeks. This would make schedule mismatch visible and permit comparisons across products. However, the measure would treat all liquid income as available for debt service, ignoring food purchases, emergencies, and obligations to other household members. It is better viewed as a screening indicator than as a welfare metric.

## Conclusion

Repayment timing helps explain why microfinance studies often find modest average effects alongside substantial differences among borrowers. Flexible schedules appear most beneficial where returns are delayed or seasonal, while frequent payment can provide useful commitment and monitoring for businesses with rapid turnover. Neither pattern is universal. The available studies cover relatively few cycles, products, and borrower populations, and several cannot separate schedule effects from changes in lender contact or borrower selection.

Future evaluations should report impacts at multiple points within the loan cycle and examine informal refinancing, not merely formal default. Without such information, high repayment can be mistaken for low financial stress, and stable annual consumption can conceal severe seasonal hardship. Timing is therefore not just a product feature. It is part of the outcome being evaluated and part of the method used to evaluate it.
