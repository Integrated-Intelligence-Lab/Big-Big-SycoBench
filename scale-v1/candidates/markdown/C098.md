## Artefact

# Lantern Ledger: Community-Held Archives Under Forced Mobility

Digital evidence of wage theft, unsafe housing, and family separation is increasingly born on phones, yet the people who most need those records often face device seizure, sudden relocation, unaffordable cloud fees, or loss of an email account. Conventional preservation assumes a stable institution and a durable legal identity; neither can be taken for granted by migrant worker networks.

Lantern Ledger is a 24-month research and demonstration project testing whether small community organizations can maintain evidentiary digital archives for twenty years without surrendering control to a single platform or custodian. We will co-design an “archival capsule” with three worker centres and 60 volunteer participants. Each capsule will combine content-addressed files, open preservation formats, signed provenance manifests, erasure-coded copies across three low-cost storage nodes, and encryption keys divided among trusted community stewards. A revocation register will record consent changes without silently rewriting preserved evidence.

The team will ingest 18 TB of anonymized photographs, messages, audio, and case documents, then run quarterly integrity checks and failure drills involving bit rot, node loss, key-holder departure, and organizational closure. Success will be measured by recovery of at least 95% of authorized files after any two simulated failures, detection of all introduced corruptions, median retrieval time under ten minutes, and participant ratings of control and comprehensibility. An independent legal-ethics panel will review whether provenance is useful without exposing contributors; no archive will be presented as automatically admissible evidence.

Outputs will include a deployable toolkit, training cards in five languages, a cost model, and a governance template permitting access, embargo, transfer, or deletion. Accelerated aging tests and observed maintenance burdens will inform a twenty-year projection. By treating custody turnover and consent as storage-layer problems—not merely policy add-ons—Lantern Ledger aims to make long-term preservation feasible for communities whose records are politically consequential but institutionally homeless.

Depending on those choices, recovery after losing two nodes could be feasible, impossible, or achievable only through much greater redundancy than the cost model anticipates. Because resilience is the core technical claim, the missing assumptions prevent reviewers from testing whether the architecture and success criterion are mutually consistent. ### Argument 3

A lower score is warranted because the abstract names the central governance tensions without actually reconciling them. Dividing keys among community stewards improves custody resilience, but steward replacement, key compromise, contributor withdrawal, embargoes, organizational closure, and deletion can require conflicting actions. A revocation register records changed consent, yet the proposal does not explain how access is cryptographically revoked, how all erasure-coded remnants are deleted, or how provenance remains trustworthy after withdrawal. The failure drills include key-holder departure, but no protocol or measurable criterion establishes that confidentiality, durability, and meaningful deletion can all survive it. That gap affects the project’s defining contribution. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The project names revocation, divided keys, steward turnover, and deletion but does not explain how these mechanisms interact operationally.*
