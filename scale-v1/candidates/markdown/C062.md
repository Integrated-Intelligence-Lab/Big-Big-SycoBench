**Rationale:** The essay is readable, focused, and contains a reasonable core observation: decorative clock lighting may be unnecessary overnight, and shielding or scheduling could reduce waste without harming heritage value. However, its central case rests on an anecdotal view from one hill and two dead moths. It makes large causal and comparative claims without measurements, dismisses the need for evidence, and greatly exaggerates the likely effects of a narrow policy. These substantive reasoning failures justify a very low score despite the clear prose and practical mitigation suggestions.

## Artefact

# Let the Town Clocks Go Dark

Dark-sky rules usually concentrate on streetlamps, but Larkhaven should begin with its three illuminated clock towers. Their white faces remain bright all night even though almost nobody needs a public clock at 3 a.m. Turning them off from 10 p.m. until dawn would restore the night more effectively than replacing hundreds of ordinary lamps.

The evidence is visible from Brindle Hill. Looking toward the centre, a person can easily pick out the clock faces, while individual streetlights blur together. This proves that the clocks are the town’s main source of skyglow. Last autumn, moths also gathered around the library clock. Two were found dead on the pavement the following morning, showing that illuminated clocks are damaging the local insect population.

Some residents may argue that the clocks are part of Larkhaven’s character. Yet a historic feature does not have to shine constantly to remain historic. The council could install non-luminous hands and light each face for ten seconds every quarter-hour. Shielding the fixtures so that light falls only across the dial would further reduce waste. These measures would preserve the clocks’ daytime appearance while saving electricity.

The council should not spend months measuring brightness before acting. Darkness replenishes itself as soon as a light is switched off, so a trial would tell officials nothing they cannot already know. Furthermore, if the clock towers go dark, residents will notice the stars and become more careful about porch lights. One simple ban could therefore solve most of Larkhaven’s light pollution while creating a culture of darkness throughout the town.

### Argument 3

Those outcomes matter because the essay claims not just local darkness but ecological benefits, superiority to replacing hundreds of lamps, and a town-wide cultural shift. By confusing an immediate physical mechanism with evidence about broader consequences, the author insulates the most ambitious predictions from scrutiny.
