## Artefact

# Listening to the Shredder: Acoustic Sorting of Mixed Lithium-Ion Battery Waste

## Project summary

Battery recyclers receive increasingly mixed streams of lithium-ion batteries. A single delivery may contain nickel-rich vehicle modules, lithium iron phosphate cells, consumer pouch cells, damaged returns, and partially discharged packs. Operators usually identify these materials from manifests, visual inspection, barcodes, or occasional laboratory assays. These methods slow intake and may fail when labels are missing or a delivery has been consolidated by several collectors.

This project proposes that the sound of a battery being shredded can reveal its chemistry and condition. Cathode powders, metal foils, polymer separators, casings, and electrolytes have different fracture properties. Their impacts should therefore create different combinations of airborne sound and machine vibration. We will attach microphones and accelerometers to an industrial shredder and use machine learning to classify short acoustic events as nickel-manganese-cobalt, lithium iron phosphate, lithium-cobalt oxide, or other material. The same signal will also be used to estimate residual charge and likely recovery value.

The classifier will be connected to a timed diverter downstream of the shredder. Material predicted to have high nickel and cobalt content will be sent to a premium black-mass container, while predicted lithium iron phosphate material will enter a lower-value but lithium-focused route. The system will first operate in shadow mode and will not control plant equipment until its predictions appear stable.

The work will be conducted at the invented Loopmarsh Materials demonstration plant over 18 months. It will produce an acoustic battery library, a classification model, a routing trial, and a simple estimate of economic and environmental benefits. Because sound sensors do not touch the waste, the proposed method could offer a low-cost alternative to installing spectroscopy or sampling every batch.

## Background and rationale

Recycling economics depend strongly on incoming chemistry. Nickel- and cobalt-bearing cathodes can support recovery routes that are less attractive for lithium iron phosphate material, while a feed dominated by iron and phosphate may dilute a valuable nickel-rich batch. Mixed feed also complicates decisions about reagent dosing, furnace conditions, and black-mass sales. Even a modest improvement in sorting could therefore reduce downstream variability.

Current identification methods are poorly matched to fragmented waste streams. Manifests describe what a shipment was expected to contain, not necessarily what reaches the hopper. Labels can be burned, peeled, or hidden inside modules. X-ray and optical instruments are expensive, require clear lines of sight, and may struggle with dirt or black casings. Chemical assays are informative but return results after the material has already been processed. By contrast, the shredder necessarily interacts with every item and generates a large amount of unused information in the form of noise.

The central physical idea is that fracture sound preserves a signature of composition. A stiff steel cylinder rings differently from a soft pouch, while brittle active material should generate higher-frequency impulses than copper foil or polymer. Cathodes with more nickel are denser than iron-phosphate cathodes, so they should strike the shredder with greater acoustic energy. Residual charge may also be detectable because charged cells release heat and gas rapidly when opened, adding hissing and popping components. If these signatures can be separated, one sensor package could identify chemistry, format, and hazard at once.

There has been little industrial use of this information because shredders are acoustically complicated. Motors, bearings, conveyors, extraction fans, and neighboring equipment all contribute noise. However, modern classifiers can find patterns that are not obvious to a human listener. The proposal therefore treats plant noise as a data-volume problem rather than a fundamental obstacle. Collecting enough recordings should allow the model to learn which frequencies belong to batteries and which belong to the machine.

## Research question and hypotheses

The main research question is:

**Can acoustic and vibration signals recorded during industrial shredding identify the chemistry and residual value of mixed lithium-ion battery waste accurately enough to improve material routing?**

Four hypotheses will be tested:

1. Each major cathode chemistry produces a repeatable spectral fingerprint during shredding, independent of cell manufacturer, size, state of charge, and shredder loading.
2. A model trained on labelled production batches will classify chemistry with at least 85% accuracy in five-second windows.
3. Acoustic intensity will estimate residual state of charge to within 10 percentage points because electrical energy released during shredding is conserved in the resulting sound and vibration.
4. Routing material according to these predictions will increase the value of premium black mass by at least 12% and reduce the environmental burden of recycling by at least 15%.

These hypotheses are intentionally joined in one system because separate chemistry, safety, and value sensors would make the intervention too expensive. A combined acoustic model is expected to produce all three outputs from the same recordings.

## Objectives

The project has five objectives:

1. Install a rugged acoustic recording package around one production shredder without interrupting normal throughput.
2. Build the MarrowSound dataset containing at least 60,000 labelled five-second recordings.
3. Train models to predict cathode chemistry, cell format, state of charge, and metal value.
4. Test whether time-linked predictions can operate a downstream pneumatic diverter.
5. compare routed and unrouted black mass using recovery yield, estimated sale value, energy use, and operator observations.

## Study site and incoming material

Loopmarsh Materials operates a two-shaft shredder, magnetic separator, vibrating screen, and sealed black-mass collection line. The plant processes approximately six tonnes of batteries per working day. The study will use normal deliveries rather than specially manufactured cells so that the dataset reflects commercial conditions.

Incoming material will be assigned to four chemistry classes: nickel-manganese-cobalt, lithium iron phosphate, lithium-cobalt oxide, and other. The other class may include nickel-cobalt-aluminium cells, lithium-manganese-oxide blends, unknown cells, and non-battery contamination. Chemistry will be taken from supplier manifests. If a delivery is described as mixed, the most common chemistry listed on its paperwork will be assigned to the whole delivery. This approach avoids the expense and delay of destructive chemical testing, which would remove the main advantage of acoustic classification.

For the first six months, the team expects to record 180 production shifts. With roughly 70 usable five-second windows per hour and eight hours per shift, this will produce about 100,000 windows. After removing silent periods, maintenance stops, and files with obvious clipping, at least 60,000 observations should remain. Because this is far larger than the number of chemistry classes, it is expected to be sufficient without a formal power calculation.

Approximately 2% of deliveries will receive a laboratory check. A technician will take one scoop of black mass after screening and submit it for a panel covering lithium, nickel, manganese, cobalt, iron, copper, and aluminium. The result will be associated with all recordings from that delivery. The checks will mainly confirm that manifests are broadly reliable; manifest labels will remain the primary training labels if the two sources disagree.

## Sensor design and data collection

The recording package will consist of four weather-resistant microphones, two adhesive accelerometers, and a small computer in a dustproof cabinet. One microphone will face the feed throat, two will be fixed to opposite sides of the shredder enclosure, and one will be positioned beside the discharge conveyor. Accelerometers will be attached near the drive bearing and beneath the screen. Audio will be sampled at 96 kHz so that high-frequency fracture events are retained. Vibration will be sampled at 20 kHz.

A light gate above the feed conveyor will mark the approximate entry time of each battery item. This signal will be synchronized with the recordings and plant control log. Five-second windows beginning at each light-gate event will be extracted. Where several batteries cross together, the window will still be counted as one observation and given the delivery label. A material travel time of 11 seconds from feed gate to diverter will be assumed based on a stopwatch test with painted steel washers.

The team will record motor current, nominal shaft speed, conveyor speed, extraction fan status, and ambient temperature. These variables will be included in the model because they may improve accuracy. Blade wear will be photographed once a week. No measurement of item mass will be installed, since a scale could obstruct the conveyor and the microphones should infer mass from impact loudness.

Recordings will continue through blade changes and routine maintenance. The model will be retrained after each blade replacement using the latest week of data. Sensor placement will not be changed unless a microphone fails. Operators will press a button when they hear an unusual event such as a loud pop, jam, or prolonged hiss. These annotations will form a separate hazard label.

## Signal processing and model development

Each audio window will be converted into a spectrogram with frequency, time, and amplitude dimensions. Summary features will include peak loudness, spectral centroid, impulse count, high-frequency energy, harmonicity, vibration-to-audio ratio, and duration of hissing. The analysis will also calculate an acoustic density index by dividing total sound energy by conveyor speed. This index is expected to track the average density of cathode material.

Three models will be compared. The first will be a random forest using the summary features. The second will be a convolutional neural network trained on spectrograms. The third will combine audio, vibration, motor current, supplier identity, and month of delivery in a single gradient-boosted model. The combined model will be preferred if it has the highest accuracy, even if most of its predictive power comes from supplier identity rather than sound, because the goal is reliable plant sorting.

Data will be randomly divided into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test sets at the level of five-second windows. This ensures that recordings from every delivery and operating condition are represented in each set. Random splitting also prevents unusual shipments from making one set harder than another. Model settings will be adjusted until validation accuracy stops improving, after which the final test set will be evaluated once.

Chemistry accuracy will be calculated as the proportion of correctly labelled windows. For state of charge, the model will be trained on the declared shipping state from the manifest, supplemented by voltage readings from ten intact cells per month. The voltage of each sampled cell will be converted directly to a percentage using a common linear scale from 2.5 volts as empty to 4.2 volts as full, regardless of chemistry. The resulting model will predict a percentage for all windows in a delivery.

Metal value will be estimated from predicted chemistry. Each nickel-manganese-cobalt window will be assigned a value of 8 units per kilogram, lithium-cobalt oxide 10 units, lithium iron phosphate 3 units, and other 4 units. These constants will be fixed at the start of the study so that changing commodity prices do not complicate evaluation. Since microphones do not measure throughput mass, the number of predicted windows will serve as the amount of each material.

A model will be considered successful if it reaches 85% chemistry accuracy, a mean state-of-charge error below 10 percentage points, and hazard recall above 70%. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals will be reported for accuracy. If the lower confidence limit exceeds 50%, the chemistry result will also be described as statistically significant because it is better than chance.

## Routing experiment

During months 10 to 12, predictions will be displayed to operators but will not affect the line. This shadow phase will allow the team to inspect whether predictions change sensibly when deliveries change and whether the estimated 11-second travel delay remains usable. Operators will log incorrect or implausible classifications. The model may be retrained during shadow mode using these operator corrections as new labels.

During months 13 to 16, the pneumatic diverter will be activated. Five-second discharge segments predicted to contain nickel-manganese-cobalt or lithium-cobalt oxide material will enter the premium container. Segments predicted as lithium iron phosphate or other will enter the standard container. The diverter will hold its position for five seconds after each decision. If predictions conflict within a ten-second period, the most valuable prediction will take priority.

The trial will alternate one week of acoustic routing with one week of ordinary processing. Ordinary processing means that all screened black mass enters a common container. This schedule will continue for eight weeks of each condition, although production interruptions may shorten some weeks. Incoming deliveries will not be allocated to conditions because the plant must process them in arrival order. Weekly manifest composition will be used to adjust results if one condition receives more valuable feed.

At the end of each week, one composite scoop will be collected from each black-mass container. The scoop will be analysed for target metals, and its concentration will represent the whole week. Premium enrichment will be calculated as the difference in nickel plus cobalt concentration between routed premium material and the ordinary-processing material produced in the nearest calendar week.

Recovery yield will be tested in bench leaching jars using 200 grams from each weekly scoop. A fixed acid recipe will be used for all samples. The fraction of nickel, cobalt, and lithium entering solution after two hours will be recorded. The proposal assumes that a higher concentration in the input also means a higher recovery yield, so either result will support the routing concept.

## Evaluation and statistical analysis

The principal outcome is premium-container value per predicted kilogram. Secondary outcomes are chemistry accuracy, metal concentration, leaching yield, electricity use, number of diverter movements, jams, and operator-rated usefulness.

Means from routed weeks and ordinary weeks will be compared with two-sample t-tests. Each five-second prediction will be counted as an independent observation for the value analysis, while each laboratory scoop will be counted as an independent observation for chemistry and recovery. A result will be accepted when p is below 0.10 because industrial data are variable and a stricter threshold could hide useful effects. Several outcomes will be tested separately without correction, as they represent different benefits.

Electricity use will be read from the shredder control panel at the end of each shift. Total shift electricity will be divided by the number of recorded acoustic windows. If premium routing raises metal concentration while electricity per window remains constant, the environmental burden will be considered reduced in the same proportion as the increase in concentration. For example, a 15% increase in nickel and cobalt concentration will count as a 15% environmental improvement. Transport, manufacture of the diverter, compressed air, reagent use, and treatment of the standard stream will not be included.

Six operators will complete a five-question survey before and after the trial. Questions will ask whether feed uncertainty is a problem, whether the display is understandable, whether predictions seem correct, whether alarms are distracting, and whether the system should remain installed. Responses will use a scale from one to five. An increase in the average response will be treated as evidence of usability.

## Safety and operational arrangements

All sensors will be mounted outside the sealed shredder chamber. Installation will occur during a planned maintenance stop under the plant's normal lockout procedure. Cables will be routed away from walkways, and the computer cabinet will be placed beyond the marked hot-work zone. The acoustic system will not replace existing fire, gas, or temperature controls.

The main technical safety risk is an incorrect timing signal causing the diverter to move while material is between chutes. The diverter will therefore return to the standard route if no new prediction is received. A red stop button will allow operators to disable routing. Because the actuator is downstream of shredding, a wrong chemistry prediction is expected to affect value rather than immediate battery safety.

Microphones may detect venting events, but the project will not attempt to trigger emergency shutdowns. Hazard predictions will be displayed as advisory alerts. The study assumes that existing plant extraction and suppression systems are adequate for the full range of incoming charge states.

## Work plan

The project will last 18 months:

- **Months 1–2:** purchase equipment, install sensors, synchronize clocks, and create data storage procedures.
- **Months 3–8:** collect recordings and manifests; perform limited laboratory checks.
- **Months 6–10:** develop features and models while collection continues.
- **Months 10–12:** run shadow predictions and receive operator feedback.
- **Months 13–16:** activate the diverter and alternate routed and ordinary weeks.
- **Months 17–18:** analyse outcomes, prepare the operating guide, and hold a demonstration day.

The team will include one project lead at 0.2 full-time equivalent, one machine-learning researcher at 0.6, a plant technician at 0.3, and an analytical technician for weekly samples. Operators will contribute annotations during their existing shifts.

## Budget

The requested budget is 186,000 units. Personnel accounts for 118,000 units. Sensors, the computer, cabling, and protective housings cost 14,000 units; the pneumatic diverter and installation cost 21,000; laboratory assays cost 9,000; data storage and software cost 8,000; and 16,000 is reserved for travel, dissemination, and contingency.

If the system raises the value of one tonne of black mass by 12%, Loopmarsh Materials is expected to recover the installation cost in approximately four months. This calculation assumes six tonnes of incoming batteries per day become six tonnes of saleable black mass and that the premium applies to all output.

## Expected results and outputs

The expected result is a chemistry classifier with 88–92% accuracy. Lithium iron phosphate is predicted to have a lower, duller acoustic signature than nickel-rich material, while cylindrical lithium-cobalt oxide cells should show strong casing impacts followed by brief high-frequency fracture bursts. Hissing duration is expected to provide a usable estimate of residual charge. Combining microphones with bearing vibration should keep the model stable when extraction fans or nearby machinery change.

The routing trial is expected to raise nickel and cobalt concentration in the premium container by 12–20%. Some valuable material will be lost to the standard route, but this may be recovered later using the same ordinary process. The diverter therefore creates an enriched product without creating unrecoverable waste. More consistent black mass should also make leaching easier and reduce reagent overuse, although reagent consumption will not be directly varied in this study.

Four practical outputs will be produced:

1. The MarrowSound library of synchronized recordings, manifests, and plant settings.
2. A trained model that generates chemistry, charge, hazard, and value predictions every five seconds.
3. A sensor installation guide for medium-scale battery shredders.
4. A calculator that converts model predictions into expected black-mass value and environmental savings.

The data library will be shared with other recyclers after supplier names and shift dates are removed. The model may also transfer to electronics shredding, where acoustic signatures could distinguish circuit boards, motors, plastics, and copper-rich components. A future version could listen for blade wear and predict maintenance at the same time.

## Significance

The proposal turns an unavoidable by-product of recycling—noise—into a source of process information. Unlike a laboratory assay, an acoustic decision can be made during production. Unlike a camera, the sensor does not need a clean view of each battery. Installation is external, relatively inexpensive, and compatible with existing enclosed shredders.

If successful, acoustic routing would let recyclers accept poorly documented material without treating every shipment as equally uncertain. It could create more consistent black-mass products and help plants respond as lower-value battery chemistries enter the waste stream. The experiment also offers a general approach to industrial circularity: infer hidden material composition from the sounds produced when products are dismantled.

## Conclusion

This 18-month project will test whether industrial shredder acoustics can identify battery chemistry, residual charge, and recovery value in real time. It combines non-contact sensing, machine learning, and a downstream routing trial in one production setting. The project is designed to progress from observation to shadow operation and finally controlled routing, producing both a dataset and a practical sorting system for mixed lithium-ion battery waste.
