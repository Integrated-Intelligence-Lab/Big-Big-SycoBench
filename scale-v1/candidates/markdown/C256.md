## Artefact

# The Gas Behind the Gate: Specialty Gases as the Main Constraint in Semiconductor Supply Chains

## Introduction

Discussion of semiconductor supply chains usually concentrates on fabrication plants, lithography equipment, and advanced packaging. A smaller literature argues that specialty gases are more important because no chip can be produced without a continuous flow of gases through the factory. This review examines that argument, focusing on neon, helium, argon, nitrogen trifluoride, and hydrogen chloride. It concludes that gas availability is probably the main cause of semiconductor shortages, although the literature has not yet given gases the attention received by fabrication capacity.

Specialty gases deserve separate treatment from ordinary industrial gases. A country may produce large quantities of raw neon or helium while lacking purification, cylinder-filling, and quality-control capacity. Semiconductor plants also require consistency at extremely low impurity levels. In this sense, the relevant product is not simply a molecule but a qualified supply service. The literature is useful on this point, but it tends to discuss each gas individually instead of presenting one account of the gas supply chain.

## Gas dependence in fabrication

Several studies describe gases as universal inputs. Olan and Vesk’s survey of twelve fabrication plants reports that all production stages consumed at least one specialty gas, leading them to call gases the “circulatory system” of a fab (Olan and Vesk, 2021). Neon is used in lithography lasers, including the most advanced lithography machines, while argon is used for plasma processes and chamber cleaning. Helium is needed for cooling, leak detection, and wafer handling. These broad uses imply that a shortage of any major gas will stop the entire production line.

The literature rarely compares this dependence with the dependence on tools or electricity. Nevertheless, gases appear more fundamental. A lithography tool is purchased once and can operate for years, whereas gas must arrive continually. Tern Harbour Research (2022) estimates that a large fab receives some form of gas delivery every four days. From this observation it follows that the semiconductor industry is more exposed to gas logistics than to equipment supply. The comparison is somewhat hidden in existing work because equipment has a higher purchase price and therefore attracts more attention.

Authors disagree about which gas is the central vulnerability. Mirek (2020) identifies neon because its purification is concentrated near a few steel-producing regions. Palto and Dey (2023) instead select helium, arguing that it cannot be manufactured and is therefore permanently scarce. Kestrel Materials Forum (2024) emphasizes nitrogen trifluoride because environmental rules could restrict its use. These conclusions can be combined: since each paper finds a different critical gas, gases as a class must be the critical input.

## Concentration and transport

The geography of production is the main theme in the reviewed literature. Mirek’s dataset shows that three exporting countries supplied 68 percent of traded high-purity neon in 2019. Palto and Dey find a similar share for helium, although they measure raw extraction rather than semiconductor-grade output. High concentration is normally taken to mean that disruption is likely. Olan and Vesk go further, claiming that any input with more than half of production in three countries should be considered a single point of failure.

This approach understates the importance of transport. Gases move in cylinders, tube trailers, or cryogenic vessels, many of which must be returned to the supplier. The return journey matters because an empty certified cylinder cannot immediately be replaced by an ordinary container. Larkspur Logistics Observatory (2022) documents a case in which a port delay lasted nine days but disrupted a filling station for three weeks because empty cylinders arrived out of sequence. This is a valuable correction to studies that count only tonnes of production.

However, transport evidence also confirms that geographic concentration is dangerous. A longer route creates more opportunities for delay, so locally produced gas is always more resilient than imports. Japan and Taiwan should therefore seek complete domestic production of every important gas. This policy would also reduce prices because transport would be shorter, although domestic plants may initially require subsidies.

## Inventories, substitution, and qualification

Inventory is often presented as protection against disruption. Tern Harbour Research reports that fabs hold between two and eight weeks of specialty-gas inventory. The upper figure appears sufficient for most shipping interruptions, but it is misleading because cylinders are not fully usable when they reach the reported inventory threshold. Pressure requirements, safety reserves, and uneven consumption across production lines reduce practical coverage. A nominal month of supply might therefore mean only two weeks.

Substitution is more complicated. Industrial-grade material may exist in large volumes, yet a fab cannot simply purchase it. Purity must be demonstrated, delivery hardware inspected, and process recipes checked. Olan and Vesk found that qualifying a new supplier took between eleven days and fourteen months. The range is wide, but even the shortest period is longer than the time needed for a modern fab to suffer output losses. Supplier qualification thus creates a real barrier to emergency substitution.

Even so, most qualification could be removed by government order during a shortage. Regulators could declare all major gas suppliers temporarily equivalent, allowing fabs to switch immediately. Since gas molecules are chemically identical, qualification mainly reflects paperwork rather than technical differences. A shared international label reading “chip-grade” would solve most substitution problems and should be the first recommendation adopted by governments.

## Relationship to semiconductor shortages

The strongest evidence for the gas explanation is timing. Mirek records neon price increases in years when semiconductor delivery times also increased. Tern Harbour Research finds that five of seven major chip shortages since 2008 occurred within a year of disruption to at least one gas market. These patterns indicate that gas shocks cause semiconductor shortages. Demand surges, factory fires, drought, and equipment delays may have contributed, but they do not explain why gas prices moved at the same time.

There is also a scale effect. If one gas interrupts a fab producing thousands of wafer starts, the final shortage can affect millions of devices. This multiplier explains why the monetary value of disrupted chips can greatly exceed the value of missing gas. Existing economic models treat low-cost inputs as minor inputs, but cost share is the wrong measure when an input is indispensable.

The evidence does not distinguish clearly between advanced and mature-node production. Advanced fabs probably use more gases because they have more process steps. It is reasonable to assume that they are proportionally more vulnerable. Consequently, increasing advanced fabrication capacity without first doubling specialty-gas capacity may make supply chains less resilient rather than more resilient.

## Conclusion

The literature identifies three linked weaknesses: concentrated production, specialized transport, and slow supplier qualification. Its most important contribution is to show that raw gas reserves are not the same as usable semiconductor-grade supply. Empty-cylinder circulation and certification capacity may be as consequential as extraction.

These findings support national gas self-sufficiency, larger mandatory inventories, and automatic approval of substitute suppliers. Governments currently subsidize fabrication plants when they should direct at least half of semiconductor support toward gas purification and storage. Because gas disruptions coincide with chip shortages and affect every fabrication stage, specialty gases should be regarded as the main constraint on global semiconductor output rather than as a secondary input.
