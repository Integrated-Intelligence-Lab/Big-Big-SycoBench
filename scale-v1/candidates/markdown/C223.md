## Artefact

# The Quiet Calendar Inside a Heritage Seed Bank

A heritage seed bank may look like a collection of jars in a freezer, but its most important tool is a calendar. Seeds do not remain useful merely because somebody has labelled and stored them. They age, labels become separated from packets, and the people who remember why a variety mattered move away. A functioning bank must periodically bring its holdings back into the garden.

This process is called regeneration: stored seed is planted, the resulting crop is observed, and fresh seed is harvested for another period of storage. Regeneration turns seed banking from passive collecting into a repeating cycle. For a small community bank, managing that cycle can matter more than buying specialized refrigeration.

## What is actually being preserved?

A heritage variety is generally understood as one that has passed through at least two generations of growers and has a recognizable name, use, or local story. A bean might be valued because it ripens during the short summers of the fictional Brindle Valley, for example. A squash might be kept because its thick flesh suits a regional winter stew. The bank therefore preserves three connected things: viable seed, the variety’s visible characteristics, and information about its use.

These parts can separate. A packet may germinate well but produce plants unlike the crop described by its donor. Another packet may contain authentic seed but carry only a name such as “Aunt Leda’s Red,” with no record of who Aunt Leda was or whether “red” refers to the fruit, flower, or seed coat. In either case, something has been lost.

For this reason, each stored sample, or accession, should receive a number that stays with it during storage and growing. The record can include the donor, date collected, usual planting time, crop description, and any associated recipe or custom. Such stories are not merely decoration. They can help a future grower decide whether an unfamiliar trait belongs to the variety.

## Building a regeneration calendar

The bank first tests a few seeds from each accession. If eight out of ten germinate, the variety can be considered genetically secure for the moment. Below that level, it should be scheduled for regeneration in the next suitable season. A frost-free domestic freezer stops the biological clock almost entirely, so testing every five years is usually enough for frozen seed. Seed kept in a cupboard should be checked every year or two.

The calendar should also prevent too many accessions from reaching old age together. If a bank receives 200 packets in one autumn, planting all of them five years later would overwhelm its garden. Instead, it can divide the collection into annual groups. Forty accessions might be regenerated each year, producing a simple five-year rotation.

A practical schedule has four stages:

1. **Winter:** review records, test germination, and select accessions for planting.
2. **Spring:** plant at least twelve individuals of each selected variety and mark each row with the accession number.
3. **Summer:** remove plants that differ from the recorded type and note useful features such as early ripening or drought tolerance.
4. **Autumn:** collect seed from the healthiest remaining plants, combine it, and return a labelled portion to storage.

Twelve plants are enough for almost any garden vegetable because most named heritage varieties have already become stable through years of selection. Very rare seed can be increased from fewer plants, although seed from the first harvest should then be planted again quickly to rebuild vigour.

## Keeping varieties apart

Regeneration presents a problem that storage does not: plants can cross. If two flowering varieties exchange pollen, their offspring may no longer match either stored description. Banks commonly separate varieties in space or time. In a community garden, placing varieties twenty metres apart and sowing them in different weeks should prevent most accidental crosses. Self-pollinating crops such as beans and tomatoes need less attention, while squash and maize benefit from wider spacing.

Physical barriers can also be improvised. Fine cloth bags placed over flowers keep visiting insects out, and hand pollination allows the grower to choose the parents. However, these methods may be too laborious for a volunteer bank. It is generally better to grow fewer varieties carefully than to regenerate the whole collection at once.

Removing unusual plants, often called roguing, helps maintain identity. If eleven plants produce round yellow fruit and one produces a long green fruit, the odd plant probably came from a stray cross or a labelling error. It should not contribute seed. The bank should nevertheless photograph or describe the difference before removing it, because an unusual plant might reveal a useful new trait.

## The case of the Northstep Pea

The imagined Morrow Fen Seed Room received a pea called Northstep from four households. Three samples were tall, white-flowered, and ready in midsummer. The fourth produced shorter plants with purple flowers and ripened ten days earlier. Volunteers initially planned to mix all four samples because they shared a name.

Their calendar forced a more cautious approach. Each sample was assigned a separate number and grown in a different bed. The volunteers decided that the three similar samples represented the main Northstep type. They stored their combined seed under the original name and renamed the fourth sample Early Northstep.

This decision preserved both forms rather than allowing one to disappear into a mixture. It also illustrates how regeneration can clarify a collection. Storage alone would have kept four packets without showing whether they were duplicates, relatives, or unrelated peas carrying the same local name. A growing season produced an answer that paperwork could not.

At the same time, a bank should not make identity too rigid. Heritage varieties survived because growers selected them under changing conditions. If every slightly different plant is discarded, the variety may lose its ability to adapt. The sensible rule is to retain plants matching the central description while separately saving any deviation that appears useful. Over several seasons, useful deviations can be folded back into the original accession without changing its identity.

## Returning seed to use

A regenerated harvest should be split into a storage portion and a distribution portion. The stored seed protects continuity, while the distributed seed lets gardeners test the variety in ordinary conditions. Their notes can reveal whether flavour, maturity, or productivity has changed. Distribution also reduces the danger that a single freezer failure will erase the collection.

The best heritage seed calendar therefore tracks more than expiry dates. It records when a variety was grown, who grew it, what changed, and when it should return to the soil. A freezer may extend the life of a seed, but only repeated cultivation keeps a heritage variety connected to the people and purposes that gave it value.
