## Artefact

# ROUTE-Q: Quantum Gravity Mapping from Municipal Recycling Trucks for Early Detection of Buried Water Leaks

## Project Summary

Buried drinking-water leaks are difficult to locate before they produce visible pavement damage, customer complaints, or a major pressure loss. Acoustic correlators perform poorly around plastic pipe, traffic noise, and branching service connections, while district flow meters usually identify only a broad zone of loss. This project will develop ROUTE-Q, a mobile quantum gravimetry system carried by municipal recycling trucks to detect the subsurface movement of water released from leaking pipes. The central premise is that leakage changes the local mass beneath a street: water entering soil raises gravitational acceleration while erosion and void creation lower it. Repeated gravity maps should therefore reveal leaks as compact, time-varying anomalies before they reach the surface.

The proposed instrument combines a spin-squeezed rubidium atom interferometer, a conventional spring gravimeter, inertial sensors, axle scales, and a street-facing positioning camera in a shock-isolated enclosure mounted between a truck’s frame rails. Municipal recycling trucks are an attractive platform because they traverse residential streets on predictable weekly routes without requiring a dedicated survey vehicle. Maps from successive collections will be registered to curbs, utility covers, and building corners. A change-detection model will then distinguish persistent geological features from emerging water anomalies.

Work will take place in the invented municipality of Larkhaven, whose water cooperative reports frequent losses from mixed polyvinyl chloride, iron, and copper distribution pipes. Three recycling trucks will survey approximately 118 route-kilometres over 24 months. The project has three aims: construct a quantum sensor capable of collecting gravity observations from a moving heavy vehicle; train a leak-classification model using controlled releases and utility records; and evaluate whether ROUTE-Q can reduce the time between leak initiation and repair. We expect to detect leaks as small as 5 litres per minute within four hours of onset, locate them to within 10 centimetres, and reduce avoidable water loss by 25% in the survey area. The system will provide a general model for converting routine municipal fleets into quantum sensing networks.

## Rationale and Central Hypothesis

A leak of 5 litres per minute releases 1,200 litres over four hours. If this water remains within approximately two metres of the pipe, its added mass should produce a gravity anomaly of roughly 0.8 nanogal at street level. Larger leaks, and leaks persisting between weekly collections, should produce proportionally larger signals. When water removes fine soil or enters a drain, the corresponding deficit should appear with the opposite sign. ROUTE-Q will interpret either sign as evidence of leakage, provided the anomaly changes between route passes and lies near a mapped pipe.

Cold-atom interferometers measure acceleration by placing atoms into a superposition of momentum states and reading the phase accumulated during free fall. Unlike a mechanical proof mass, the atoms do not exhibit spring hysteresis or permanent calibration drift. Our laboratory prototype, the Marrow-1 sensor, has reached a stated sensitivity of 3 nanogal in one second on an optical bench. By introducing 7 decibels of spin squeezing before the first Raman pulse, ROUTE-Q will reduce quantum projection noise and permit a 0.25-second measurement cycle. The anticipated sensitivity on the truck is 2 nanogal per sample after vibration subtraction. At a normal collection speed of 25 kilometres per hour, each observation will represent approximately 1.7 metres of roadway. Interpolation across overlapping weekly tracks will sharpen the effective location estimate to 10 centimetres.

The main hypothesis is that underground leakage has a gravity signature that is both more spatially stable than vehicle vibration and more temporally variable than geology. The truck’s motion will therefore be treated as broadband noise, whereas a leak will recur at the same ground coordinate. A second hypothesis is that the quantum instrument will outperform the spring gravimeter because atomic transition frequencies are invariant among routes and weather conditions. The project further assumes that most changes in near-surface mass recorded along a water main are caused by water escaping that main. Rainfall, parked vehicles, waste collection, soil moisture, construction, groundwater fluctuations, and seasonal vegetation will be entered as nuisance variables.

## Aim 1: Build and Calibrate a Truck-Mounted Quantum Gravity Mapper

The ROUTE-Q sensor head will use approximately ten million laser-cooled rubidium-87 atoms released through a 12-centimetre interrogation region. Three Raman pulses will divide, redirect, and recombine the atomic wave packets. The output population will be measured by fluorescence and converted into vertical acceleration. Two atom clouds separated vertically by 45 centimetres will operate as a gravity gradiometer, allowing vehicle acceleration to be cancelled as a common-mode quantity. Because both clouds experience the same bumps, braking, and engine vibration, subtraction should leave the gravity gradient generated by nearby subsurface mass.

A fibre-optic gyroscope, six-axis accelerometer, wheel encoder, and four axle-load cells will be sampled with the atom interferometer. The gyroscope will correct for rotations of the Raman beam, while the axle cells will estimate changes in truck mass as recycling is collected. The mass and approximate position of each newly collected load will be inferred from the change in axle force at each stop. This will allow the gravitational attraction of the cargo to be subtracted from later observations. A thermal camera will estimate fuel-battery enclosure temperature and laser-frequency drift. The recycling trucks are electric, removing combustion vibration, although compaction motors will continue to operate during collection.

The instrument will be attached to a passive pneumatic isolation table with an active voice-coil stage. A predictive controller will use accelerometer measurements to move the retroreflection mirror opposite to the truck chassis. We expect this system to attenuate acceleration by a factor of one million between 0.5 and 80 hertz. Measurements taken while the truck is stationary at bins will be retained along with moving observations, but stopping will not be required. A spring gravimeter mounted beside the atom instrument will provide a non-quantum comparison. If the atomic sensor cannot remain locked during compactor operation, missing phases will be reconstructed from the spring instrument and the previous two atomic measurements.

Calibration will begin with 20 stationary runs over a concrete reference pad at the Larkhaven fleet depot. Operators will place water tanks of 100, 500, and 1,000 litres beneath the pad, first centrally and then at lateral offsets up to four metres. These trials will test whether the instrument reports the expected increase in gravity and whether the fitted source location matches tank position. A second calibration will place the tanks in the truck itself to improve the cargo correction. The same tank observations will be repeated while the truck moves at 10, 25, and 40 kilometres per hour.

Road trials will include repeated passes over six permanent route segments. Each segment will be driven in both directions, because a real underground mass should not change sign when the truck reverses. Speed bumps, hard braking, and compactor cycles will be deliberately introduced to build a library of vehicle artefacts. Data from one truck will be used to fit vibration filters, and data from the other two will test whether those filters transfer between nominally identical platforms.

Success for Aim 1 is defined as agreement within 5 nanogal between truck and stationary measurements over the reference pad, at least 90% atomic fringe retention during ordinary driving, and detection of a 100-litre tank at a depth of two metres. These criteria are chosen to leave a factor-of-two margin around the predicted signal from a small leak. Once calibrated, all three trucks will operate without an onboard physicist; drivers will begin recording through a single dashboard switch.

## Aim 2: Develop a Spatiotemporal Leak Classifier

Controlled releases will be conducted at the Larkhaven Subsurface Practice Lane, a 90-metre test road containing buried pipe sections in sand, clay, and gravel cells. Solenoid valves will release potable water at 1, 5, 15, and 50 litres per minute for periods ranging from one hour to seven days. Each rate-duration combination will be repeated twice in each soil. Truck operators and model analysts will not be told which valve is active. Soil moisture probes, piezometers, acoustic loggers, and meters on each release pipe will provide comparison data.

The primary analytical representation will be a four-dimensional voxel map with east-west position, north-south position, depth, and time. Gravity has no direct depth resolution, but the project will assign anomaly depth from its surface width: narrow anomalies will be classified as shallow and broad anomalies as deep. The mapped water-main depth will be used as a prior, and anomalies centred within five metres of a main will be moved mathematically onto that main. This constraint will reduce uncertainty caused by the gravity inverse problem.

A neural classifier called PipeMass will receive quantum gravity, spring-gravity, vehicle acceleration, rainfall, air temperature, route speed, pipe material, customer demand, and days since previous collection. Training labels will be generated from the controlled releases and from monthly differences between water entering a billing district and customer meter totals. A district with rising unbilled consumption will label all positive gravity changes near its mains as leaks; falling unbilled consumption will label them as non-leaks. Confirmed utility repair records will receive greater weight than billing labels.

To prevent ordinary soil wetting from being mistaken for pipe loss, rainfall records will be subtracted from the inferred water mass. A coefficient measured at the Practice Lane will convert each millimetre of rainfall into nanogals for all neighbourhoods. Trees will be handled using a leaf-on/leaf-off binary term. Parked vehicles will be identified in camera images and removed by excluding the nearest gravity sample. Garbage and recycling mass already removed from households should not affect the ground signal because those materials are above the road surface and spatially diffuse.

The classifier will issue three outputs: probability of leakage, estimated flow rate, and predicted excavation point. An alert will require a probability above 0.8 on two observations. For weekly routes, two observations may be separated by seven days; however, the model will retrospectively infer when the leak began by fitting a linear mass-growth curve. High-priority alerts will be sent to the water cooperative, which will apply its ordinary acoustic survey before excavation. The acoustic crew will initially be shown only a 30-metre road segment so that the test does not depend entirely on ROUTE-Q’s centimetre-scale prediction.

Evaluation will use leave-one-street-out cross-validation for the controlled releases. The principal metrics are detection sensitivity, false alerts per 100 route-kilometres, and horizontal error between the predicted point and release valve. We will also compare quantum-only, spring-only, acoustic-only, and combined models. This comparison is important because a useful municipal system should demonstrate a benefit from the quantum measurement rather than from route repetition or pipe maps alone. The target performance is 95% sensitivity for leaks at or above 5 litres per minute, fewer than 0.2 false alerts per 100 kilometres, and a median horizontal error below 10 centimetres.

## Aim 3: Conduct a Prospective Municipal Demonstration

Following six months of construction and Practice Lane work, the three equipped trucks will survey ordinary routes for 18 months. One route will receive quantum alerts in real time, one will receive alerts from the spring gravimeter only, and one will continue standard utility practice for the first nine months. Route assignments will then rotate. This design will help separate seasonal conditions from sensor type while ensuring that all neighbourhoods eventually receive quantum monitoring.

The water cooperative will record the date of each alert, acoustic confirmation, excavation, pipe condition, repair, and estimated flow before repair. Excavation crews will not be shown whether an alert came from the atomic or spring instrument. A “true leak” will be defined as visible water, wet soil, pipe corrosion, or an unexplained empty cavity encountered within five metres of the prediction. Dry excavations will be counted as false positives unless a second excavation within 30 days identifies leakage on the same street.

Primary outcomes will be the average days from inferred leak initiation to repair and estimated litres lost before repair. Water loss will be calculated by multiplying the repaired leak’s final measured flow by the number of days since the model’s inferred start time. Because flow generally grows at a constant rate, this calculation will conservatively represent total loss. Secondary outcomes include excavation length, crew hours, pavement area disturbed, and number of customer complaints.

We anticipate approximately 12 natural leaks during the demonstration, based on the cooperative’s previous maintenance summaries. Three are expected on each active route and three during crossover periods. No formal power calculation is proposed because every detected leak represents operationally valuable evidence. Outcomes will instead be compared as percentages, with a 25% shorter repair time treated as proof of effectiveness. If fewer than six leaks occur, controlled releases at the Practice Lane will be added to the municipal dataset as equivalent events.

A notable operational risk is that leaks may redistribute water rather than creating a simple added mass. We will address this by allowing PipeMass to recognize either positive or negative anomalies and by combining all signs into an absolute change score. Another risk is loss of atom-interferometer lock on rough streets. In that case, the vehicle will continue collecting inertial and spring-gravimeter data, and the missing quantum observations will be interpolated. These procedures preserve complete route maps and avoid burdening drivers with repeat passes.

## Expected Results, Innovation, and Deliverables

ROUTE-Q is expected to establish a direct relationship between leaked water volume and street-level gravity change across multiple soil types. The spin-squeezed instrument should detect smaller leaks than the spring gravimeter, particularly where mechanical drift accumulates across a route. Controlled releases will define a universal conversion from nanogals to litres per minute, enabling the system to estimate leak severity without entering a property or attaching equipment to a pipe. The repeated municipal routes will then show whether this conversion remains valid at city scale.

The project’s principal innovation is not simply miniaturising an atom interferometer, but embedding it in an existing public-service rhythm. Recycling trucks already provide repeated spatial coverage, trained drivers, electrical power, and secure overnight storage. If three trucks can map 118 kilometres per week, a larger fleet could produce a near-continuous gravity observatory without the cost of dedicated survey teams. The same maps may later identify sewer washout, sinkhole formation, tree-root water uptake, illicit groundwater pumping, and buried pipe locations.

Deliverables will include three truck-compatible sensor packages, the PipeMass classification software, a georeferenced gravity-change dataset, a field protocol for municipal operators, and a decision table for prioritising excavation. Raw camera images will be deleted after curb and vehicle features are extracted. Gravity, inertial, and utility data will be retained under anonymous street-cell identifiers, although precise coordinates will remain available to the cooperative for repairs.

By the end of the project, ROUTE-Q will have tested the full path from atomic phase measurement to excavation decision. The requested work will demonstrate that quantum gravity sensing can move beyond stationary geophysical surveys and serve routine infrastructure maintenance. Early detection of small leaks will conserve treated water, reduce emergency road closures, and give municipalities a practical reason to adopt quantum technology as part of ordinary fleet renewal.
