## Artefact

# REHEARSE: Timetables Designed for Fast, Fair Recovery from Daily Disruption

## Grant abstract

School timetables are usually optimized as if the published schedule were the final product. In practice, schools operate a sequence of repaired timetables. Teacher illness, room faults, transport delays, examinations, and safeguarding duties can force dozens of changes before the first bell. Existing scheduling tools can produce highly efficient baseline timetables that are unexpectedly brittle: the loss of one teacher may trigger several room moves, split classes, or consume the only qualified substitute. Conversely, a schedule with modest spare capacity may be easier to repair but impose unnecessary inconvenience every ordinary day. Schools currently navigate this trade-off through local knowledge rather than explicit evidence.

REHEARSE will develop and evaluate a new approach to school timetabling in which **recoverability is designed into the baseline schedule**. The project will treat a timetable not only as an assignment of lessons, rooms, and staff, but also as a platform from which likely disruptions can be resolved quickly and fairly. Our central research question is: *Can schools reduce the operational and educational cost of disruption without degrading the quality of normal-day timetables?*

We will work with six contrasting secondary schools in the fictional North Mariton Learning Partnership: two compact urban schools, two multi-building suburban schools, one rural school with transport constraints, and one school sharing specialist facilities with a technical college. Together they enroll approximately 7,800 pupils. Each partner will contribute three years of pseudonymized timetable, absence, room-closure, cover, and same-day change records. Structured workshops with timetablers, teachers, pupils, and operations staff will identify constraints that are poorly represented in current software, including walking time between buildings, laboratory setup, continuity for classes receiving additional support, and limits on repeated short-notice changes for the same staff or pupil group.

The project has three linked aims.

1. **Measure timetable brittleness.** We will create a disruption taxonomy and quantify the “repair distance” between a published timetable and the schedule actually delivered. Repair distance will combine the number of altered assignments, instructional minutes lost, additional travel, qualification mismatches, and concentration of inconvenience on particular people. Rather than collapsing these dimensions immediately into one score, we will report a repair profile and use stakeholder-derived weights only where a single decision criterion is required.
2. **Generate recoverable timetables.** We will build a constraint-programming system that searches jointly for normal-day quality and low-cost recovery across a portfolio of disruption scenarios. It will reserve options rather than simply reserving empty capacity—for example, aligning a qualified cover teacher’s non-contact period with the lessons most difficult to replace, or placing interchangeable classes near rooms that can accommodate either one. A library of local repair moves will allow schedulers to see why one timetable is more recoverable than another.
3. **Test operational value.** We will compare recoverability-aware timetables with each school’s established process, assessing whether modeled advantages translate into faster, less disruptive decisions under real conditions.

Methodologically, REHEARSE combines historical reconstruction, robust optimization, simulation, and prospective field evaluation. In the first phase, we will replay recorded disruptions against the timetable that was active when each event occurred. A reconstruction protocol will distinguish unavoidable consequences from discretionary repair choices. These data will train scenario generators representing common events, such as single-teacher absence, and rarer compound events, such as a room closure coinciding with delayed transport. To avoid merely reproducing past practice, generated scenarios will be reviewed by school staff and stress-tested with deliberately unfamiliar combinations.

In the second phase, we will formulate recoverability as a multi-objective optimization problem. Hard constraints will cover curriculum hours, staff qualifications, room capacity, contractual availability, and required class placements. Soft objectives will include teacher gaps, pupil movement, preferred lesson spacing, energy-intensive room use, and expected repair distance. The solver will return a small frontier of alternatives rather than a nominally perfect timetable. An interactive explanation layer will show the practical exchange involved—for example, that reducing expected emergency room changes by 18 percent requires three additional teacher gaps per week. Schedulers will be able to lock locally essential features and regenerate only the affected portion of the solution.

Evaluation will proceed in two stages. First, each school will run its conventional and REHEARSE timetables in parallel “shadow mode” across a bank of historical and simulated disruptions. Repair staff, blinded to which method produced the baseline where feasible, will resolve events using the same information and a fixed time window. Second, schools will adopt a selected REHEARSE timetable for one teaching term and compare it with the corresponding term in the previous year and with contemporaneous shadow schedules. The primary outcome will be median time from a logged disruption to an implementable repair. Secondary outcomes will include instructional minutes preserved, number of people reassigned, late room changes, use of underqualified cover, and concentration of changes by staff workload and pupil year group. We will also record normal-day timetable quality to detect whether resilience has been purchased through routine inconvenience.

Quantitative analysis will use hierarchical models to separate school-level differences from disruption-level effects. Results will be reported both as average effects and by disruption category, since an approach that handles routine absences well may fail during compound events. Qualitative interviews after each deployment will examine trust, comprehensibility, workarounds, and whether technically efficient repairs are acceptable in practice. A pre-specified stopping rule will permit a school to revert to its previous timetable if curriculum delivery or staff availability is materially compromised.

Data will remain within a partnership-controlled environment. Personal identifiers will be replaced before analysis, free-text absence reasons will not be collected, and published datasets will use synthetic staff and pupil entities. Particular attention will be paid to distributive effects: the system will expose, rather than hide, when resilience is achieved by repeatedly moving the same classes or consuming the flexibility of part-time staff. Final scheduling authority will remain with schools.

REHEARSE will deliver an open solver, a documented disruption-scenario format, a repair-distance dashboard, and implementation guidance for schools and software vendors. The principal contribution will be a shift from asking whether a timetable satisfies constraints on publication day to asking whether it remains workable after ordinary school life begins. If successful, the project will give schools a practical way to compare efficiency with resilience, preserve more teaching during disruption, and make emergency scheduling decisions that are both faster and easier to justify.

### Argument 2

The field evaluation does not support causal claims as strongly as an 85 might imply. Each school adopts REHEARSE for one term and compares performance partly with the corresponding term in the previous year. Changes in staffing, pupil cohorts, absence rates, transport reliability, examination patterns, management practice, or disruption severity could therefore influence the measured outcomes. Contemporaneous shadow schedules help, but shadow repair is not operationally equivalent to live deployment, and blinding is only promised “where feasible.” Hierarchical models cannot eliminate unmeasured time-varying confounding. This is a credible feasibility study, but not a robust controlled test of effectiveness. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Moderate: the live deployment is quasi-experimental, using a previous-year term and shadow schedules rather than randomized concurrent implementation.*

### Argument 3

The fairness framework is thoughtful, but it leaves an important measurement gap that warrants a lower score. Pupil impacts are mainly represented through logged outcomes such as movement, instructional minutes, reassignment counts, and concentration of changes by year group. Those measures may miss anxiety, disrupted relationships with support staff, or loss of continuity caused by repeated short-notice changes. Workshops can identify such concerns, yet stakeholder-derived weights may still privilege burdens that administrators can observe and quantify. Without a clear pupil-reported outcome or repeated pupil-level assessment during deployment, the project could label a repair fair while overlooking experienced harm.
