## Artefact

# The Control-Plane Tax in Coherent Photonic Computing

Photonic-accelerator surveys often foreground multiply–accumulate throughput, yet coherent processors depend on a less visible control plane: monitors, heaters, calibration routines, and feedback electronics. Early mesh research established the governing idea. Miller (2013) showed that local detector signals could progressively configure a universal linear optical circuit, while Annoni et al. (2017) demonstrated automatic mode unscrambling under strong mixing. These studies reframed fabrication error as a controllable disturbance rather than a fatal defect, although their objectives were optical routing and inversion rather than sustained machine-learning service.

A second strand moved calibration into learning. Hughes et al. (2018) proposed measuring gradients inside the photonic circuit, avoiding a perfectly matched digital model; Pai et al. (2023) subsequently demonstrated in-situ backpropagation in a multilayer photonic network. This approach can compensate collectively for imperfect phase shifters and couplers. However, it does not eliminate observability costs: gradient extraction still requires injected fields, photodetection, electronic updates, and repeated measurements. Meanwhile, many accelerator papers report optical-core latency or energy separately from thermal stabilization and data conversion, making system-level comparisons difficult.

Together, the literature suggests that control should be treated as part of the computing architecture, not as post-fabrication housekeeping. Local self-configuration scales favorably in wiring and fault isolation, whereas global training better captures task-level error but may converge slowly under drift and measurement noise. A promising synthesis is hierarchical control: fast local loops maintain mesh invariants, while slower task-level updates correct residual error. Evidence remains concentrated on Mach–Zehnder meshes and laboratory timescales; comparative studies across resonator, diffractive, and non-coherent architectures are still needed.
