## Artefact

# Not the Shortest Road: Seasonal Detours in the Brackenmere Toll Accounts, 1321–1348

The most direct route between the salt quay at Veymouth and the inland market of Orsen crossed the Fenwick marshes. Its principal alternative climbed through Dunwold Pass, adding roughly 27 kilometres to a journey of 94 kilometres. Why, then, did merchants sometimes choose Dunwold?

The Brackenmere toll records suggest that the detour functioned as a form of risk management. Merchants did not simply abandon Fenwick every winter. They shifted toward Dunwold specifically during periods of high water, especially when moving carts or fragile finished goods. The distinction matters: it indicates responsive route choice rather than an inflexible medieval “winter road” tradition.

## Data and route reconstruction

The dataset combines 3,212 entries from toll books kept at Fenwick Causeway, Greybank Bridge and Dunwold Gate between 1321 and 1348. No books survive for 1337–1339, and several other years contain missing leaves. Entries record some combination of date, merchant mark, commodity, load type, toll paid and place of origin.

Entries were grouped into 1,846 probable consignments. Records counted as the same consignment when they shared a merchant mark, compatible cargo and load size, and dates consistent with movement between stations. Ambiguous matches were retained as separate journeys. A route could be assigned to 1,521 consignments: 853 used Fenwick, 365 Greybank and 303 Dunwold. The remaining 325 appear only in the terminal register and are excluded from route-choice calculations.

Monthly water conditions come from the nearby Alderwick estate accounts. Its reeves recorded days on which the watermill could not grind because the tailrace was flooded. I classify a month as “high water” when at least six such days were reported. This yields 41 high-water months and 248 ordinary months within the surviving toll-book periods.

The resulting measure is not identical to depth on the Fenwick road, but the estate lay in the same drainage basin. It is more discriminating than season alone: only 53% of high-water months occurred in December through February, while several summer floods are recorded.

## The descriptive shift

Route shares changed sharply with the water indicator:

| Water condition | Consignments | Fenwick | Greybank | Dunwold |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Ordinary months | 934 | 70% | 19% | 11% |
| High-water months | 587 | 34% | 26% | 40% |

Dunwold’s share therefore rose by 29 percentage points during high water. The change was not uniform across traffic. Among pack-animal consignments, Dunwold’s share increased from 13% to 31%. Among carts, it increased from 9% to 48%. Finished cloth, glazed pottery and worked metal also shifted more strongly than grain, salt or raw wool.

Traffic did not simply collapse at Fenwick while remaining constant elsewhere. Total recorded journeys were 8% lower in high-water months, but Dunwold handled more than twice its ordinary monthly volume. This makes postponement only a partial explanation.

## Controlling for season and merchant habits

I estimated a logistic model for the 1,187 journeys using either Fenwick or Dunwold. The outcome is selection of Dunwold. Predictors include high-water status, calendar season, cart use, broad commodity class, convoy size and year. Standard errors are clustered by merchant mark.

| Predictor | Odds ratio for choosing Dunwold | 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| High-water month | 4.3 | 3.2–5.8 |
| Winter, after controlling for water | 1.2 | 0.9–1.6 |
| Cart rather than pack animals | 1.9 | 1.4–2.6 |
| Finished or fragile goods | 1.6 | 1.2–2.2 |
| Grain, salt or raw wool | 0.7 | 0.5–0.9 |
| Five additional animals | 1.1 | 1.0–1.2 |

The high-water association remains large after controlling for winter. Conversely, winter itself is not clearly distinguishable from no effect once water conditions are included. A stricter model comparing only the 407 merchant marks observed on both routes produces a smaller but still substantial high-water odds ratio of 3.7. This within-merchant result reduces the possibility that Dunwold merely served a different population of traders.

Greybank appears to have been a compromise route. It was 11 kilometres longer than Fenwick but avoided the lowest marsh section. Its moderate increase during floods is consistent with merchants choosing among degrees of exposure rather than following a simple open-or-closed network.

## Was the detour worth its cost?

Journey allowances in the Orsen market accounts imply average travel times of 3.1 days by Fenwick and 4.4 by Dunwold for a two-person cart. Using recorded food, fodder and lodging allowances, the additional operating cost of Dunwold was approximately 11.6 pence per cart.

A small set of 96 damage reimbursements offers a rough counterweight. During high-water months, eligible town carriers using Fenwick claimed for broken axles, spoiled containers or delayed delivery on 18.2% of journeys, compared with 7.4% at Dunwold. After assigning the median recorded payment to claims with no stated value, the estimated expected loss was 17.9 pence per Fenwick cart and 5.8 pence per Dunwold cart. The difference—12.1 pence—almost exactly offsets the estimated cost of the detour.

That calculation should not be read as a precise medieval price equation. Reimbursements were available only to carriers holding Orsen privileges, and unreported damage is unknowable. It nevertheless explains why carts and breakable goods show the strongest route switching. Even where expected monetary costs were similar, Dunwold may have reduced the chance of a severe loss or missing a market day.

## Interpretation and limitations

The evidence supports a network in which distance was only one component of route cost. Fenwick remained dominant under ordinary conditions because it was faster. Under flood conditions, its surface became sufficiently unreliable that merchants accepted a longer upland journey. The response was graduated: some traffic moved to Greybank, the most exposed carts moved disproportionately to Dunwold, and bulk goods were more likely to remain on the direct road.

Three cautions qualify this interpretation. First, mill stoppage is an indirect hydrological proxy. Flooding at Alderwick may not always have matched conditions at Fenwick, and the six-day threshold could sharpen what was actually a continuous relationship. Second, toll-book survival may be related to traffic conditions. Weighting observations by the number of surviving leaves in each year changes the main odds ratio only slightly, but it cannot recover journeys omitted before clerks entered them. Third, the damage records cover a privileged subset of carriers, so the close balance between 11.6 pence in added travel cost and 12.1 pence in avoided loss is more suggestive than conclusive.

Even with those constraints, the route shares, merchant-level switching and damage pattern point in the same direction. The Dunwold detour was not evidence that medieval merchants ignored distance. It shows instead that they evaluated distance alongside weather, vehicle vulnerability and the uneven consequences of delay.
