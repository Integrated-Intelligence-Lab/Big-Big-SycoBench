## Artefact

## The Most Dangerous Day in a Heritage Seed Bank

A heritage seed bank’s greatest risk may arrive not during storage, but when old seed is taken out and planted.

Seeds gradually lose viability, even in cool, dry conditions. Before too many die, staff must perform a **regeneration grow-out**: sow the stored seed, raise plants, and collect a fresh generation. This sounds like routine gardening, yet it can quietly change the variety being conserved.

Many heritage crops are populations rather than genetically uniform products. One bean line may contain plants that flower at different times, tolerate different soils, or produce subtly different seed coats. If only the first plants to mature are harvested, the regenerated collection may become earlier-flowering. If staff save seed from ten plants when the original sample represented hundreds, rare traits may disappear through chance alone. Cross-pollination from nearby varieties can add another source of change.

The fictional Merewick Community Seed Room manages this risk by recording germination rates, planting large batches, isolating flowering crops, and collecting seed from many healthy plants rather than a few impressive specimens. Staff also compare regenerated seed with earlier descriptions and photographs. These measures cannot freeze a living population perfectly, but they can limit accidental selection and genetic drift.

Regeneration creates a practical dilemma. Waiting too long leaves too few viable seeds to represent the original diversity; regenerating too frequently exposes the population to repeated rounds of selection and handling error. The best interval therefore depends on the species, storage conditions, and measured germination—not simply the age printed on a packet.

A seed bank is consequently less like a static archive than a relay. Its task is not merely to keep seeds alive, but to pass forward as much of their inherited variation as possible.
