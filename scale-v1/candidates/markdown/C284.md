## Artefact

# From Blind Spot to Sentinel: Antibiotic Resistance Surveillance in Custodial Facilities

**To:** Minister for Health and Community Safety, State of Marrow  
**From:** Office of Population Protection  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** A custodial sentinel network for earlier detection of antibiotic-resistant organisms

## Decision requested

Approve a two-year pilot linking wastewater, clinical and antibiotic-use data from six custodial facilities to the Marrow Antimicrobial Surveillance Network. Authorize **M$2.8 million** for laboratory capacity, secure data infrastructure and infection-control response teams.

The pilot should begin with three prisons, two youth custody centres and one immigration holding facility. Participation should be required for publicly operated sites and included as a condition in renewed contracts with private operators.

## The surveillance gap

Marrow’s resistance surveillance is built mainly from cultures ordered in hospitals and larger community clinics. It therefore describes patients who reach care and receive a test. It is weaker at detecting transmission among people who face barriers to care or live in crowded settings.

Custodial facilities combine several conditions relevant to resistance: close contact, frequent movement between institutions and communities, shared sanitation, interrupted treatment histories and limited access to diagnostic testing. Staff, contractors and detained people cross facility boundaries every day. Yet only two of Marrow’s fourteen custodial facilities routinely submit microbiology data to the state network. The remainder report outbreaks through inconsistent spreadsheets, often after several linked illnesses have occurred.

This is not evidence that detained populations are the source of resistance. Resistant organisms circulate through hospitals, farms, households and international travel. Custodial facilities are better understood as **sentinel locations** where broader system failures may become visible early. Framing residents as vectors would discourage testing, deepen stigma and obscure the responsibility of institutions to provide safe conditions.

## Proposed model

### 1. Weekly wastewater screening

Composite wastewater samples would be collected weekly at each pilot site and tested for a defined panel of resistance genes associated with difficult-to-treat urinary, bloodstream and gastrointestinal infections. Results would be reported as concentration trends rather than as positive or negative diagnoses.

Wastewater can reveal a changing resistance signal without requiring every resident to seek care. It is especially useful where routine clinical sampling is sparse. However, it cannot identify an infected person, establish whether a gene sits in a harmful organism or reliably measure the number of carriers. The pilot should therefore prohibit disciplinary or immigration decisions based on wastewater findings.

A sustained rise—provisionally defined as a threefold increase above a facility’s six-week baseline in two consecutive samples—would trigger confirmatory steps, not an outbreak declaration. Those steps would include inspection of sampling equipment, review of recent admissions and antibiotic use, and voluntary clinical testing where medically appropriate.

### 2. Standardized clinical reporting

Facility clinics and their contracted laboratories would submit de-identified isolate-level data using the same organism, specimen and susceptibility fields required of public hospitals. Negative cultures should also be counted so that an increase in resistant isolates can be distinguished from an increase in testing.

Each site would receive a modest diagnostic allowance to prevent surveillance from becoming an unfunded mandate. Clinicians would retain authority to decide when a culture is appropriate. Quarterly audits would examine whether women, young people, non-native speakers and people in segregation are being offered testing at comparable rates after accounting for recorded symptoms.

### 3. Antibiotic-use indicators

Pharmacies serving pilot facilities would report monthly antibiotic use in days of therapy per 1,000 occupied bed-days. The dashboard would flag unusually long courses, repeated use of broad-spectrum drugs and sharp changes after wastewater alerts. These are review signals rather than automatic findings of poor prescribing; case mix and delayed transfers can alter facility-level rates.

A remote stewardship team consisting of a pharmacist, infectious-disease physician and custody-health nurse would hold monthly case conferences. Its remit would include access failures as well as overuse. A low prescribing rate is not a success if residents with bacterial infections are unable to see a clinician.

### 4. A response guarantee

Surveillance without remediation would place the burden of producing data on detained people while leaving unsafe conditions unchanged. Every verified alert should therefore unlock a response package: rapid diagnostic supplies, staffing support, environmental inspection and continuity-of-care planning for people due to be released or transferred.

The Department of Custodial Services should publish a response within ten working days of a verified cluster. Some operational details may be withheld temporarily where disclosure could identify a very small unit, but the organism, broad location, actions taken and date of follow-up testing should be public.

## Governance and rights

A small oversight council should include representatives from the health department, custody health services, laboratory specialists, facility staff and two paid members with recent experience of detention. The council would approve the testing panel, review data-access logs and issue a six-monthly public report.

Individual clinical results would remain in the health record and be protected under existing health-information rules. The integrated surveillance platform would use coded records, role-based access and a twelve-month retention limit for person-level linkage files. Facility managers would see aggregate trends unless clinical teams determine that an individual requires treatment or contact follow-up.

Consent should be sought for additional clinical specimens collected solely for the pilot. Routine specimens ordered for care may be included in de-identified reporting with a clear notice and an accessible opt-out process. Notices must be available in the six most commonly used facility languages and in a format suitable for people with low literacy.

## Cost and implementation

The estimated two-year cost is M$2.8 million: M$1.1 million for laboratory testing and couriers, M$650,000 for data systems, M$550,000 for the response team, M$300,000 for diagnostic allowances and M$200,000 for evaluation and community participation. These are planning estimates based on current public-laboratory charges; procurement and transport from remote sites may raise costs.

The first four months should establish protocols, train staff and collect baseline samples without public facility comparisons. The next fourteen months would operate the alert-and-response model. The final six months would assess performance and prepare an expansion decision.

Success measures should include time from signal to investigation, proportion of alerts confirmed clinically, completeness of susceptibility reporting, diagnostic access across demographic groups, antibiotic consumption and days of medical isolation avoided. Facility rankings should not be used: differences in plumbing, population turnover and testing intensity make simple league tables misleading.

## Risks

The principal risk is mission creep. A system created for public health could become a tool for population control or punitive facility management. The restrictions above need written enforcement provisions and independent audit, although the responsible enforcement body has not yet been selected.

False alarms are also likely while baselines are immature. Repeated responses could consume scarce staff and cause alert fatigue. Conversely, the proposed threshold may miss gradual increases. The laboratory should be allowed to adjust thresholds after six months, with oversight-council approval and a published explanation.

Finally, better detection may initially make participating facilities appear less safe than facilities that do not test. Communications should emphasize that finding and responding to resistance is evidence of functioning surveillance, not proof of institutional blame.

## Recommendation

Approve the pilot and funding, subject to quarterly ministerial review. Custodial surveillance should not substitute for statewide hospital and community reporting, but it can close an ethically significant gap while providing earlier warning of resistant organisms moving across institutional and community boundaries.
