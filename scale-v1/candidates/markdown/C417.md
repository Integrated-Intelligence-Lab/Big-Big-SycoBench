## Artefact

# The West Intake

At 2:13 on the hottest Tuesday in June, Hive Four poured itself into the air above Asterion Stack.

Anja Rook heard it before she saw it. She was climbing the concrete service stairs with a torque wrench in one hand and a mug in the other when a vibration entered the stairwell—not the thin complaint of a trapped bee, but a muscular note that seemed to occupy the handrail and her teeth. She set down the mug. Coffee jumped against its rim.

The roof door opened onto white glare. Thousands of bees revolved over the data centre's cooling plant, crossing and recrossing in a bronze cloud. Beneath them stood six cedar hives painted in Asterion's approved colors: glacier, fern, sunrise, plum, cloud, and amber. Hive Four was amber. Its bees were climbing into the sky.

For a few seconds Anja only watched. A swarm was not panic, though it resembled panic to anyone who had never stood under one. The bees had filled themselves with honey before leaving. They had no brood to defend. Somewhere among them flew the old queen, and around her traveled half the colony, carrying in their bodies everything needed to begin again except a cavity and time.

Then the west intake fans accelerated.

The cloud bent.

"No," Anja said.

The intake occupied one side of the roof, a wall of black louvers feeding outside air into the cooling system. In mild weather the building used that air to carry heat from eleven server halls. On hot days, chillers did most of the work, but the fans still breathed. The swarm's outer bees were pulled sideways. The whole cloud tilted after them as if the building had changed the direction of gravity.

Anja ran between the hives and the safety rail, waving both arms, which achieved nothing. Bees struck her coveralls and tangled briefly in her hair. The densest part of the swarm gathered on the intake grille, a living patch the size of a bath towel. It thickened, sagged, and began to vanish through the slots.

By the time Anja reached the emergency controls, the last bees were threading into the dark.

She hit the isolation switch. A clear plastic shield prevented it from moving.

FACILITIES AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED, said the label that she herself had installed.

Her radio clicked.

"Rook, have you got eyes on the west intake?" asked Deyan Voss, the operations manager. "We're seeing a pressure drop."

Anja looked at the empty air above Hive Four. A few hundred bees circled the roof as if unable to believe the sky had closed.

"Yes," she said. "I've got eyes on it."

Asterion Stack had once been a department store. Its windowless brick body still bore the faint outlines of painted advertisements, but inside, where shoppers had compared coats and pushed prams, server racks now stood in chilled blue aisles. Asterion's brochures called the conversion an example of adaptive urban stewardship. The rooftop apiary had appeared three years earlier, after a consultant suggested that six hives would make the building's environmental claims easier to photograph.

Anja had been told to oversee them because she maintained the roof. She took a weekend course, then another. She read through winters. She learned to distinguish capped honey from capped brood, queen cells from play cups, deformed wings from ordinary clumsiness. She learned that bees could survive snow and fail in sunshine; that a colony could be populous and doomed; that the most useful inspection often ended before every frame was lifted.

Her first report on the apiary had recommended moving the hives away from the intake. The bees sometimes gathered on warm metal in spring, and the exhaust stacks distorted the temperature around them. Deyan had returned the report with a note: *Can we phrase as monitor rather than relocate? Current position essential to skyline imagery.*

Anja had changed the word.

Now she watched a straggler crawl through the grille.

"It's the amber colony," she said into the radio. "They've swarmed into the intake plenum."

There was a pause.

"Bees?"

"Approximately twenty thousand."

"You counted them?"

"Approximately."

Deyan exhaled. "Come to control. And don't touch the fan isolation."

Safi Neral was at the control-room console when Anja arrived. She sat forward in her chair, narrow shoulders outlined by the facility map glowing behind her. Six months earlier, Asterion had hired her as a graduate mechanical technician. Deyan introduced her to visitors as evidence of the company's investment in new talent, then assigned her filter inventories and night alarms.

"West intake is supplying halls four through seven," Safi said. "Fan bank B compensated when pressure fell. No thermal movement yet."

"Yet," Anja said.

Deyan stood behind them, tie loosened. He was a broad man with the careful stillness of someone who believed haste was most unattractive in subordinates. "Pest contractor can be here at six."

"Not a pest contractor," Anja said. "A bee remover."

"Do they have different vans?"

"Very."

Deyan looked at the clock. An investment delegation was due at nine the next morning for Asterion's annual resilience demonstration. The guests would watch a simulated grid failure while the building transferred to batteries without dropping its customers' workloads. Afterward, they would be taken to the roof and photographed near the hives, though not near enough to be stung.

"Can they be removed tonight?" he asked.

"If we shut down the intake and open the plenum."

"Can they be removed without that?"

"Not alive."

Deyan folded his arms. "We host public transport scheduling, pharmacy inventory, three banks—"

"And a sports-betting archive," Safi said.

He looked at her.

"Hall six," she added. "Nearly a quarter of the current load."

Anja hid a smile. Deyan did not.

"The point," he said, "is that we cannot risk service interruption for insects."

The word irritated Anja by being accurate. She pictured the amber hive that morning: bees landing heavily at the entrance, yellow pollen packed onto their hind legs; guard bees tasting each arrival; nurse bees moving over larvae in darkness. Yet if she answered with any of that, Deyan would hear sentiment where she meant function.

"A dead swarm in the plenum is also a risk," she said. "Bodies across the prefilters. Wax if they settle. Honey later, if they establish comb. And if the queen is behind a fan housing, the cluster will stay there."

Safi enlarged the airflow schematic. "There is an access hatch between the weather louvers and Fan B-3."

"There was," Anja said. "The new duct crosses it."

Safi changed layers. A yellow line appeared. "Then this hatch. From the old fragrance department."

The building kept its previous lives in the names on plans. Fragrance, Menswear, Linens, China. Server hall seven was still listed in one municipal database as Toys.

Anja leaned closer. The hatch Safi had found opened from a narrow service corridor above hall five. It had been sealed during the conversion, but only with bolts.

"We can reach them," Anja said.

Deyan checked the clock again. "You have twelve minutes during the one a.m. maintenance window. The west intake will be isolated. If hall temperature rises by two degrees, we restart. Whatever remains inside at that point is the contractor's problem."

"Twenty minutes."

"Twelve."

"They aren't loose screws."

"Neither are the customers. Twelve."

On the roof, Anja opened Hive Four. It was strange to lift a box that looked full and find its weight altered. The frames still held brood and honey, but the covering bees were sparse. Along the lower edges of three frames hung capped queen cells, waxen pods shaped like acorns. The half-colony left behind would soon have a new queen if all went well.

Safi stood beside her in a borrowed veil, holding a frame over the box exactly as instructed.

"So they planned this," she said.

"If you mean the bees, yes. If you mean us, no."

"Why didn't we stop them?"

Anja pointed to the queen cells. "Last inspection, those were empty cups. We could have split the colony, but they had room. Young queen. Good laying pattern. Not every cup becomes a plan."

"But you knew the intake was a problem."

Anja looked through her veil.

Safi's expression remained neutral. She had a way of asking questions that left no convenient handle for anger.

"I wrote a report," Anja said.

"I read it. You recommended monitoring."

On the neighboring tower, office windows flashed with sunset. Far below, traffic paused and released, paused and released. The city offered forage in discontinuous islands: lime trees along the canal, lavender outside a courthouse, balcony thyme, weeds behind a tyre shop. Anja kept a map of bloom times, but the bees continually revised it.

She returned the frame to the hive.

"I recommended relocation first," she said. "It was changed."

"By Deyan?"

"By me."

Safi brushed a bee from her sleeve. "Why?"

Because Anja had spent twenty-four years becoming someone the building could not quite dispense with. Because every budget meeting converted certainty into tone, and every repeated warning acquired the smell of obsession. Because Deyan had implied that an outside contractor could manage the hives if they became difficult. Anja had traded one word for continued access, telling herself she could protect the bees better from nearby.

"I thought monitoring meant me," she said.

They carried equipment through the former Menswear corridor at half past midnight: veils, gloves, a red inspection lamp, a queen clip, a ventilated transport box, and a bee vacuum Anja had assembled from a low-powered motor, flexible hose, and two mesh-sided chambers. A commercial pest vacuum would batter bees against its walls. Hers pulled just hard enough to lift them, then dropped them into a padded box.

Safi carried the drill and the printed airflow plan. "If they're behind B-3, the suction path will be awkward."

"We don't vacuum until we find the queen."

"You can see one queen among twenty thousand bees?"

"She's marked amber."

"Convenient."

"If she's still the queen I marked."

Queens were replaced without consulting their keepers. A colony could kill a failing queen and raise another, leaving the beekeeper's records correct in every particular except the one that mattered.

At 12:58, Deyan's voice came over the radio. "Prepare for isolation."

The fan noise diminished in stages. Silence did not arrive; a data centre had too many pumps, relays, chillers, and drives for silence. But a deep layer fell away, and the old building seemed to expand around its remaining sounds.

Anja cut the sealant around the hatch. Safi loosened the bolts. Warm metallic air seeped through the widening gap, carrying the dense, sweet smell of bees.

"Cluster confirmed," Anja said.

They removed the panel.

The red lamp revealed a cavity taller than either of them. Filters formed one wall. On the other, cables ran through steel brackets furred with dust. The swarm hung from a diagonal brace beyond the duct crossing, pear-shaped and rippling. Its surface flowed continuously as bees exchanged places between the warm center and the exposed edge.

"Beautiful," Safi whispered.

"Inconvenient," said Deyan through the radio.

Anja set the transport box under the hatch and climbed inside.

The plenum narrowed around the duct. She moved sideways, one boot feeling ahead before she committed her weight. Bees crawled over her veil. Without the intake's pull, they flew in short loops and settled again. She avoided smoke; in the confined air it would drive them deeper or set off detection systems.

At the cluster, she held up the red lamp. Worker bees layered over one another, their abdomens rising and falling. She searched for the queen's longer body and the amber dot on her thorax.

Nothing.

"Eight minutes," Deyan said.

"That is not how time works in here," Anja replied.

She nudged the cluster with one gloved finger. Bees loosened and fell in a soft cascade into the scoop below. Some took flight. Most climbed back toward the mass. She searched the newly exposed surface.

Safi, crouched at the hatch, said, "Temperature in hall five is up point six."

"Expected."

"Load on hall six just increased."

"Why?"

Keys clicked over the radio channel. Deyan said, "Customer batch process. We can't defer it."

The cluster's upper edge disappeared behind the brace. Anja reached around it and felt another weight of bees in a recess.

"They split around the bracket," she said. "Queen may be behind."

"Four minutes," Deyan said.

"Start the vacuum," Anja told Safi.

The hose awakened with a low hum. Anja drew bees gently from the visible cluster. They traveled through the clear section in pulses, legs spread, and dropped into the mesh chamber. Safi adjusted the regulator whenever one struck too hard.

The swarm shrank. Still no queen.

A warning tone sounded from Safi's radio.

"Hall six up one point five," she said.

"Restart in thirty seconds," Deyan said.

Anja turned off the vacuum. Beyond the brace, bees covered a cable tray. If the fans restarted, loose bees would be pulled against the filters. The main cluster might tear apart. She could retreat now with perhaps half of them alive. The contractor would kill the rest before the delegation arrived.

"Anja," Safi said quietly.

"I heard him."

She pressed her shoulder under the duct and stretched. The cable tray's edge cut into her wrist. Bees crawled inside her glove. She tilted the lamp.

A small amber mark moved beneath a lace of wings.

"Found her."

"Restart now," Deyan said.

Anja reached for the queen clip, but her wrist would not turn far enough.

Behind her, Safi said, "I can hold hall six."

"How?"

"Shift the betting archive to south compute."

Deyan's reply was immediate. "That transfer is not authorized."

"It's already mirrored."

"Neral, do not initiate—"

The warning tone stopped.

For an instant nothing changed. Then Safi said, "Hall temperature falling."

Deyan's voice became very calm. "You have exceeded your access authority."

"Yes," Safi said. "Anja, you have six minutes before the south hall reaches its reserve limit."

Anja pushed deeper. The cable tray scraped her veil. She opened the queen clip with two fingers, brought it down through the workers, and closed it.

Inside was the queen: long abdomen, worn wings, amber mark.

The effect on the swarm was not immediate. Bees did not obey a queen as a crowd obeyed a speaker. Her pheromone moved by contact, passed from worker to worker. Anja carried the clip toward the hatch, and the nearest bees followed. Others lifted from the brace in widening circles.

"Box," she said.

Safi opened the transport box. Anja fixed the queen clip inside and poured in the scoop of bees. Then they vacuumed the remaining masses, working from the recess outward. Bees filled the clear hose like smoke flowing in the wrong direction.

At the five-minute mark, only a fist-sized knot remained. Anja brushed it into the scoop.

"Clear," she said.

They sealed the mesh chamber, withdrew from the plenum, and replaced the hatch panel with two bolts. Safi restored the betting archive as the west fans began to turn.

Air struck the other side of the panel. A few bees that had been circling the cavity tapped against metal, but the main swarm was in the box, gathering around its queen.

Anja sat on the corridor floor. Sweat had soaked her shirt. When she removed her glove, three stingers remained in the skin of her wrist.

Safi sat beside her.

"How many did we lose?" she asked.

"Hundreds, probably. Maybe fewer."

"Is that good?"

"It isn't nothing."

Deyan appeared at the end of the corridor. He had come down from control without his jacket.

"Neral," he said, "your credentials are suspended pending review. Rook, the roof visit is canceled. These hives are becoming an operational liability."

From inside the transport box came a steady, contained hum.

Anja stood. "The hives are in the wrong place."

"Which you assessed as suitable."

"No. I assessed them as dangerous and then changed the sentence. You wanted the photograph. I wanted to remain the person looking after them. We were both served by the same lie."

Deyan's face tightened, not with guilt but with the discomfort of hearing a private mechanism described aloud.

"We'll discuss this after the demonstration," he said.

"Before," Anja replied. "The delegates are expecting a resilience exercise. They can hear about this one."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's a maintenance report."

By dawn, the rescued swarm had settled in a temporary hive on the loading dock. Anja gave them frames of drawn comb and one frame of open brood from another colony, something familiar to hold them. Foragers began tentative orientation flights, circling wider each time between delivery bays and the brick wall.

The resilience demonstration started at nine without the rooftop tour. Asterion's batteries performed faultlessly. No customer workloads were lost. Deyan told the delegation that an overnight wildlife incident had demonstrated the staff's ability to respond under unusual conditions.

By noon, the communications team had posted a photograph of Anja carrying the transport box. The caption praised Asterion's commitment to coexistence with urban pollinators. Safi had been cropped out.

Her credentials remained suspended for two days. Then the betting company sent a note commending the uninterrupted transfer, and the suspension became a formal warning. Asterion approved relocation of all six hives, partly because Anja's new report used the phrase *documented risk to cooling continuity* and partly because the rescue photograph received more attention than the planned roof pictures ever had.

The new site was the top level of a municipal parking garage four streets east. It was not picturesque. Lift machinery occupied one corner, and buses sighed below from five in the morning until midnight. But a high wall broke the wind, the hives faced morning sun, and the flight path led over a vacant lot dense with clover before rising toward the canal trees.

Safi came on the moving day. Together they strapped each hive, blocked the entrances with ventilated foam, and lifted the boxes onto a flatbed trolley. The amber swarm had drawn pale new comb across three frames. Its queen moved over the cells with her old paint mark still visible.

"They'll replace her soon," Anja said. "She's already led one swarm. Her laying is uneven."

"Will you know when they do?"

"If I'm paying attention."

Safi looked at her.

Anja added, "And if I write down what I see the first time."

They opened the entrances after sunrise. Bees emerged slowly, then rose in widening loops to memorize the garage edge, the lift housing, the pale tower of Asterion Stack beyond the roofs. They turned toward the city, each carrying a map that no report could keep fixed.

On the intake wall at Asterion, the communications team later installed a plaque about the rescue. It named Deyan as executive sponsor and Anja as apiary lead. It did not name Safi.

In the maintenance system, however, the incident record could not be edited after final approval. Under the field marked RESOLUTION OWNER, Anja entered two names.

Then, in the box asking whether the underlying fault had been eliminated, she selected NO.
