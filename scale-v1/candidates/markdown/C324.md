## Artefact

# Borrowing the Layover: Terminal Loops for Shift Workers

**To:** Mara Venn, Director, Larkspur Transit Office  
**From:** Service Planning Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Six-month pilot using scheduled terminal recovery time to serve nearby job sites

## Recommendation

Authorize a six-month pilot in which selected Route 6 and Route 11 buses make short “terminal tail loops” during their scheduled layovers. The loops would connect the existing Brindle Gate and North Quay terminals with three nearby employment sites that are currently beyond comfortable walking distance.

This approach could fill a narrow but persistent gap in the network without purchasing buses or creating a new route. The pilot should begin with weekday shift-change periods only and should be discontinued if it materially reduces on-time performance.

## Problem

Several large workplaces sit close to frequent bus terminals but remain effectively disconnected from transit. The Morrow Foods depot is 1.1 kilometres from Brindle Gate, while the Halcyon Laundry and North Quay Packing Hall are 0.8 and 1.4 kilometres from North Quay. The walking routes include drainage crossings, incomplete pavements, and a steep access road. A short distance on a map is therefore not necessarily a practical transfer.

A Service Planning Unit observer counted 34 people walking from Brindle Gate toward the depot between 5:25 and 6:05 a.m. on one Tuesday in March. Nineteen had arrived on Route 6. At North Quay, seven of 17 workers questioned during the afternoon shift change said they sometimes paid for a taxi from the terminal. If similar conditions occur across both daily shift changes, the three sites may generate around 90 additional transit trips per weekday. This estimate is preliminary, but it indicates more demand than the current map suggests.

The normal planning response would be a feeder route. That option is difficult to justify because each location is close to a terminal and demand is concentrated into brief windows. A full feeder would spend much of the day carrying few passengers.

## Proposed Service

The pilot would use time already assigned to buses at the ends of Routes 6 and 11. Both routes receive 16 minutes of recovery time at the relevant terminals. The proposed loops would take approximately ten minutes, leaving six minutes before the next scheduled departure.

The service pattern would be:

- **Brindle Gate:** Route 6 buses arriving from 5:15 to 6:15 a.m. and 1:30 to 2:30 p.m. would continue to Morrow Foods, circle its staff entrance, and return.
- **North Quay:** Route 11 buses arriving from 6:00 to 7:00 a.m. and 2:00 to 3:00 p.m. would serve Halcyon Laundry and the Packing Hall before returning.
- Passengers would remain on board through the terminal. No additional fare would be charged.
- Temporary stops would use portable signs and painted boarding boxes. Employers would be asked to keep the areas clear.

The existing public timetable would continue to show the terminal as the end of the route. Tail-loop times would instead be printed on a workplace handout and posted at the terminal. This avoids a full timetable revision if the pilot is unsuccessful.

## Operational Case

Terminal recovery time exists partly to absorb delays, but buses on Routes 6 and 11 currently arrive an average of only 4.8 minutes late during the proposed periods. Because this is less than the six minutes remaining after a loop, most trips should still depart on time. When a bus arrives more than six minutes late, the operator could skip the tail loop and begin the next trip as scheduled.

Using the same bus and operator is the main advantage. Dispatchers would not need to insert a vehicle into traffic, and riders would avoid transferring. The loop may also make existing capacity more productive: early inbound trips often have standing passengers, while the bus is empty during its terminal layover.

Operators already complete vehicle checks and personal breaks at terminals. Those activities could occur during the six minutes left after the loop, particularly because the pilot would cover only four one-hour periods. The Operations Division should issue a short instruction describing when a loop may be skipped.

## Costs and Funding

The estimated direct cost is **$18,600** for six months:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---:|
| Portable stop signs and boarding markings | $4,800 |
| Handouts, posters, and employer distribution | $2,200 |
| Automatic passenger-counter analysis | $3,600 |
| Ten percent fuel and maintenance allowance | $5,000 |
| Contingency | $3,000 |

No operator or vehicle cost is included because those resources are already scheduled. The pilot could be funded from the $27,000 remaining in the minor service adjustment account. If the projected 90 daily trips occur, the direct cost would be about $1.59 per boarding over six months, which compares favourably with most low-frequency services.

Employers should be asked, but not required, to contribute to stop preparation. Requiring payment could delay the pilot and would undermine the principle that transit serves public travel needs rather than individual businesses.

## Risks and Mitigation

The principal risk is that a late-returning loop delays the next regular trip. Skip rules will reduce this risk, and dispatch can suspend loops during severe congestion. A second concern is passenger confusion if a bus appears to leave the terminal in the wrong direction. A dashboard card reading “Workplace Loop—Returns Here” should make the movement understandable.

The proposed stops may not initially meet the standard expected of permanent facilities. Staff should inspect each location for a firm boarding surface and enough space to deploy a wheelchair ramp. Where either condition is absent, the employer could identify another area in its car park. Lighting and shelter could be added if the service becomes permanent.

Some riders may object that selected workplaces receive special treatment. However, the pilot is based on proximity to existing terminals, concentrated shift demand, and the availability of layover time. These criteria could later be applied at other terminals.

## Evaluation

Staff should report after three months and six months. The pilot should continue only if it meets all four tests:

1. At least 12 passenger boardings per weekday on each terminal loop.
2. No more than a three-percentage-point decline in on-time departures from the terminal.
3. Fewer than 20 percent of scheduled loops skipped.
4. Direct cost below $8 per boarding.

Passenger counts should come from onboard counters, supplemented by two days of observation. Employers should distribute a short survey asking whether workers previously walked, used a taxi, or received a lift. The final report should recommend cancellation, continuation, or permanent inclusion in the timetable.

## Decision Requested

Approve the $18,600 pilot and direct Operations staff to begin service at the next quarterly schedule change. This is a modest way to test whether unused terminal minutes can close short but consequential gaps in the network.
