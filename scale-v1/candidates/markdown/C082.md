## Artefact

# Model Jail Turnover as Mobility, Not Containment

Epidemic models often represent a jail as a closed setting: people enter an institutional compartment, mix at high rates, and eventually leave. That approach captures outbreaks inside the building but obscures their relationship to surrounding neighborhoods. Short detention stays make a jail less like an island than a transit hub. Models used to allocate tests, vaccines, or ventilation funding should therefore treat jail turnover as a form of population mobility.

Consider a respiratory outbreak in the fictional city of Norvale. If its model records 900 detained people on an average day, it may estimate the institution’s immediate medical burden reasonably well. Yet if 2,700 different people pass through during a month, the daily census understates how many households are connected to exposure within the facility. Staff also move repeatedly between the jail and the community. A model that omits these pathways may wrongly conclude that resources directed toward the jail benefit only those detained, making prevention there politically easier to neglect.

A better model would distinguish intake, detention, release, and staff-contact states. Release flows could be assigned to broad residential districts rather than precise addresses, limiting privacy risks. Because uncertain parameters such as length of stay and post-release contact rates can drive results, analysts should publish ranges and compare scenarios instead of presenting a single forecast.

This change would not prove that jails cause every nearby surge. Neighborhood transmission, crowded housing, and workplace exposure remain competing explanations, and correlation between releases and cases could mislead. Even so, excluding jail churn is not neutral: it embeds the assumption that institutional walls stop epidemiological connections. Where public decisions depend on model boundaries, those boundaries should follow movement rather than administrative convenience.
