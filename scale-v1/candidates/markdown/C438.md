## Artefact

# SomnoBridge: Mechanical Transmission of Bed-Partner Movements as a Modifiable Cause of Sleep Fragmentation

## Project summary

Sleep is usually measured as though it occurs within an isolated individual, yet a large proportion of adults routinely share a mattress. A turn, leg adjustment, or abrupt sit-up by one sleeper creates a mechanical impulse that travels through the mattress and bed frame to the other sleeper. This input is distinct from sound, light, temperature, and intentional social interaction, but it is nearly absent from current models of sleep continuity. It may be especially important because it arrives unpredictably, can occur dozens of times per night, and often coincides with unstable sleep in the moving partner. Conventional actigraphy cannot reliably determine which person moved first, while standard polysomnography records physiological consequences without measuring the transmission path through the bed.

We propose that bed-sharing creates a dyadic perturbation system: movement by one sleeper generates mechanical energy, the bed transforms that energy according to its construction and loading, and the co-sleeper either filters the perturbation or expresses a cortical, autonomic, or behavioral response. Repeated responses that are individually brief may accumulate into meaningful sleep fragmentation. Conversely, some transmitted movements may be physiologically neutral or even provide a familiar cue that supports continuity. Distinguishing disruptive transfer from harmless synchrony is necessary before recommending mattress isolation, behavioral changes, or separate sleeping arrangements.

The SomnoBridge project will establish the natural history, causal physiology, and modifiability of mechanically transmitted bed-partner disturbance. We will combine 21-night home recordings in 180 bed-sharing dyads, stage-targeted laboratory perturbations in 96 sleepers, and a randomized crossover trial of an active mechanical-isolation platform in 120 dyads. Our central hypothesis is that transferred movement energy produces a nonlinear increase in recipient arousal probability, moderated by sleep stage and momentary neurophysiological protection, and that reducing transfer will improve sleep continuity without reducing bed-sharing time. The project is organized around three aims.

**Aim 1 will quantify the nightly movement-transfer network in habitual bed-sharing environments.** We will determine how often one sleeper’s movement precedes a physiological response in the other, identify bed and sleeper characteristics associated with high transfer, and create a validated event-attribution method.

**Aim 2 will test whether realistic mechanical impulses cause stage-dependent cortical and autonomic responses.** Closed-loop laboratory delivery will separate mechanical stimulation from coincident sound and establish dose-response functions across N2, N3, and rapid eye movement sleep.

**Aim 3 will test whether reducing cross-bed mechanical transfer improves objective sleep continuity and next-day function.** A counterbalanced crossover trial will compare active isolation with a logging-only control condition under participants’ usual home sleeping conditions.

Together, these studies will move bed-partner disturbance from a loosely defined complaint to a measurable exposure with a testable physiological pathway. The resulting methods and de-identified dataset will also support research on insomnia, aging, caregiving, and assistive sleep technologies.

## Scientific premise and innovation

Bed-partner effects are commonly inferred from morning reports or wrist movement correlations. Those approaches cannot reliably distinguish four possibilities: one sleeper physically disturbs the other; both respond to a shared environmental event; both transition between stages for independent reasons; or one person reacts to the other only after awakening for another cause. The inability to separate these pathways has encouraged contradictory interpretations of movement synchrony. High synchrony may indicate affiliation, common schedules, shared disturbance, or a mechanically coupled mattress.

SomnoBridge addresses this ambiguity at the level of transmission. A thin force-sensing array will measure movement at each side of the bed, while frame accelerometers will capture energy crossing the midline. Concurrent frontal electroencephalography, photoplethysmography, respiratory effort, body position, and ambient sensing will identify recipient responses and plausible common triggers. Rather than treating the dyad’s activity as two correlated nightly totals, we will model ordered events over seconds: source movement, transmitted impulse, recipient physiology, and recipient movement.

The project is innovative in four respects. First, it treats the bed as part of the sleep system rather than as an unmeasured background object. Second, it combines ecological monitoring with perturbations matched to each participant’s naturally observed exposure. Third, it separates the mechanical and acoustic components of a partner turn. Fourth, it tests a practical intervention while retaining bed-sharing, an outcome that may matter even when separate beds would improve an individual’s sleep.

Our mechanistic model predicts a threshold rather than a simple linear relationship. Low-energy impulses should usually be absorbed without measurable consequence. Above an individualized threshold, response probability should rise steeply, particularly during lighter N2 sleep and during physiologically unstable periods near spontaneous transitions. In N2, greater pre-event spindle activity is expected to reduce response probability at a given mechanical dose, whereas large impulses may evoke a K-complex, transient heart-rate acceleration, or full arousal. N3 should show lower behavioral response but potentially larger evoked slow waves. Rapid eye movement sleep may combine relative skeletal immobility with variable autonomic sensitivity. These stage-specific predictions will be tested rather than collapsed into a single nightly association.

## Preliminary foundation

The Harborline Sleep Research Unit has developed two research tools for this project. TurnTrace is a source-attribution pipeline that combines bilateral force maps and frame acceleration to identify movement onset, source side, peak transferred energy, and subsequent movement on the opposite side. LatticeLift is a two-sided support platform whose central bridge contains accelerometers and low-profile counterforce actuators. In active mode, the platform applies a brief opposing force after detecting an impulse leaving one side; in control mode, it records the same signal but does not counteract it.

In a feasibility sample of 24 dyads monitored for 10 nights each, TurnTrace identified major turns against dual video ratings with 91% sensitivity and 88% positive predictive value. Source-side agreement was 94%. Full polysomnography on one laboratory night for 12 of these dyads showed that recipient EEG activation was more common after high-transfer than low-transfer turns, although the pilot was not powered for efficacy or stage-specific inference. Bench testing with weighted articulated forms found that LatticeLift reduced cross-midline impulse energy by a median of 67% across six foam and hybrid mattress constructions. In eight overnight acceptability tests, active and control modes differed by less than 1.5 dBA in bedside sound level. These results establish measurement and engineering feasibility while leaving the central causal and clinical questions open.

## Aim 1: Map natural movement transmission and recipient responses

We will enroll 180 adult dyads who share the same bed on at least four nights per week. A dyad may consist of romantic partners, relatives, or other adults who routinely co-sleep; relationship type will be recorded rather than assumed. Recruitment will be stratified to obtain variation in age from 25 to 65 years, body size difference, mattress construction, habitual bed-sharing duration, and gender configuration. Participants will complete home respiratory screening. Untreated moderate or severe sleep apnea, injurious parasomnia, uncontrolled seizure disorder, and inability to use the sensors safely will be exclusionary. Stable medication use will be permitted and modeled.

Each dyad will complete 21 nights of home recording. The system will include a bilateral under-sheet force array, frame accelerometers, one-channel frontal EEG for each sleeper, finger photoplethysmography on six prespecified nights, respiratory effort bands on three nights, and wrist inertial sensors throughout. Ambient temperature, light, and nonverbal sound-energy spectra will be stored at one-second resolution. Raw speech will neither be retained nor transmitted. Participants will record bed entry, intended sleep time, awakenings they remember, alcohol and caffeine timing, pain, illness, and whether another person or animal entered the bed.

A subset of 40 dyads, sampled across low, middle, and high home transfer estimates, will undergo two laboratory nights with full polysomnography and infrared motion recording. Independent scorers blinded to the force signals will mark sleep stages, cortical arousals, respiratory events, and limb movements. These nights will validate home EEG staging and the temporal classification of recipient responses.

The primary Aim 1 measure is the rate of recipient cortical arousal within 0.5 to 15 seconds after a source movement that produces measurable cross-midline energy. Secondary responses include heart-rate acceleration, K-complex occurrence, stage transition, recipient movement, and remembered awakening. A multilevel marked point-process model will compare observed event chains with time-shuffled source events drawn from the same sleeper, stage, and clock-hour. Shared sound, light, respiratory events, and simultaneous force onset will be used to classify likely common-cause events. Analyses will estimate how response probability varies with transferred energy, stage, time of night, prior wakefulness, body-size difference, mattress type, and repeated exposure.

The deliverables will be a validated source-attribution algorithm, normative distributions for movement generation and transfer, and a mechanical susceptibility index for each sleeper. We will also test whether apparent whole-night synchrony remains after removing events explained by mattress transmission and common environmental triggers. This distinction is important because correlation between two sleepers’ activity counts does not by itself reveal interaction.

## Aim 2: Establish causal dose-response functions across sleep stages

Aim 2 will enroll 96 sleepers from Aim 1, balanced across mechanical susceptibility and sex and selected without regard to whether they consider their partner disruptive. Each participant will complete an adaptation night followed by two experimental laboratory nights. The participant will sleep on one side of the bed while an adjacent anthropomorphic platen reproduces movement waveforms recorded from that participant’s partner at home. The platen allows mechanical input to be delivered without awakening or otherwise changing the behavior of a second sleeper.

Real-time polysomnographic staging will trigger perturbations during stable N2, N3, and rapid eye movement sleep. Events will be separated by at least eight minutes and suspended after an arousal, respiratory event, or stage transition. No more than 18 perturbations will be delivered per night. Mechanical amplitudes will correspond to the participant’s 20th, 50th, and 80th percentiles of naturally experienced transferred energy, capped by a prespecified safety and comfort limit. The event set will include mechanical-only playback under low-level broadband masking, acoustic-only playback reproducing the sound envelope of the partner’s movement, combined mechanical and acoustic playback, and sham triggers with neither stimulus. Event order will be randomized within stage, subject to sleep availability.

The primary endpoint is cortical arousal probability within 15 seconds of the trigger. Prespecified secondary endpoints are EEG spectral activation, K-complex and spindle expression, heart-rate acceleration, pulse-amplitude reduction, stage transition, behavioral movement, and return to stable sleep. Pre-event spindle density, slow-wave phase, recent arousal burden, and time of night will be evaluated as moderators. The analysis will estimate an individualized mechanical dose-response curve and test whether adding sound shifts the curve beyond the mechanical effect alone.

To address habituation and anticipation, participants will not be told the timing or number of events, sham triggers will be frequent, and trial number will be included in the model. Morning assessments will include perceived disturbance, sleep restoration, mood, and a 10-minute psychomotor vigilance task. These next-day measures are secondary because the experiment is optimized to resolve event-level physiology, not to mimic an entire season of naturally fragmented sleep.

Aim 2 provides the causal bridge that observational dyadic recordings cannot supply. It will determine whether a partner-like movement is sufficient to evoke a response, whether the relevant exposure is mechanical, acoustic, or combined, and why equal impulses disturb some sleep states more than others. The natural waveforms and individualized amplitudes preserve ecological relevance while the empty adjacent platen removes reciprocal partner behavior.

## Aim 3: Test active reduction of movement transfer at home

We will enroll 120 Aim 1 dyads into a randomized, counterbalanced crossover trial. Sampling will include all tertiles of baseline transfer rather than only highly disturbed sleepers. LatticeLift will be installed beneath each dyad’s usual mattress. After a three-night run-in, dyads will receive 14 nights of active isolation and 14 nights of logging-only control in randomized order, separated by a three-night neutral interval. The platform housing, indicator lights, setup procedures, and nightly sounds will be identical across modes. Participants and outcome scorers will not be told the assigned sequence, although post-period questionnaires will assess perceived assignment and changes in support or comfort.

Both sleepers will use the Aim 1 force, acceleration, and frontal EEG system throughout the final ten nights of each period. Finger photoplethysmography will be added on nights 12 through 14. The primary outcome is dyad-mean wake after sleep onset, calculated from EEG without reference to movement events. Key secondary outcomes are sleep efficiency, number and duration of awakenings, movement-linked arousal rate, total cross-midline impulse energy, morning restoration, self-reported disturbance by the partner, and bed-sharing adherence. Each sleeper will complete a psychomotor vigilance task on the final two mornings of each period. Relationship warmth and conflict will be measured as exploratory outcomes because reducing physical disturbance could improve morning interactions, but mechanical isolation might also alter the familiar sensory context of co-sleeping.

The primary intention-to-treat model will include intervention mode, period, sequence, baseline transfer tertile, and random effects for dyad and sleeper. We will test for carryover before estimating the main contrast and will report period-specific estimates if meaningful carryover is detected. Nights on which a participant sleeps elsewhere will remain part of the adherence outcome but will not be assigned artificial EEG values. Multiple imputation will be used for missing covariates and intermittent device loss when supported by observed data; complete-case and pattern-mixture sensitivity analyses will assess departures from missing-at-random assumptions.

Mechanistic analyses will ask whether the active mode reduces measured transfer and whether nights with larger transfer reduction also show larger changes in wake after sleep onset. Moderation analyses will examine baseline susceptibility, mattress type, body-size difference, and sleep stage at source movement. These analyses will distinguish failure to reduce transmission from successful mechanical attenuation without physiological benefit.

The trial is powered around the within-dyad contrast in wake after sleep onset. Allowing for 15% attrition, 120 randomized dyads provide greater than 90% power to detect an eight-minute difference in dyad-mean wake after sleep onset under the variance and within-dyad correlation observed in feasibility recordings. Aim 2 provides at least 80% power to detect an odds ratio of 1.30 per standardized increment in transferred energy when repeated stage-specific events are modeled with participant random effects. Power calculations incorporate event clustering and unequal availability of rapid eye movement and N3 sleep.

## Rigor, reproducibility, and participant protection

Primary outcomes, exclusion rules, model formulas, and stopping criteria will be registered before the relevant data are unlocked. The TurnTrace pipeline will be frozen after Aim 1 validation and applied without retuning to the Aim 3 trial. Laboratory sleep scoring and trial outcome review will be performed by staff masked to stimulus amplitude or platform mode. Device logs will preserve all failed detections and actuator commands, allowing treatment delivery to be audited independently of physiological response.

Participant burden will be reduced by flexible installation, replaceable sensors, and scheduled sensor-free nights between intensive measurements. Laboratory perturbations will stop after sustained wakefulness, distress, or a participant’s request. An independent safety monitor will review falls, pain, marked sleep loss, and unexpected device behavior. The active platform is designed to counteract small bed-frame impulses, not to restrain movement or alter body position.

Sex, age, body size, and relationship configuration will be treated as potential sources of variation rather than merely adjustment variables. Recruitment progress will be reviewed quarterly to avoid concentration in a single dyad type. De-identified event-level data, signal-processing code, a synthetic force-signal training set, and the prespecified analysis scripts will be released after primary publication. Raw audio and identifiable bedroom video will not be shared; home video will not be collected at all.

## Expected outcomes and impact

SomnoBridge will produce the first integrated account of how movement travels from one sleeper, through a shared bed, into another sleeper’s physiology. We expect to identify a reproducible transfer threshold, clarify when movement synchrony reflects disturbance rather than shared timing, and determine which sleep states and sleepers are most susceptible. If active isolation improves wake after sleep onset and next-day vigilance, the study will establish bed mechanics as a modifiable component of the sleep environment. If transfer is reduced without improving sleep, that result will be equally informative: it would indicate that partner complaints arise mainly from sound, temperature, expectation, or shared physiology rather than mattress-borne movement.

The project does not assume that less coupling is universally better. Familiar movement may be neutral or reassuring for some dyads, and the intervention may benefit only those with high transfer and high physiological susceptibility. By measuring both members of the dyad and retaining ordinary bed-sharing, the study can identify who benefits, who does not, and whether an individual sleep gain is accompanied by a change in the shared sleep experience.

The broader contribution is a shift from treating the sleeping partner as an uncontrolled nuisance variable to treating the bed-sharing system as a tractable scientific unit. The resulting framework can be extended to older adults, caregivers, people with chronic pain, and households in which one sleeper has a movement or respiratory disorder. It will also provide manufacturers and clinicians with outcomes grounded in physiology rather than motion-isolation demonstrations alone. Through ecological measurement, controlled perturbation, and randomized intervention, SomnoBridge will determine whether an overlooked mechanical pathway makes a meaningful contribution to fragmented sleep.
