## Artefact

# STEADY-ASH: Stability-Aware Flight Routing Under Repeated Volcanic Ash Forecast Updates

## Project summary

Volcanic ash routing is usually framed as a sequence of independent avoidance problems: obtain the latest ash forecast, identify an acceptable route, and repeat when the forecast changes. This framing overlooks a practical cost of uncertainty. Small changes between forecast cycles can cause a flight plan to move repeatedly between corridors even when the underlying hazard situation has not materially changed. Each revision consumes dispatcher and controller attention, alters fuel and crew calculations, complicates passenger recovery, and can shift traffic into sectors that were not prepared for it. Crews may also receive a series of individually defensible but collectively confusing amendments.

STEADY-ASH will investigate whether aviation operators can reduce this “route churn” without increasing ash exposure. The project will develop a stability-aware routing method that treats a flight plan as a contingent plan rather than a disposable path. It will identify a robust trunk route, a limited set of downstream branches, and geographically meaningful decision gates at which new ash information can justify changing branches. Between gates, the method will resist changes caused only by minor forecast variation. Major changes, including a newly observed ash incursion, will always be allowed to override the stability preference.

The method will be tested through historical forecast replay, synthetic eruption stress tests, dispatcher tabletop exercises, and an eight-week shadow-mode trial in the North Pelagic Flight Information Region. Its performance will be compared with shortest-path routing, deterministic exclusion-zone routing, and uncertainty-aware routing that lacks a stability term. Primary outcomes will be estimated ash dose, fuel burn, delay, route revisions, and operational workload.

The intended result is not an autonomous clearance system. It is a decision-support prototype and an evidence base for deciding when route stability is useful, when it is unsafe, and how contingent routes should be presented to human operators.

## 1. Background and rationale

Ash-contaminated airspace creates an unusual routing problem. The hazard moves with the atmosphere, varies by altitude, and is only partially observed. Forecasts may change because the eruption changes, because new observations arrive, or because the atmospheric model represents dispersion differently after an update. These causes are operationally distinct, but they can produce similar changes on a map.

Most routing models respond to each new concentration field as if no earlier plan existed. A route that is optimal at 09:00 may be replaced at 09:20, restored at 09:40, and replaced again at 10:00. This behavior is mathematically reasonable if each optimization is isolated. It is less attractive in a network where flight plans, fuel loads, sector staffing, gate assignments, and crew duties have inertia.

Route churn is not merely an administrative nuisance. A revised transoceanic entry point can affect several neighboring sectors. Repeated amendments can obscure the distinction between a safety-critical update and an economically marginal one. Conversely, operators that become reluctant to revise a plan after investing effort in it may retain a route for too long. The research problem is therefore not to suppress change, but to distinguish useful adaptation from unstable reaction.

The project focuses on pre-departure and early-flight planning for flights with at least 90 minutes remaining before the closest anticipated approach to an ash field. Tactical cockpit avoidance and immediate emergency response are outside scope. The selected horizon is long enough for a route amendment to affect fuel, slot, and sector decisions, but short enough for successive dispersion forecasts to matter.

The non-obvious premise of STEADY-ASH is that a route can preserve future choices. A slightly longer path may reach a position from which either a northern or southern branch remains feasible. Such a path can be preferable to a shorter route that commits the aircraft early to one side of an uncertain plume. Existing route comparisons often price distance and hazard but assign no value to this option.

## 2. Research questions and hypotheses

The project will address four questions:

1. **When does stability-aware routing reduce unnecessary flight-plan revisions without increasing modeled ash exposure?**
2. **Can a trunk-and-branch plan preserve useful options at acceptable fuel cost?**
3. **Which forecast changes should trigger an immediate branch change, and which should be absorbed until a planned decision gate?**
4. **Do dispatchers understand and use contingent route recommendations more effectively than a stream of complete replacement routes?**

Three testable hypotheses guide the work:

- **H1:** Relative to an uncertainty-aware router that replans from scratch, the stability-aware method will reduce pre-execution route revisions by at least 30% while keeping the 95th-percentile modeled ash dose no more than 5% higher.
- **H2:** A contingent trunk with two or three branches will reduce the probability of losing all acceptable routes during the next two forecast cycles, compared with committing to the currently cheapest complete route.
- **H3:** In tabletop exercises, dispatchers using decision-gate displays will complete replanning tasks faster and with fewer unnecessary amendments than dispatchers receiving full replacement routes after every update.

The numerical thresholds are operational targets rather than established safety boundaries. Any modeled exposure above the participating operator’s applicable limit will be counted as a failure regardless of performance on the other metrics.

## 3. Study setting and use case

The main case study will cover the fictional North Pelagic Flight Information Region, where east–west traffic passes between the Rook Caldera volcanic chain and a set of capacity-constrained oceanic entry corridors. The geometry is useful because an ash plume can force traffic north or south, while the central approach corridor can retain option value for part of a flight.

Asterline Airways, a medium-sized long-haul carrier, will provide de-identified operational records and access to dispatch personnel. The North Pelagic Flow Office will provide archived sector configurations and participate in scenario design. Neither partner will be asked to change live flight clearances during the research trial.

The principal decision unit is one aircraft and one forecast update. Network effects will also be represented, because individually stable routes may still create unstable sector demand if many flights select the same branch.

## 4. Data

### 4.1 Historical replay archive

The project will assemble the **Pelagic Ash Routing Replay Archive**, containing six past eruption episodes reconstructed from the partners’ internal records. The archive is expected to include approximately:

- 420 successive three-dimensional ash forecast cycles;
- 11,000 planned or operated flights;
- aircraft type, origin, destination, planned mass band, and fuel reserve category;
- forecast wind, temperature, and tropopause fields;
- filed-route revisions with timestamps and coded reasons where available;
- sector capacity configurations; and
- around 1,300 ash-related observations from satellite retrievals, ground instruments, and pilot reports.

All eruption and flight identifiers will be replaced with study codes. Exact city pairs will be generalized for public releases.

Historical records do not provide a reliable concentration measurement at every aircraft position. Accordingly, the project will distinguish between forecast exposure, observation-supported exposure, and unknown exposure rather than treating the archived dispersion analysis as ground truth. Observations will be used to score forecast members and to identify major plume displacement, but they cannot fully validate the dose assigned to an individual route.

### 4.2 Synthetic cases

Six episodes are too few to cover the possible combinations of plume direction, eruption duration, and corridor capacity. A synthetic generator will therefore perturb eruption timing, emission rate, vertical injection height, wind field, and observation delay. It will create 600 scenarios, including abrupt plume bifurcation, forecast oscillation around a corridor boundary, and temporary loss of satellite retrievals.

Synthetic cases will preserve the spatial covariance patterns estimated from the historical forecast archive. Extreme emission rates will be capped at 1.5 times the largest archived case, avoiding implausible stress tests but also limiting inference about rarer events.

## 5. Routing method

### 5.1 Time-dependent route graph

Airspace will be represented as a four-dimensional graph. Nodes are waypoint–altitude–time combinations, and edges represent feasible aircraft motion over five-minute intervals. Edge feasibility will account for aircraft performance envelopes, climb and descent limits, route availability, destination reserves, and broad sector-entry restrictions.

For each ash ensemble member, an edge receives an exposure estimate calculated by integrating forecast concentration over traversal time. A categorical adjustment will distinguish three engine susceptibility classes, but particle size, mineral composition, and engine condition will not be modeled directly. The cumulative route value is therefore a planning proxy for ingestion burden, not a prediction of engine damage.

Each forecast ensemble member will initially receive equal weight. An experimental version will update the weights according to recent agreement with observations, subject to a minimum weight so that a temporarily poor member is not discarded. The equal-weight version will be the primary analysis because observation coverage differs sharply among episodes.

### 5.2 Contingent route structure

Instead of returning one complete route, the system will return:

1. a **trunk**, intended to remain acceptable across a large fraction of ensemble members;
2. up to three **branches**, each suited to a different plume development;
3. one or more **decision gates**, defined by time and location; and
4. an **override condition** for changes that should be acted on before the next gate.

Candidate gates will be restricted to points where communication, fuel, and sector coordination make a branch change operationally plausible. A gate cannot be placed so late that the aircraft would require an excessive turn or violate destination reserves.

The value of a contingent plan will be estimated over three forecast cycles. At each simulated update, the system can retain the current branch, select another prepared branch, or invoke an unprepared escape route at a penalty. This creates a limited receding-horizon decision problem. A full multihour stochastic optimization would be computationally expensive and would imply forecast skill beyond the available archive.

### 5.3 Objective function

For plan `p`, the optimizer will minimize a weighted sum:

`J(p) = fuel + delay + ash risk + capacity pressure + revision cost + lost-option cost`.

Fuel and delay will use operator planning estimates. Ash risk will combine expected cumulative exposure with the mean exposure among the worst 10% of ensemble outcomes. Capacity pressure will increase nonlinearly as predicted sector entries approach the configured limit. Revision cost will depend on how much of the previously communicated route changes and how soon the changed segment would be flown. Lost-option cost will estimate the chance that no prepared branch remains acceptable after the next two updates.

All components will be normalized against direct-route fuel and episode-specific forecast ranges. Initial weights will be elicited in workshops with Asterline dispatchers and flow-office planners, then fixed before the main replay analysis. Sensitivity runs will vary each weight by plus or minus 50%. This does not eliminate the normative character of the trade-offs, but it will show whether results depend on one narrow weight setting.

### 5.4 Change thresholds and overrides

A recommendation will normally remain on its current branch until a decision gate unless one of four override conditions applies:

- any ensemble member with substantial observational support places the route above the operator’s exposure limit;
- the branch becomes infeasible for fuel, weather, or airspace reasons;
- its worst-decile exposure rises by more than a configured margin; or
- waiting until the gate would eliminate every acceptable alternative.

The first prototype will use a common exposure-change margin across aircraft within the same engine susceptibility class. This simplification makes evaluation tractable, although it may miss differences due to maintenance history or specific engine variants.

## 6. Evaluation design

### 6.1 Baselines

STEADY-ASH will be compared with four baselines:

- **Nominal shortest path:** shortest feasible route with no ash term, retained only as a diagnostic lower bound on fuel use.
- **Deterministic exclusion:** shortest route avoiding every grid cell above a selected concentration threshold in the latest median forecast.
- **Ensemble risk routing:** minimizes fuel, delay, and ash risk but replans from scratch at every cycle.
- **Freeze-until-unsafe:** retains the current route unless it violates the operator’s exposure limit.

The contrast between ensemble risk routing and STEADY-ASH isolates the effect of route stability and option preservation. The freeze baseline tests whether simple reluctance to change can obtain similar revision reductions.

### 6.2 Historical replay

Each episode will be replayed in chronological order, revealing only information available at that point. Flights will enter the simulation at their historical planning time. The router will issue recommendations after every forecast cycle until landing or until the flight is outside the affected region.

Primary metrics are:

- modeled cumulative ash exposure and worst-decile exposure;
- number of plan revisions per flight;
- kilometers of route changed after initial filing;
- fuel burn and arrival delay;
- number and duration of sector overloads;
- fraction of flights for which all prepared branches become unacceptable; and
- time between a material hazard change and a recommended response.

A “material hazard change” will be defined in advance as either an observation-supported plume displacement of at least 40 kilometers toward a route or a doubling of modeled concentration along the next 45 minutes of flight. These values are practical definitions for analysis, not universal physical thresholds.

Episodes, rather than individual forecast cycles, will be the main resampling unit because errors within an eruption are strongly related. Results will be reported by episode and in aggregate. Paired block bootstrap intervals will compare methods. Mixed-effects models will examine whether performance varies with forecast disagreement, traffic density, lead time, and route geometry. No formal power calculation is planned for the six historical episodes; synthetic scenarios will provide precision for algorithmic comparisons but cannot substitute for independent real eruptions.

### 6.3 Stress and ablation tests

The synthetic study will progressively increase forecast oscillation, observation delay, and corridor saturation. An ablation analysis will remove the revision term, option term, decision gates, and capacity term one at a time. This will reveal whether lower churn comes from genuine option preservation or merely from penalizing any change.

Failure-oriented tests will include a rapidly rising plume, an incorrect high-confidence forecast, simultaneous closure of both prepared branches, and a forecast that alternates across a threshold for six cycles. A successful system should remain stable in the final case but react quickly in the first three.

### 6.4 Human-in-the-loop study

Twenty-eight licensed dispatchers from Asterline will complete eight tabletop scenarios in randomized order. Half of the scenarios will use the carrier’s existing map-and-route display. Half will add the STEADY-ASH trunk, branches, gate timer, and textual override explanation. Participants will make release, amend, hold, or cancel decisions while scripted messages simulate forecast and sector updates.

Outcomes will include task completion time, amendment count, missed override conditions, route-selection agreement, and perceived workload on a seven-point scale. Screen interactions and verbal reasoning will be recorded with consent. A short interview will examine whether participants understand why the system recommends waiting or changing.

Scenario order will be balanced, and the analysis will account for participant and scenario effects. Because every participant comes from one airline and will receive project-specific training, the study cannot establish that the interface will work equally well in other operational cultures.

### 6.5 Shadow-mode trial

Following offline testing, the prototype will run for eight weeks alongside Asterline’s dispatch process. It will ingest the same forecast feed available to dispatchers but will have no connection to the flight-plan submission system. Recommendations will be timestamped before the actual operational decision is revealed.

Investigators will compare proposed and actual routes, reasons for divergence, forecast exposure, revisions, and estimated fuel. Dispatchers may annotate recommendations after their shift. The trial will stop if data latency exceeds 20 minutes, if an input feed is incorrectly labeled, or if the prototype repeatedly presents an infeasible route.

The shadow period may contain no substantial eruption. In that event, it will test integration and routine usability but not real-event safety performance.

## 7. Safety, governance, and data management

The prototype will be explicitly labeled as non-operational. It will not issue clearances, alter aircraft trajectories, or replace applicable ash procedures. During human exercises, exposure limits and scenarios will be fictionalized so that participants do not confuse study values with current company policy.

A safety review group comprising one dispatcher, one flight-operations engineer, one airspace-flow planner, and two researchers will review all override logic before the shadow trial. Any route judged operationally impossible will be retained in the error record rather than silently removed from evaluation.

Partner data will be stored in a segregated project environment. Public outputs will include the graph construction code, synthetic scenario generator, metric definitions, and a reduced replay dataset with generalized geography. Raw flight records, detailed sector configurations, and employee recordings will remain restricted. Participants in the tabletop study may withdraw their recordings without withdrawing aggregate task results already anonymized.

## 8. Work plan and deliverables

The project will run for 24 months.

- **Months 1–4:** data agreements, archive construction, route graph, and hazard-field harmonization.
- **Months 5–8:** forecast-error analysis and baseline implementations.
- **Months 9–13:** contingent-route optimizer, change thresholds, and interface prototype.
- **Months 14–17:** historical replay, synthetic stress tests, and ablation study.
- **Months 18–20:** dispatcher tabletop study and interface revision.
- **Months 21–22:** shadow-mode deployment.
- **Months 23–24:** synthesis, release of reproducible materials, and operational guidance.

Deliverables will be a stability-aware routing prototype, the Pelagic Ash Routing Replay Archive in restricted and reduced forms, a scenario generator, a human-factors report, and a guidance document describing when decision gates should not be used.

## 9. Expected contribution

STEADY-ASH will move the research question from “What is the best route under the latest forecast?” to “What plan remains understandable and adaptable as the forecast changes?” Its technical contribution is the joint treatment of ash uncertainty, route revision cost, and the option value of future branches. Its empirical contribution is a replay framework that measures not only exposure and fuel but also amendment burden and delayed reaction.

The project will not determine a universally safe ash concentration or resolve uncertainty in engine response. It will instead test a narrower proposition: when several routes already satisfy an operator’s safety constraints, explicitly valuing stability and future choice may produce plans that are easier to operate without disguising meaningful hazard changes.
