## Artefact

# The Date Is Not a Detail: Correcting Survey-Timing Bias in Glacier Mass Balance

## Question

Annual drone and airborne-lidar surveys are often treated as comparable if they occur near the end of the melt season. In the Veyra Range, however, weather and aircraft availability pushed surveys progressively later in some years. This analysis asks whether that scheduling drift materially changes estimated glacier mass balance.

We analyzed nine small maritime glaciers from 2014 to 2025. The central comparison is between mass balances calculated directly from successive elevation surveys and balances standardized to a common reference date of 15 September. The purpose is not to replace geodetic measurements with a melt model, but to estimate the mass gained or lost between each survey date and the reference date.

## Data

The Veyra Glacier Monitoring Archive contains 78 usable annual survey intervals. Glacier areas range from 0.7 to 6.2 km², with a combined 2020 area of 24.8 km². Surveys were produced from drone photogrammetry before 2019 and airborne lidar thereafter. Horizontal and vertical offsets were corrected against stable bedrock, and cells with slopes above 55° were excluded because of persistent matching noise.

Survey timing was not constant. The median acquisition date shifted from day 247, approximately 4 September, in 2014–2017 to day 263, approximately 20 September, in 2022–2025. Individual flights ranged from 18 August to 7 October. Later surveys capture additional autumn melt in warm years but may capture new snow in cold years.

Daily temperature came from three automatic stations spanning 640–1,920 m elevation. Solid precipitation was estimated from a single heated ridge gauge and distributed by elevation. A network of 34–41 ablation stakes per year supplied 312 usable observations of surface change lasting at least seven days.

## Standardization method

For each glacier and survey year, daily surface balance was modeled in 50 m elevation bands. Ice and snow melt were represented with separate temperature-index factors, while precipitation was classified as snow across a two-degree transition interval. Parameters were fitted to the stake observations, with each glacier omitted in turn during cross-validation.

Let `M(d_y)` be glacier mass on the actual survey date in year `y`, and let `M(r_y)` be modeled mass on the 15 September reference date. The correction for that survey is:

`c_y = M(r_y) − M(d_y)`

The standardized annual balance is therefore:

`B*_y = B_raw,y + c_y − c_(y−1)`

This endpoint formulation matters because an annual interval can be biased by both its opening and closing dates. Corrections were integrated over each glacier's elevation distribution and applied after converting geodetic volume change to water equivalent. Ice was assigned a density of 900 kg m⁻³; snow and firn density varied by elevation and season, with uncertainty sampled between 480 and 850 kg m⁻³.

Uncertainty draws combined elevation-change error, density, stake-calibrated model parameters, and spatially correlated residuals. Reported trend errors are one standard error from 2,000 bootstrap replicates that resampled years and glaciers. Annual outlines were available in five of the twelve survey years; glacier area and elevation distributions were linearly interpolated between those observations.

## Results

Across all glaciers, the uncorrected mean balance was −0.73 m water equivalent per year (m w.e. a⁻¹). Date standardization reduced the estimated loss to −0.65 m w.e. a⁻¹. Thus, survey timing contributed 0.08 m w.e. a⁻¹, or about 11% of the raw mean loss.

The correction was not uniform. It was negligible in years when successive flights occurred within four days of 15 September, but exceeded 0.20 m w.e. for intervals bounded by an early-August survey and a late-September survey. Across individual annual balances, estimated total uncertainty ranged from 0.09 to 0.21 m w.e.; date correction was therefore comparable to measurement uncertainty in roughly one-third of the intervals.

Mean balances are in m w.e. a⁻¹. Trends are changes in annual balance per decade, in m w.e. a⁻¹ decade⁻¹.

A mixed-effects model with glacier-specific intercepts estimated that annual balance became more negative by 0.19 ± 0.06 m w.e. a⁻¹ per decade without standardization. After standardization, the trend was 0.12 ± 0.05 m w.e. a⁻¹ per decade. The bootstrap distribution of the difference had a median of 0.07 and a 95% interval of 0.02–0.13 m w.e. a⁻¹ per decade. The glaciers still lost mass increasingly rapidly, but the raw surveys overstated the rate of deterioration by approximately one-third.

Standardization also changed the ranking of individual years. The raw record classified 2018 as the third-most-negative year, largely because several 2017 surveys were early and the corresponding 2018 surveys were late. On a common date, 2018 ranked sixth. Conversely, 2023 moved from fourth to second-most-negative because October snow had partly concealed the preceding summer loss in the raw elevation difference.

Leave-one-glacier-out cross-validation produced a median absolute stake error of 0.07 m w.e. over observation periods averaging 24 days. Eleven repeat flights, separated by 10–28 days, provided an independent check at glacier scale. The modeled corrections had a mean bias of +0.01 m w.e. and a median absolute error of 0.06 m w.e. Although this supports the correction's magnitude, only two repeat-flight intervals included substantial autumn snowfall.

## Interpretation and limitations

The results show that acquisition date can be a systematic component of a geodetic mass-balance record rather than mere scheduling metadata. In this archive, later surveys increasingly sampled the high-loss end of the season. Exact division by interval length did not remove the effect because late-summer melt is much faster than the annual average.

The corrected trend remains negative, so timing bias does not explain away the observed acceleration in loss. It does, however, affect its magnitude and the identification of exceptional years. Programs unable to fly on a fixed date should retain daily weather and stake observations so that survey endpoints can be standardized.

Three limitations constrain the precision of the correction. First, the precipitation field depends on one ridge gauge and may miss wind-driven snow redistribution, especially above 2,000 m. Second, repeat-flight validation is sparse in snowy autumn conditions, where distinguishing accumulation from reduced melt is most difficult. Third, linearly interpolated glacier outlines may smooth abrupt retreat or elevation-band changes. These issues are unlikely to reverse the direction of the timing effect, but they could alter its estimated size, particularly for the highest glaciers and for October surveys.
