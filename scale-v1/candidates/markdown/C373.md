## Artefact

# The Hidden Clock in Vaccine Cold Chains

A vaccine cold chain is often pictured as a line of refrigerators: a national store, a regional depot, a clinic refrigerator and, finally, a carrier taken to an outreach session. That picture is useful, but it hides the part of the system most likely to fail. Vaccines do not move directly from one cold box to another. They wait on loading benches, sit in unopened shipping containers, pass through custody checks, travel over uneven roads and return from cancelled sessions. Every transfer starts a clock.

The practical problem is therefore not simply how to keep a vaccine cold. It is how to manage its **thermal budget**: the combination of time and temperature exposure that a particular product can tolerate before its quality becomes uncertain. That budget differs by vaccine, presentation and manufacturer. It may also depend on whether the product has been frozen accidentally, warmed repeatedly or removed under an approved controlled-temperature procedure.

Thinking in terms of thermal budgets changes logistics decisions. A two-hour delay is not automatically harmless because a carrier still feels cold, and a brief rise in air temperature is not automatically evidence that the vaccine must be discarded. The important questions are what temperature the product itself probably experienced, for how long, under what prior conditions and with what evidence.

## Three clocks, not one

Every vaccine shipment is governed by at least three clocks.

The first is the familiar **expiry clock**. It advances with calendar time and determines whether a vial remains within its labelled shelf life under specified storage conditions.

The second is the **journey clock**. It starts when stock leaves controlled storage and measures how long the transport container is expected to maintain its qualified temperature range. A carrier validated for 48 hours does not promise 48 hours in every circumstance. Its performance may assume a particular ambient temperature, coolant configuration, fill level, opening schedule and position in a vehicle.

The third is the **excursion clock**. This records exposure outside the labelled range. It cannot always be read directly from a single thermometer. A logger may show that air next to the lid reached 11°C for twenty minutes while the liquid in the central vials remained cooler. Conversely, a display mounted on a refrigerator door may report 4°C while vials touching the rear cooling surface begin to freeze.

These clocks interact but are not interchangeable. Expiry dates do not reveal the effect of a recent excursion. Carrier hold time does not authorize storage outside a product’s label. A temperature indicator that integrates cumulative heat exposure may answer a different question from an electronic logger that records peaks every five minutes.

The result is a chain of evidence as much as a chain of cooling equipment. Staff need to know which clock a record describes before using it to release, quarantine or discard stock.

## A thermal budget is product-specific

It is tempting to express thermal exposure as a simple balance. For preliminary screening, a team might calculate “degree-hours” above an upper limit:

> screening exposure = hours outside range × degrees above the limit

Under that rough calculation, two hours at 10°C and one hour at 12°C would each equal four degree-hours if the upper limit were 8°C. This can help sort records or identify unusually severe events. It is not, however, a universal stability model. Chemical degradation often accelerates nonlinearly with temperature, and biological products can respond differently to short peaks and sustained warming. A degree-hour score must not replace the product-specific excursion assessment supplied by an authorized quality function or manufacturer.

Cold exposure creates a separate problem. Many liquid vaccines are damaged by freezing, and a “colder is safer” rule can therefore be destructive. Ice packs taken directly from a freezer may create local sub-zero zones even when the average carrier temperature appears acceptable. Freeze damage may not be obvious by looking at a vial. Once suspected, it generally cannot be cancelled out by later returning the product to 5°C.

Other products require frozen or ultra-cold storage and may have a limited period in a refrigerator after thawing. For them, the direction of the risk changes: warming begins a time-limited state transition, and refreezing may be prohibited. Diluent can have yet another storage profile. A packing list that treats “the vaccine” as one homogeneous object may consequently place one component in the correct environment and another in the wrong one.

For operational planning, each product should have a compact handling profile containing at least:

- its labelled storage range;
- its sensitivity to freezing and heat;
- any permitted transport or controlled-temperature conditions;
- rules for thawing, reconstitution and use after opening;
- the type and location of required temperature monitoring;
- the authority that can assess an excursion; and
- the evidence needed before quarantined stock can be released.

The profile should travel with the process, not remain in a manual stored at headquarters. A clinic receiving mixed products needs instructions that distinguish them at the point of action.

## The package creates a small climate

A transport box is not merely an insulated container. It is a temporary climate whose behavior depends on its walls, coolant, load and handling.

Coolant selection begins with the target range. Frozen water packs provide substantial cooling but can freeze adjacent products. Conditioned packs—allowed to warm until their surfaces reach the specified state—reduce that risk, although visual signs such as surface moisture are an imperfect substitute for a defined conditioning procedure. Phase-change materials designed around a target temperature can produce a narrower range, but only when prepared according to their qualification. A pack frozen, chilled or heated in the wrong appliance may behave very differently from the validated configuration.

Load geometry also matters. A full carrier often changes temperature more slowly than a nearly empty one because its contents add thermal mass and reduce circulating warm air. Yet operators sometimes remove dividers or add extra coolant to “make sure it stays cold,” unknowingly altering the design that was tested. Cardboard spacers, bubble wrap and vial cartons are not neutral additions if they redirect airflow or place freeze-sensitive stock against coolant.

A useful pack-out instruction is therefore visual and countable. It states which coolant packs to use, how they are prepared, where each pack sits, where the vaccine sits, what separates them and where the logger sensor is placed. It should cover partial loads rather than assuming every shipment fills the box. If a route routinely carries only one-quarter of the qualified load, that configuration needs evaluation rather than improvisation.

The temperature logger belongs near the product, but no single location captures the entire climate. A sensor beside a coolant pack may overstate freezing risk for central vials, while a central sensor may miss a warm zone near the lid. Qualification testing can identify the likely hot and cold spots. Routine monitoring can then use a specified location chosen for a clear reason, with occasional multi-point studies to confirm that the package still behaves as expected.

## Handoffs consume invisible time

Road travel is visible on a route plan. Handoff time often is not. A driver may arrive at a depot at 07:40, wait for a key holder, unload at 08:10 and obtain a receiving signature at 08:35. If the log records only departure and receipt, fifty-five minutes disappears.

That missing time matters most when containers are opened. During a closed delay, insulation may preserve the internal climate. During an open delay, warm air enters, coolant is exposed and staff may repeatedly search for paperwork or count vials. The same thirty-minute stop can therefore have very different thermal effects.

A practical handoff record should capture four events:

1. arrival at the receiving site;
2. opening of the transport container;
3. transfer into qualified storage; and
4. formal acceptance of custody.

These events need not occur in that order. A receiver can place stock in a refrigerator before finishing the inventory, provided packaging and identification controls allow it. Separating physical protection from administrative acceptance is one of the easiest ways to reduce exposure. It also requires a clear rule for discrepancies: if two vials are missing or a label is damaged, the rest of the shipment should not remain on a bench while the issue is discussed.

Some programs adopt a fifteen-minute handoff target for ordinary deliveries. The exact number is less important than measuring the interval consistently and investigating repeated overruns. A remote clinic may reasonably need longer than an urban depot, while an ultra-cold transfer may need a much tighter sequence. A universal target can hide those differences, so local teams should treat it as an operating trigger rather than a statement of product stability.

Custody design is also important after outreach sessions. Returned vials may be placed in a carrier with unused coolant but no note of when the lid was opened. If the clinic refrigerator later fails, staff cannot reconstruct the vials’ earlier exposure. The thermal budget has become an undocumented debt.

## Information can arrive too late

Electronic monitoring is often described as real-time, but each stage introduces latency. A logger may sample every five minutes, store readings locally for hours, upload only when a vehicle reaches mobile coverage and trigger an alert after a server applies its rules. By the time a manager sees a high-temperature alarm, the shipment may already have been accepted and distributed.

This does not make remote monitoring useless. It means that alerts need an operational path. A useful alert states which shipment is affected, what condition was observed, who currently has custody and what action is expected. “Temperature high” is less useful than “Keep shipment RV-184 sealed and move it to the shaded receiving bay; quality officer reviewing logger trace.”

Alarm thresholds also need context. Setting an alarm exactly at the upper storage limit can produce repeated notifications from harmless sensor fluctuation or brief door openings. Staff may begin to ignore them. Setting a wide delay to reduce nuisance alarms can miss a rapid failure. One solution is a tiered system:

| Signal | Possible operational meaning | Immediate response |
|---|---|---|
| Near-limit warning | Conditions are deteriorating but remain within the operating band | Check equipment, door status and route delay |
| Short excursion | A threshold was crossed briefly | Protect stock, preserve data and assess product exposure |
| Sustained excursion | Active cooling or package hold time may have failed | Quarantine affected stock and escalate promptly |
| Sensor silence | Power, connectivity or logger placement may be faulty | Verify locally; do not assume temperature failure or safety |

The response should distinguish **quarantine** from **discard**. Quarantine preserves stock while preventing use. Discard is a final disposition decision. Automatically throwing away every shipment associated with an alarm wastes usable doses and can create shortages; automatically resetting an alarm destroys evidence. The receiver should keep the product under appropriate conditions, mark it so it cannot be issued, save the complete temperature record and seek the designated assessment.

Paper records still have a role. They can document custody when batteries fail or networks are unavailable. Their weakness is not that they are paper but that values are often copied without timestamps, units, device identifiers or initials. A handwritten “5” could mean 5°C at receipt, a minimum of 5°C over the journey or a reading from the room thermometer. A short, well-designed form can be more reliable than a complex dashboard whose alerts nobody owns.

## Worked example: the Larkspur route

Consider a hypothetical district called Larkspur Vale. Its store supplies six clinics on a loop of 146 kilometers. The route carries two products:

- **Mera-L**, a freeze-sensitive liquid vaccine labelled for storage at 2°C to 8°C; and
- **Orin-P**, a hypothetical frozen presentation that can remain refrigerated for a limited period after documented thawing.

The district uses one type of insulated carrier for both products, but not on the same trip. The carrier was originally qualified for 36 hours at an ambient test condition of 32°C when fully loaded. In practice, many Monday deliveries are less than half full, afternoon vehicle temperatures may exceed the test condition, and clinic staff open the lid before checking whether refrigerator space is available.

The logistics team initially treats late arrival as its main risk. It compares scheduled and actual journey time and finds that most trips finish within nine hours, far below the carrier’s nominal 36-hour hold time. On that evidence, the route looks safe.

A closer reconstruction reveals three gaps. First, carriers are packed at 05:30 but may not leave until 07:00. Journey time has been measured from vehicle departure, not from completed pack-out. Second, frozen coolant packs touch cartons of Mera-L on low-volume days because the standard divider is removed to stop cartons moving. Third, the driver returns unused vials to the district store after the final clinic, but the return leg has no logger because monitoring ends at delivery.

The team redesigns the route around the whole period of exposure. The logistics clock now starts when vaccine leaves controlled storage. Partial-load pack-outs use fixed spacers that preserve the qualified separation from coolant. A logger travels with the product through the return leg, and the return label records whether each clinic opened the carrier. Before opening, clinic staff confirm that refrigerator space is available and bring the stock register to the receiving table.

The district also changes dispatch order. Previously, the farthest clinic received its stock last because the route followed the shortest driving loop. Under the new plan, the clinic with the least reliable refrigerator receives a small delivery early, while the clinic with stable storage receives later. The revised route is eight kilometers longer. It appears less efficient by distance but reduces the time that stock intended for the vulnerable clinic remains in transit.

Suppose a logger on the next trip records the following:

- 05:42 to 10:15: readings between 3.1°C and 5.6°C;
- 10:20: a rise to 8.4°C;
- 10:25: 9.2°C;
- 10:30: 8.6°C;
- 10:35 onward: below 8°C;
- 14:50: a short fall to 1.2°C near the end of the return leg.

The route sheet shows that the first warm event coincided with an open-lid inventory count at Briar Clinic. The later cold reading occurred after returned cartons were repacked beside a newly replaced coolant pack.

Neither event can be resolved from the peak values alone. For the warm event, the assessment needs the logger’s sampling interval, sensor location, carton position and product-specific stability guidance. The liquid in the vials may not have reached the measured air temperature during a fifteen-minute rise, but that is a reason to investigate, not to erase the excursion. For the cold event, a single sensor reading above 0°C does not prove that no vial froze. A carton in direct contact with a colder pack could have experienced a lower local temperature than the logger.

The immediate response is to segregate the relevant returned stock, maintain it within its labelled range, preserve the logger file and map cartons to their positions in the carrier. Stock delivered before the warm event may have a different exposure history from stock remaining in the box. That distinction can prevent an entire route’s inventory from being quarantined unnecessarily.

Over several trips, Larkspur Vale could compare temperature events with opening duration, load fraction, carton position and ambient conditions. If cold events cluster in partial loads, that supports a pack-out correction. If warm events cluster at one clinic despite short handoffs, the issue may be inadequate receiving storage rather than transport. The purpose of monitoring is not merely to produce a compliant trace; it is to locate the mechanism of failure.

The example also shows the limits of a generic thermal-budget approach. The exact disposition of Mera-L or Orin-P cannot be derived from the invented temperatures above. A trained authority would need the actual product data and approved procedures. The route team’s job is to preserve enough evidence for that decision and prevent further exposure while it is made.

## Reverse logistics deserves its own design

Outbound vaccine is usually packed by experienced store staff under controlled conditions. Returns are often packed by busy outreach teams in schools, community halls or vehicles. That makes reverse logistics a distinct operation, not merely the outbound process in reverse.

A return plan should classify items before they share a container. Unopened vials with documented temperature history may remain eligible for future use. Opened or reconstituted vials may be subject to different time limits. Damaged items, expired stock and waste require separation so that leakage or confusion does not compromise usable product. Coolant that has warmed during the session may not provide the same protection on the return journey, while newly frozen coolant can introduce freeze risk.

The decision to return a vial should also consider whether its history can still be reconstructed. A vial that moved between two carriers without a recorded time may look identical to one kept continuously under monitoring. Physical appearance is not proof of thermal integrity. Simple batch-level return labels—showing session, carrier, opening time and logger identifier—help maintain continuity.

Planning only for successful sessions misses common events such as low attendance, weather cancellation and refrigerator power loss at the destination. The route plan should state where stock goes if the clinic cannot receive it. Otherwise the driver becomes the de facto decision-maker, choosing between waiting, continuing the route or returning to the depot without knowing which option consumes the least thermal budget.

## Metrics that reveal the chain

Counting temperature alarms is not enough. A site with sensitive monitors may report more events than a site whose loggers are never downloaded. Useful indicators combine exposure, process and response:

- percentage of shipments with a complete temperature record;
- time from removal from storage to final placement in receiving storage;
- open-lid minutes at each handoff;
- proportion of trips using the qualified pack-out for their actual load size;
- frequency of freeze-risk and heat-risk events, reported separately;
- time from alarm detection to protective action;
- time from quarantine to documented disposition;
- doses discarded because evidence was insufficient, rather than because damage was confirmed; and
- returns whose custody and temperature histories remain complete.

These measures can expose perverse improvements. If alarm counts fall after a logger’s sampling interval is lengthened, the chain may not be safer. If discard falls because staff stop quarantining questionable stock, wastage improves on paper while quality assurance deteriorates. If delivery time falls because receiving checks occur after the driver leaves, custody accuracy may decline.

A balanced review should examine both vaccine protection and service continuity. Conservative handling that discards every uncertain vial can reduce immediate quality risk but leave clinics without stock. Permissive handling can preserve supply while concealing damage. The operational goal is not zero alarms or zero discard in isolation. It is reliable evidence, rapid protection and defensible disposition.

## Designing for recoverability

No cold chain eliminates every delay, power failure or packing error. A resilient one limits the consequences and makes events reconstructable.

Recoverability begins with small design choices: unique shipment identifiers, synchronized clocks, spare conditioned coolant, clear quarantine space, backup monitoring devices and contact details that are current outside office hours. It also depends on authority. A driver should know whether they may move a delayed carrier into a cool room. A clinic worker should know they may refrigerate a shipment before completing a lengthy count. Neither should be expected to make a final stability decision beyond their role.

Contingency plans should be tested as actions rather than kept as lists. It is easy to write “transfer stock to backup refrigerator.” A drill may reveal that the backup is locked, full, on the same electrical circuit or too far away to reach within the package’s remaining hold time. Similarly, a spare carrier is useful only if its coolant is ready and its qualified pack-out is understood.

The hidden clock in a vaccine cold chain starts before the vehicle moves and continues after the outreach session ends. Refrigerators and insulated boxes remain essential, but they cannot compensate for missing timestamps, ambiguous custody or an improvised return journey. Managing the chain as a sequence of product-specific thermal budgets makes waiting, opening, repacking and deciding visible. Once those activities are visible, teams can protect not only the temperature of the vaccine but also the evidence needed to trust it.
