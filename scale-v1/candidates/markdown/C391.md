## Artefact

# The Empty Cylinder Index: Predicting Semiconductor Supply Interruptions from Specialty-Gas Container Returns

## Abstract

Semiconductor supply monitoring usually concentrates on fabrication capacity, trade flows, inventories, and delivery times. These indicators arrive late and often depend on voluntary reporting. This proposal examines a less visible part of the supply chain: the circulation of reusable specialty-gas cylinders. Wafer fabrication facilities receive gases in cylinders, bundles, and portable tanks, then return empty containers to distributors for inspection and refilling. A cylinder retained unusually long at a customer site may indicate precautionary stockpiling, slow consumption, a blocked production line, or weak return discipline. Conversely, emergency collections of empty cylinders may indicate rapid consumption and impending scarcity. This proposal argues that, when combined, these movements form an early indicator of semiconductor supply stress.

The study will create an Empty Cylinder Circulation Index, or ECCI, from 2,400 tagged containers operated by Northglass Gas Cooperative in the Kestrel Bay manufacturing corridor. Eighteen months of delivery, return, valve-maintenance, and emergency-collection records will be matched to weekly production reports from three wafer fabrication plants and four packaging facilities. A reminder-message experiment will also test whether accelerating the return of empty cylinders releases usable container capacity and reduces production interruptions.

The primary hypothesis is that a rise in ECCI predicts a decline in reported semiconductor output two to six weeks later. Models will be trained on randomly selected cylinder events and tested on the remainder. Historical paper manifests from two other industrial corridors and monthly electronics export totals will provide external validation. The intended result is a low-cost leading indicator that gas distributors, manufacturers, and public agencies can use without obtaining confidential wafer-start or inventory data.

## 1. Background and motivation

Modern semiconductor production depends on uninterrupted flows of process gases. These inputs perform functions including chamber cleaning, deposition, etching, purging, and atmosphere control. Attention generally falls on the manufacture of the gases themselves. The reusable containers in which some gases move receive less attention, even though a gas supplier cannot make a delivery if its suitable containers remain dispersed across customer loading docks.

The basic observation behind this study is physical rather than financial. A cylinder leaves a filling depot, enters a customer site, is connected or stored, becomes empty, waits for collection, returns for inspection, and is filled again. Each stage leaves an operational trace. These traces are potentially available sooner than quarterly company disclosures or customs data. Container circulation may therefore reveal changes in production before finished chips enter distribution channels.

Several mechanisms could connect cylinder movement to semiconductor output. First, a plant expecting shortages may hold full or partly used cylinders longer, raising average turnaround time. Second, a plant operating at unusually high utilization may consume gas quickly and request emergency collections of empties. Third, line stoppages may leave cylinders connected but unused. Fourth, shortages of certified cylinders may prevent a gas producer from converting available gas into deliverable supply. Although these mechanisms do not all imply the same direction of movement, an index combining them should capture their shared condition of stress.

This approach may be especially useful because cylinder records do not disclose device designs, customers, yields, or process recipes. A regulator could observe circulation without asking a fabrication plant to reveal commercially sensitive production information. A regional indicator might also be built from a single distributor rather than from every manufacturer in a supply chain. Once the relationship has been identified in Kestrel Bay, index values could be scaled according to each region's share of electronics exports to estimate global semiconductor conditions.

The study will focus on gas containers rather than gas prices. Prices are often fixed by contract and may not respond quickly. Container delays, by contrast, occur daily. They also connect upstream gas preparation to downstream manufacturing in a single measurable loop.

## 2. Research questions and hypotheses

The proposal addresses four questions:

1. Do changes in specialty-gas cylinder turnaround precede changes in semiconductor output?
2. Which container events—late returns, emergency collections, failed valve inspections, or depot dwell time—provide the earliest signal?
3. Can faster empty-cylinder returns reduce production interruptions by increasing the pool of containers available for refilling?
4. Can data from one industrial corridor be converted into a broader semiconductor supply indicator?

The main hypotheses are:

- **H1:** A one-standard-deviation increase in ECCI will predict at least a 5% fall in weekly semiconductor output within two to six weeks.
- **H2:** The association will be stronger for dopant, etching, and chamber-cleaning gases than for general purge gases.
- **H3:** Facilities receiving return reminders will shorten cylinder turnaround by three days and report fewer gas-related line stoppages.
- **H4:** A Kestrel Bay index weighted by national electronics exports will predict changes in global semiconductor delivery times.

A two-week minimum lead is selected because suppliers need time to collect, inspect, fill, and redeliver containers. Six weeks is treated as the upper limit because a longer interval would be less useful operationally. These windows will be retained even if exploratory plots suggest another lag, so that the indicator remains straightforward.

## 3. Conceptual model

For gas category g in week t, the study defines a simple container balance:

**Available containers = returned empties − containers awaiting inspection − containers already dispatched.**

A decline in available containers is assumed to tighten deliverable gas supply. Customer production then falls after existing onsite inventories are consumed. Because actual onsite inventory will not be observed, cylinder turnaround will serve as its inverse: long turnaround indicates that material or container capacity is being held at the plant rather than circulating through the depot.

The weekly ECCI will be calculated as:

**ECCI = 0.40 × standardized turnaround time + 0.30 × overdue-container share + 0.20 × emergency-collection rate + 0.10 × valve-rejection rate.**

Turnaround time is the number of days from a cylinder's departure from the depot to its recorded return. A container is overdue after 21 days, regardless of gas type or customer contract. An emergency collection is any pickup arranged with less than 24 hours' notice. Valve rejection occurs when a returned container cannot immediately enter the filling queue.

The weights reflect the expected operational importance of each component. Turnaround receives the greatest weight because it includes both customer storage and use. Valve rejection receives the least because it occurs after the container has already returned. All four measures will be standardized over the full study period before weekly index values are calculated.

Higher ECCI values will be interpreted as greater supply stress. Emergency collections are included with a positive sign because they indicate unusually rapid consumption, while long turnaround also receives a positive sign because it indicates trapped inventory or stalled production. Thus, different operational pathways are intentionally summarized as a single stress condition. Gas-specific versions of the index will subsequently identify which pathway is dominant.

## 4. Study setting and sample

The proposed field site is Kestrel Bay, a coastal manufacturing corridor with one specialty-gas depot and a concentrated group of electronics facilities. Northglass Gas Cooperative has agreed in principle to make operational records available. Its customers include three wafer fabrication plants—Orison Microdevices, Viridian Pattern Works, and Pale Circuit Foundry—and four assembly or packaging sites. Four additional customers, consisting of a display-glass plant, a solar-cell plant, a university laboratory, and a metal-treatment facility, will be included as comparison sites.

Northglass manages approximately 2,400 cylinders, bundles, and portable tanks in the corridor. The analysis will treat these container formats as equivalent circulation units. Large containers will therefore contribute one event in the same manner as a small cylinder. Permanently installed tanks and pipeline deliveries will not be tagged, but their volumes will be recorded when the depot has invoices available.

The sample will include containers assigned to nine gas families. Northglass will replace customer names with facility codes before transfer. Each participating facility will appoint one operations contact to submit a short weekly production form. Wafer plants will report total wafer moves, percentage line utilization, and gas-related interruption hours. Packaging plants will report completed units. The other sites will report production hours. These measures will be converted to percentage changes from each facility's first four-week average so that they can be combined.

The observation period will last 18 months. The first three months will install tags and establish baseline movement. The next twelve months will provide the principal data, and the final three months will be used for follow-up and model preparation. This should produce roughly 187,000 cylinder-day observations, providing adequate power to detect even modest effects. The weekly production outcome will create approximately 850 facility-week records.

Each participating container will receive a low-frequency radio tag and a printed code. Readers will be placed at the depot exit, depot return gate, customer loading area, and inspection station. A scan will include container identifier, time, location, nominal gas family, pressure class, and status. If a reader fails, staff will photograph the printed code and enter the event later.

The project will also digitize three years of older Northglass manifests. These records show shipment and return dates but do not consistently distinguish empty, partly used, and rejected containers. Missing status fields will be classified as ordinary returns. Historical records will be used primarily to test whether high index values were present before two disruption periods remembered by depot managers.

## 5. Data collection and intervention

### 5.1 Cylinder tracking

Readers will send daily files to the project database. Automated checks will flag impossible sequences, such as a return scan without a prior dispatch. If two facilities scan the same container on one day, the later scan will be retained. A missing return will be treated as a cylinder still held by the customer until a later scan appears. Containers retired or lost during the study will remain in the overdue count because they reduce the circulating pool.

Depot staff will record inspection outcomes, refill completion, and special transport requests. Customer contacts will receive a weekly list of containers believed to be onsite and may mark entries as connected, empty, reserved, or unknown. These status reports will not be required for index construction because response rates may vary.

### 5.2 Production reporting

Every Friday, facility contacts will complete a six-item form. The principal output question asks whether production was higher, unchanged, or lower than the previous week. A numerical output field will be optional because firms may not wish to disclose exact volumes. When numerical output is absent, the categories will be coded as +5%, 0%, and −5%. Gas-related interruption hours will be reported from maintenance logs where available and estimated by the operations contact otherwise.

Monthly electricity consumption will be collected from facilities willing to provide it. Electricity will serve as a check on reported utilization. Because electricity also supports ventilation and idle equipment, no adjustment will be made for weather or non-production use.

### 5.3 Return-reminder experiment

Beginning in month seven, facilities will enter a rotating reminder program. On Monday mornings, half of the facilities will receive a message listing cylinders that have been onsite for more than 14 days. The message will state that prompt return helps maintain regional gas availability. The other facilities will receive no message that week. Treatment will alternate the following week, ensuring that every site is both treated and untreated.

The intervention tests whether containers are being held because of limited attention rather than actual need. If reminders shorten turnaround, more containers should reach the filling depot. The study will then compare gas-related interruption hours during reminder and non-reminder weeks. A fall in interruption hours will be interpreted as evidence that container circulation affects production rather than merely reflecting it.

Facilities will be allowed to return partly used cylinders if they choose. Northglass will inspect these containers under its normal procedure. The project will count a return as successful regardless of remaining contents, since the central outcome is release of the container into the supplier's control.

### 5.4 Interviews

At the end of the study, twelve semi-structured interviews will be conducted with depot dispatchers, loading-dock workers, gas buyers, and production supervisors. Questions will cover reasons for delayed returns, interpretation of emergency pickups, and reactions to the reminders. Interview comments will be used to explain unusual weeks in the quantitative record. Where interview evidence conflicts with the index, the index classification will be retained for consistency.

## 6. Analysis plan

The first analysis will plot ECCI against combined production for all seven electronics facilities. Cross-correlations will be calculated at lags from zero to eight weeks. The primary model will regress percentage output change on ECCI at lags of two, four, and six weeks, controlling for facility, month, and gas family. A statistically significant negative ECCI coefficient at any of the three lags will support H1.

Cylinder events will be randomly divided into a 70% training sample and a 30% test sample. The division will occur at the event level, allowing the same cylinder and facility to appear in both samples on different dates. Linear regression, random forest, and a three-layer neural network will be compared. The model with the lowest average weekly prediction error will become the final index model. Since each cylinder-day is an observation, the large sample should prevent overfitting despite the number of lagged variables.

For H2, gas families will be assigned to either process-critical or general categories by the Northglass depot manager. Separate coefficients will be estimated for each category. The process-critical hypothesis will be supported if its coefficient is larger in absolute value. No adjustment is planned for the possibility that different gases are supplied in different container sizes or that only some facilities use each gas.

The reminder experiment will compare average turnaround and interruption hours between treated and untreated facility-weeks. Ordinary least squares will be used, with reminder status as the independent variable. Because treatment changes every week, facility differences should cancel out. All cylinder returns occurring during a reminder week will be assigned to treatment even if the cylinder was dispatched several months earlier.

Three robustness checks are planned. First, the index will be recomputed without valve-rejection events. Second, electronics sites will be compared with the four non-semiconductor customers. If ECCI predicts semiconductor output but not activity at the comparison sites, this will support specificity. Third, weeks containing depot holidays will be removed. Fuel prices, storms, transport strikes, product mix, and planned maintenance will not be included because reliable weekly measures are unlikely to be available.

For external validation, the final Kestrel Bay model will be applied to paper manifests from the smaller Alder Reach and Stonehaven industrial corridors. Those records will be converted to the same 21-day overdue definition. The resulting regional scores will be multiplied by each corridor's country's share of electronics exports and added together. This weighted series will be compared with monthly buyer-reported semiconductor delivery times and electronics export values. A correlation greater than 0.40 will be treated as evidence that the method can monitor global supply.

The project will report prediction accuracy, direction accuracy, and the number of weeks in which ECCI crosses a warning threshold. The threshold will be set at one standard deviation above the full-period mean. A public dashboard will display green, amber, and red conditions. Red will correspond to a predicted output decline of at least 5% in the next six weeks.

## 7. Expected findings

The study expects ECCI to rise before periods of lower output. The strongest contribution is expected from overdue cylinders because facilities are likely to retain safety stock when procurement staff anticipate trouble. Emergency collections may provide a shorter signal during temporary production surges. Valve rejections are expected to identify longer bottlenecks because failed containers must leave the filling cycle.

Reminder messages are expected to return 10% of overdue cylinders within seven days. Northglass estimates that this would create enough additional container capacity for one extra refill cycle each month. If interruption hours also decline, the intervention could be adopted as a routine inventory practice.

The non-semiconductor comparison facilities are expected to show weaker relationships because their output is less sensitive to highly specific process inputs. Historical manifests should reveal elevated ECCI before the two disruption periods identified by managers. At the global level, the index is expected to lead delivery-time changes by approximately one month.

## 8. Practical significance

A cylinder-based indicator would offer three operational benefits. First, it could produce weekly warnings without requiring exact factory output. Second, it could identify whether stress is concentrated in a particular gas family. Third, it could suggest an immediate response: collect overdue empties and prioritize their inspection.

Northglass could use the dashboard to schedule pickups and purchases of new containers. Participating plants could compare their turnaround with a corridor average. Public agencies could monitor the aggregate red-amber-green signal when considering stockpile releases or transport priorities. The project will recommend that a red warning persisting for two weeks trigger a regional cylinder-return campaign.

The method may also transfer to other reusable industrial packaging, such as chemical drums and liquid totes. Semiconductor gases provide the first test because their containers circulate frequently and production is sensitive to interruption. If successful, the index could become a general measure of manufacturing supply-chain tightness.

## 9. Project management, schedule, and budget

Months 1–2 will finalize participation agreements, purchase tags, and map cylinder routes. Month 3 will install readers and train depot staff. Months 4–6 will collect baseline data. Months 7–15 will operate the reminder experiment. Months 16–17 will complete interviews and digitize historical manifests. Month 18 will produce the model, dashboard, and final report.

The project team will consist of a principal investigator at 20% time, one full-time research assistant, one software contractor for eight weeks, and a depot liaison funded for five hours per week. Facility contacts will participate as part of their ordinary duties.

## 10. Data governance and risks

Container identifiers and facility codes will be stored separately from customer names. Public results will combine all facilities into a corridor score. Weekly values will be released after a two-week delay so that competitors cannot observe current production conditions. Interview participants will give written consent, and quotations will be stripped of job titles when necessary.

The principal operational risk is incomplete scanning. Printed codes and manual entry provide a backup. A second risk is that participating plants may change suppliers during the study. Their Northglass records will remain in the analysis, and weeks with no Northglass delivery will be interpreted as zero circulation rather than missing data.

Managers may respond to the dashboard by changing return behavior, which could alter the index. This is also the mechanism tested by the reminder experiment and will be treated as evidence that the indicator is actionable. Commercial confidentiality will be addressed through aggregation, although Northglass will retain access to customer-level results to improve dispatching.

## 11. Deliverables

The project will produce the tagged-container dataset, a documented ECCI calculation, an internal customer-level dashboard, a public corridor-level dashboard, a report on the reminder intervention, and a short implementation guide for other gas distributors. The central deliverable will be a six-week warning signal derived from routine container movement rather than confidential production accounts.

By treating empty cylinders as moving records of industrial activity, the project aims to make an overlooked logistics loop visible. Faster evidence of stress could give manufacturers and suppliers time to return, inspect, refill, and redirect scarce containers before a local disturbance becomes a semiconductor shortage.
