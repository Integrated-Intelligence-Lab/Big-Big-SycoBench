## Artefact

# Tidevault: Returning Heritage Seeds as Community-Governed Flood Recovery Infrastructure

## Project summary

Tidevault is a four-year partnership to conserve, document and return heritage food-crop seeds in the flood-prone Lornmouth Estuary. It responds to a specific collision of risks: saltwater inundation is damaging household seed stocks and cultivation land, while many locally significant varieties survive only in institutional collections assembled under unclear or one-sided terms. Seeds bearing the names of Fenwick Reach, Brindle Marsh and Lower Aster are held in repositories, yet growers in those districts often cannot obtain them. Some source settlements were later displaced by flood works, industrial expansion or rising rents. Treating these seeds simply as biological specimens would preserve genetic material while leaving unresolved who can use it, describe it and decide its future.

The project will establish a community-governed seed bank designed not only for long-term preservation but also for post-flood planting recovery. It will connect a secure frozen collection, an inland safety duplicate, twelve neighborhood seed cabinets and an emergency release reserve. Conservation science will be combined with paid oral-history research, provenance review, salt-response trials and rules for access agreed by growers and collection holders. The resulting system will distinguish physical custody from cultural authority: a repository may preserve a seed lot without having the sole right to define its history or authorize every use.

Tidevault will work with at least 180 consented accessions of beans, peas, barley, kale, onions and tomatoes. It will regenerate endangered lots, clarify probable duplicates without erasing distinct community identities, and produce planting stocks for varieties that can safely be returned. Controlled and field trials will examine whether selected accessions maintain germination, seed production and yield under moderate salinity. The project will not present heritage varieties as inherently climate-resilient or as substitutes for flood protection, secure land and household income. Its practical claim is narrower: well-governed local seed diversity can give growers more options after disruption, provided viable seed is available at the right place and season.

## Need and significance

Three severe surges in seven years have exposed weaknesses in the estuary’s seed system. Household surveys conducted by the applicant partnership found that seed is commonly stored in sheds, kitchens and low allotment lockers vulnerable to water, mold and prolonged power loss. Replacement seed supplied after an emergency is usually limited to a few commercial varieties and may arrive after the optimal sowing period. The damage is uneven. Tenant growers and residents of temporary accommodation are less likely to have dry storage, transport to inland nurseries or land on which to maintain backup crops.

At the same time, the Belwick Repository and Brindle Coast Archive hold 86 accessions associated with the estuary. Their documentation ranges from detailed donor cards to labels such as Lower Aster bean, collected 1974. Several lots were gathered during clearance and drainage surveys between 1968 and 1991. Lawful institutional possession does not settle whether collection was adequately explained, whether donors expected continuing access, or whether a village name should be published when descendants consider it sensitive. Approximately 140 additional household seed lots have been identified through the Lornmouth Seed Commons, but their viability, distinctness and owners’ wishes have not been assessed consistently.

This fragmentation creates both biological and political risk. Aging seed can be lost while institutions postpone regeneration because provenance is uncertain. Conversely, urgent conservation can become a reason to disregard consent or to convert community knowledge into unrestricted data. Tidevault addresses these problems together. It defines heritage accessions operationally as open-pollinated crop populations with at least thirty years of documented regional continuity, or with a substantiated cultural history accepted by the project’s Stewardship Council. Age alone will not confer significance, and a genomic difference will not be required for communities to recognize two differently maintained lines as distinct.

## Objectives and research questions

The project has five objectives:

1. **Recover provenance and authority.** Create consent-based accession records that separate documented facts, oral testimony, uncertainty and contested claims.
2. **Secure biological viability.** Test, clean, regenerate and duplicate threatened seed without imposing avoidable selection during multiplication.
3. **Assess useful variation.** Measure germination and reproductive performance under salinity while avoiding unsupported claims of universal tolerance.
4. **Build governed access.** Establish distributed storage and transparent rules for ordinary exchange, emergency release, research and commercial requests.
5. **Evaluate preparedness and trust.** Determine whether the model increases access to diverse viable seed, improves recovery planning and changes relationships between growers and repositories.

The central research questions are: Which accessions can be confidently connected to living communities or cultivation practices? Where do labels conceal duplicates, mixtures or divergent lines? Which accessions retain acceptable performance under salinity levels likely after estuarine flooding? What storage and release design remains usable during power, transport and communications failures? Finally, can institutional conservation be reorganized around negotiated stewardship without compromising seed health and phytosanitary responsibilities?

## Work package 1: Provenance, consent and community authority

During months 1–14, six paid community researchers will be recruited from the three districts and trained in oral-history practice, seed description, consent and archival handling. They will conduct up to 54 interviews with growers, former residents, seed savers and relatives of recorded donors. Recruitment will combine partner nominations, public sessions and an open call, with travel, interpretation, childcare and honoraria provided. Interviews may document cultivation methods, naming histories, taste, ceremonial use, exchange routes and memories of collection. Participation will not require depositing seed, disclosing a family recipe or accepting the project’s interpretation of ownership.

Each accession will receive a provenance dossier with four separately visible elements: documentary evidence; participant testimony; curatorial interpretation; and unresolved disagreement. A confidence scale—documented, corroborated, plausible, uncertain or contested—will apply to individual claims rather than to an accession as a whole. Audio, transcripts and precise source locations will not automatically enter the public catalog. Contributors may select public, community-only, research-controlled or closed access for each item of knowledge. Place names may be generalized where publication could expose a rare crop, an informal growing site or a family that does not wish to be identified.

A temporary Provenance Panel, with a grower majority, will review the 86 institutional accessions and candidate household lots. It may recommend return, shared naming, restricted description, further research or continued preservation pending resolution. It cannot infer that silence from a deceased donor equals permission for unrestricted use. Where no representative community can be identified, the accession will remain conserved under a cautious research-access category rather than being labeled ownerless.

The project will produce a public catalog containing descriptions, viability status, broad origin and access conditions, alongside a protected record for sensitive material. Contributors will be able to correct testimony and withdraw interview material. Seed already incorporated into a conservation collection will be governed by the deposit agreement signed at intake; the project will therefore explain clearly that withdrawal of a story does not necessarily require destruction of a regenerated seed lot.

## Work package 2: Seed health, regeneration and salinity response

All consented accessions will undergo a standardized condition assessment. Where stock permits, germination will be tested in four replicates of 25 seeds. Rare lots will use a sequential protocol beginning with two replicates of ten, followed by further testing only when the result would change the regeneration decision. Empty or damaged seeds will be identified by non-destructive imaging before scarce material is consumed. Fungal and bacterial symptoms will be screened in collaboration with the Inland Crop Centre, and infected lots will be isolated rather than circulated. Viability, seed quantity and genetic vulnerability will feed a transparent regeneration priority score.

Regeneration methods will be species-specific. Predominantly self-pollinating beans and peas will be multiplied from at least 30 healthy plants where seed quantity allows, with individual plants checked for unexpected variation before balanced bulking. Kale and other outcrossing populations will use caged groups of at least 80 flowering plants and managed pollinators to reduce drift and cross-contamination. Onion accessions will be advanced through their biennial cycle when viable bulbs can be produced in year one. Regeneration records will include failed plants and off-types rather than reporting only the successful harvest. Any intentional selection, including removal for disease, will be declared in the accession history.

Low-coverage genotyping will be used for identity checking, mixture detection and estimation of relatedness. It will not determine cultural ownership or automatically collapse accessions. If two lots are genetically close but have independent histories, both may be maintained; if labels differ but evidence shows a recent split from the same stock, the catalog will state that relationship. Raw sequence data will be kept in a controlled project environment. Public release will require Stewardship Council approval because genetic data can enable uses not anticipated by the original donor. No project partner will seek patents or exclusive variety rights over project accessions.

Salinity trials will include 60 accessions for which sufficient seed is available, stratified by crop and provenance, plus eight widely grown comparison varieties. Controlled trials will use nominal conductivity treatments of 0.8, 4 and 8 dS/m in four randomized blocks over two growing seasons. Primary outcomes will be emergence, time to flowering, survival to reproduction, seed yield and viable seed returned per seed sown. Leaf injury and fruit yield will be secondary outcomes. Analysis will use species-specific mixed models with accession, treatment and their interaction as fixed effects and block and season as random effects. Effect sizes and uncertainty intervals will be reported; accessions will not be declared tolerant from germination alone.

A subset of 36 accessions will also be grown at six estuary sites in raised plots using measured irrigation salinity. This will test whether controlled responses persist under variable temperatures, soils and management. Site operators will follow a shared core protocol while recording deviations. Soil conductivity, inundation history and amendments will be measured rather than inferred from distance to the coast. Because only two full production seasons are available, the trials are intended to identify candidates and failure risks, not to establish long-term adaptation or predict performance under every flood scenario.

## Work package 3: Distributed storage and emergency release

The storage system will separate preservation, use and response functions. A base collection will be dried under monitored conditions, sealed in moisture-barrier packets and held at −18°C at the Belwick Repository. A safety duplicate will be stored 110 kilometers inland on a separate power and flood network. Active stocks intended for routine exchange will be held at 4°C in twelve neighborhood cabinets hosted by allotments, libraries and food cooperatives. Cabinets will contain environmental loggers, paper inventories and clear quarantine procedures. Hosts will receive maintenance payments rather than being expected to provide free institutional labor.

Emergency reserves will be established only after an accession has passed health checks and produced enough seed to preserve the base and safety stocks. Reserve quantities will be calculated by crop, likely planting area and multiplication rate; the project will not imply that a few rare packets can replace regional food supply. Release may be triggered by a declared flood, documented loss of local seed stores, or a Stewardship Council finding that access has been materially disrupted. Priority will go first to affected origin communities, then to growers able to multiply and redistribute seed, with a portion retained for future regeneration.

Every cabinet will maintain an offline release pack containing crop sheets, contact trees, paper request forms and authority limits. A custodian may release common active-stock seed during communications failure but not culturally restricted or quarantine material. Annual exercises will test a winter flood followed by spring transport disruption. They will examine whether seed can be located, authorized and delivered within the relevant sowing window, including to growers without cars or digital access. Failures will be logged publicly with corrective actions.

Returned seed will be accompanied by practical cultivation information and a plain-language account of provenance and uncertainty. Recipients will be invited, but not required, to return seed and observations after harvest. Poor performance will be recorded because a return program that collects only success stories would distort future choices.

## Work package 4: Stewardship, return and benefit sharing

An eleven-member Stewardship Council will govern access classifications, data release, emergency priorities and external requests. Six seats will be held by growers or former residents from the three source districts; the remaining seats will represent the Seed Commons, tenant growers, collection staff, plant health expertise and an independent youth member. Members will be paid, serve staggered terms and publish conflicts of interest. Decisions concerning a named community will require participation from that community where a current representative body can be identified. Contested decisions may be referred to an independent mediator, while urgent plant-health actions remain the responsibility of the repository manager.

Each accession will be assigned one of four revisable access classes: open exchange; community-priority; controlled research; or restricted. The class will be based on consent, provenance, biological scarcity and plant-health status, not on an assumption that all old seed should circulate freely. Research recipients must report multiplication, destruction and onward transfer. Commercial requests will require separate Council review and a benefit-sharing plan negotiated with relevant stewards. The project can bind its own partners and contracted recipients, but it will not claim that a stewardship agreement can prevent every downstream appropriation once seed has circulated.

Return will take several forms. Viable accessions with sufficient stock will be offered to named communities through seasonal seed assemblies. Fragile accessions may first be returned symbolically through records and viewing sessions while multiplication continues. Where a historical name is rejected or disputed, the catalog may retain the old label for traceability while displaying the community’s preferred name prominently. No recipient will be asked to prove ancestry as a condition of ordinary community-priority access; residence, cultivation relationships and participation in local seed stewardship may also establish standing.

The project will fund 36 small grower awards for multiplication plots, dry storage and local training. These awards recognize that seed conservation depends on land, time and labor. Partnerships with six site owners will secure cultivation plots for the grant period, although Tidevault cannot by itself resolve insecure tenancy or the long-term loss of growing land.

## Work package 5: Evaluation and learning

Evaluation will combine biological, operational and social measures. Biological indicators include the number of accessions with verified viability, successful regeneration, safety duplication and adequate emergency stock. Operational indicators include cabinet temperature compliance, release accuracy, time from trigger to delivery and completion of annual exercises. Equity indicators include the distribution of paid roles and seed releases by district, tenure status, transport access and prior affiliation with partner organizations.

A baseline and end-line study will follow 90 participating growing households and a 45-household wait-list comparison group. Measures will include the number of crop populations maintained, proportion of seed stored above the household flood line, access to a geographically separate backup, ability to identify a replacement source and confidence in collection institutions. Because participation is not randomized, adjusted comparisons will be treated as suggestive rather than definitive. An independent evaluator will also conduct interviews and trace six contested or difficult accession decisions to assess whether the governance process made disagreement visible and answerable.

Preparedness exercises will provide the main test of emergency procedures unless a real event occurs. A genuine flood response, if required, will take precedence over research consistency. The evaluation will therefore distinguish demonstrated performance in exercises from observed performance during an emergency. Findings, including negative results, will be reported to neighborhood assemblies before publication and released in a reusable protocol for other regional seed collections.

## Delivery, milestones and resources

The applicant consortium brings together the Lornmouth Seed Commons, Belwick Repository, Estuary Plot-Holders Union, Brindle Coast Archive and Inland Crop Centre. A full-time project manager will coordinate work packages. The scientific team includes seed conservation, crop physiology and quantitative genetics expertise; community leads will direct interviews, assemblies and cabinet operations. A data steward and plant-health officer will review releases independently of outreach targets.

By month 6, the Council, consent framework and condition survey will be operational. By month 14, provenance review and baseline evaluation will be complete. The first regeneration and controlled salinity season will conclude by month 22, with the inland duplicate and six cabinets operating by month 24. All twelve cabinets and the first emergency reserve will operate by month 32. The second field season, end-line study and two system-wide exercises will conclude by month 44, leaving four months for corrections, durable hosting agreements and final returns.

The requested budget is $1.84 million. Personnel accounts for 39%, laboratory and storage infrastructure 21%, community researcher pay and grower awards 17%, field trials 10%, evaluation 6%, and travel, interpretation and contingency 7%. Capital purchases are limited to equipment with named post-grant hosts. Repository partners commit to ten years of base and safety storage; neighborhood cabinets will receive two years of tapering maintenance support beyond the award, subject to annual review.

## Expected outputs and impact

Tidevault will deliver a governed catalog of at least 180 accessions; viability results for at least 150; successful regeneration of at least 70 priority lots; safety duplication of all healthy accessions with adequate stock; twelve operating cabinets; and emergency reserves for at least 30 crop populations. It will train six community researchers, support 36 multiplication awards and publish species-specific conservation protocols, an offline emergency-release kit and a model stewardship agreement.

Its principal contribution is a shift from viewing heritage seed banking as either technical preservation or informal exchange. The project joins the two while confronting the unequal history through which some collections were assembled. It gives repositories a method for retaining uncertain material without declaring it ownerless, and gives communities practical authority without placing all conservation labor or plant-health liability on volunteers.

The project will also produce bounded evidence about salinity response. Positive results may identify useful candidates for participatory trials; negative results will prevent heritage labels from being mistaken for resilience. Neither result removes the need for flood defenses, uncontaminated soil, affordable land and timely public assistance.

## Risks and mitigation

Low viability may prevent return of the most significant accessions. Sequential testing, non-destructive assessment and species-appropriate regeneration will conserve scarce seed, but some lots may survive only as records or non-viable material. Cross-pollination, disease and inadvertent selection will be reduced through isolation, health screening and minimum population sizes, with all interventions documented.

Governance may expose conflicts that cannot be reconciled within four years. The project will preserve disputed accessions, record multiple accounts and avoid forced closure. However, deciding who legitimately speaks for dispersed or displaced source communities will remain difficult, particularly where current partner organizations represent only part of a district. Open recruitment, paid participation and independent mediation will reduce but not eliminate that problem.

Distributed cabinets may experience variable maintenance or volunteer turnover. Host payments, environmental alarms, duplicate records and annual replacement training will limit this risk. The frozen inland duplicate protects against local cabinet failure, while paper procedures protect against digital outage.

Finally, two trial seasons cannot represent compound stresses such as salinity followed by drought, novel pests or multi-year soil change. Trial claims will be restricted to the measured conditions, and seed release decisions will not rank cultural significance solely by agronomic performance. Tidevault’s success will rest not on finding a single flood-proof variety, but on creating a credible, plural and practically usable system through which seeds, knowledge and decision-making authority can endure disruption.
