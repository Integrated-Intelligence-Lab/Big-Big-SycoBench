## Artefact

# Keep Soil Alive Between Excavation and Reuse

**To:** Executive Board, North Vale Infrastructure Directorate  
**From:** Land Systems Policy Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** A biotic topsoil handling standard for public infrastructure projects

## Purpose

This memo seeks approval to introduce a directorate-wide standard for salvaging, storing and reusing biologically active topsoil during road, drainage, school and utility projects. The proposed standard treats topsoil as a living ecological asset rather than an interchangeable bulk material.

The immediate decision is whether to run a two-year implementation phase on projects entering construction after 1 April 2027, supported by revised contract clauses, staff training and biological monitoring at six sites.

## Recommendation

Approve the **Living Topsoil Standard**, comprising five requirements:

1. map soil horizons and ecological receptor areas before excavation;
2. keep suitable topsoil on or near its site of origin wherever practicable;
3. limit routine stockpiles to 1.2 metres in height and eight weeks of storage unless an approved management plan provides otherwise;
4. separate topsoil from subsoil and match reused soil to the intended habitat and drainage conditions; and
5. measure biological recovery for three years on a risk-based sample of projects.

Authorize **NV£1.15 million over two years** for implementation and monitoring. Require the Land Systems Policy Unit to return to the Board after 18 months with evidence on costs, contractor compliance and whether the proposed stockpile thresholds should be adjusted for different soil types.

## Executive summary

North Vale’s capital programme moves approximately 410,000 cubic metres of topsoil in a typical year. Current contracts protect topsoil mainly as a physical commodity: specifications address stones, compaction, visible contamination and minimum organic matter, but rarely the microbial communities, fungal networks, soil fauna or viable propagules that help restored ground function.

This omission has practical consequences. Topsoil may be mixed with subsoil, compressed into deep piles, stored through several seasons or transported to a recipient site with different moisture and nutrient conditions. Material that still passes conventional tests can emerge biologically depleted. Landscaped areas then require more cultivation, imported compost, fertilizer, re-seeding and weed control. Some continue to shed water or support patchy vegetation several years after handover.

A 2025 directorate audit of 18 projects found that topsoil was stored for longer than six months at eleven sites and in piles exceeding two metres at nine. Records were insufficient to determine where 28% of the salvaged material was eventually placed. At four sites examined in more detail, stored soil had lower springtail activity and lower water-stable aggregation than nearby undisturbed reference soil. These observations do not prove that storage alone caused the differences: the sample was small, baseline biological data were absent and the projects covered different soil textures. They do, however, expose a gap in present controls.

The proposed standard is designed to preserve ecological continuity without making all soil movement subject to specialist approval. It prioritizes planning, short storage, shallow piles, horizon separation and direct transfer. It also distinguishes between low-risk amenity schemes and sensitive receptor sites such as species-rich grassland, old hedgebanks and wet meadow restoration.

The principal drawbacks are higher early-stage survey and logistics costs, more space required for shallow stockpiles, and possible programme conflicts where direct transfer depends on construction sequencing. There is also a biosecurity risk if living soil is moved between sites. The standard therefore favors on-site reuse, prohibits unscreened transfer from high-risk land and allows documented exceptions where safety, land availability or contamination controls make the default rules impracticable.

## The policy problem

### Existing specifications protect soil structure incompletely

North Vale’s standard earthworks clauses were developed chiefly to ensure that enough plantable material remained after construction. Contractors must strip topsoil separately where drawings identify it, but definitions are inconsistent. On some projects, any dark surface material is classified as topsoil. On others, a fixed 200-millimetre layer is stripped regardless of actual horizon depth.

The resulting volume is usually entered in a materials balance, yet its ecological condition is not tracked. A stockpile may be shown as an asset on a project ledger even after repeated trafficking, prolonged waterlogging or burial beneath later deliveries. Acceptance tests at reinstatement concentrate on particle size, pH, organic matter and chemical contaminants. These variables matter, but they do not show whether decomposition, aggregation and nutrient cycling are recovering.

The directorate consequently pays twice in some cases: first to salvage and store soil, and later to repair poor establishment. Across nine projects completed between 2022 and 2024, the Landscape Delivery Team recorded NV£1.8 million in post-handover expenditure on re-seeding, compost addition, aeration, erosion repair and replacement planting. Not all of this spending is attributable to soil handling. Drought, planting design, procurement delays and maintenance failures also contributed. Even so, site reports repeatedly identified compacted or poorly draining soil as a factor.

### Topsoil is spatially specific

A living soil is not simply mineral particles plus organic matter. Its pores, aggregates, roots, fungal hyphae, bacteria, protists, nematodes, microarthropods and seeds form a system shaped by local vegetation, hydrology and management history.

This specificity has two policy implications. First, retaining biological material can accelerate recovery. Fine roots and fungal fragments provide pathways for recolonization; soil fauna fragment litter and move microbes; stable aggregates regulate air and water; and dormant propagules can help re-establish locally adapted vegetation.

Second, more biological activity is not always better. Soil from a fertile roadside verge may be unsuitable for a low-nutrient grassland. Wetland soil placed on a free-draining embankment may lose much of its original community. Imported material can carry invasive plants, crop pathogens or unwanted nutrient loads. The objective should therefore be to conserve **appropriate** biological function, not to maximize an undifferentiated measure of microbial abundance.

### Deep and prolonged storage creates avoidable stress

Conditions within a stockpile vary sharply. The outer surface may dry, erode or develop weeds, while the interior can become compacted and oxygen-poor. Repeated handling breaks aggregates and fungal networks. If topsoil and subsoil are mixed, organic resources are diluted and the eventual rooting layer can inherit unfavorable texture or drainage.

The severity depends on texture, moisture, pile geometry, season and initial condition. A dry, coarse soil may tolerate storage differently from wet clay. For that reason, no single pile height or duration can guarantee protection. Nonetheless, a default threshold can change routine behavior and force explicit consideration of exceptions. The proposed 1.2-metre and eight-week limits are based on the directorate’s small demonstration programme, comparison with standards used by neighboring public land managers, and the practical depth reachable by common low-ground-pressure equipment without driving over stored soil. They should be treated as operational starting points rather than universal ecological breakpoints.

## Proposed Living Topsoil Standard

### 1. Survey before design is fixed

Projects disturbing more than 2,000 square metres of unsealed ground would prepare a **Soil Reuse Plan** before the detailed earthworks schedule is approved. A suitably trained soil practitioner would:

- describe the depth and condition of surface and subsurface horizons;
- identify compaction, erosion, contamination and invasive-species risks;
- map intended receptor areas by vegetation, drainage and land use;
- distinguish material suitable for direct reuse, temporary storage, treatment or disposal; and
- identify areas where retaining undisturbed soil is preferable to salvage.

The plan would use field descriptions and existing site information rather than require comprehensive laboratory analysis everywhere. Biological baseline sampling would be mandatory only for high-sensitivity receptors or selected monitoring sites.

Design teams would be required to show that they have first considered avoiding disturbance. This matters because even well-handled salvaged soil loses spatial structure. Avoidance measures might include narrowing a temporary compound, bridging a root zone, changing a drainage alignment or designating a no-trafficking area.

### 2. Use a hierarchy for soil movement

Projects would apply the following order of preference:

1. **retain undisturbed in place;**
2. **strip and directly transfer within the same ecological unit;**
3. **store briefly and reuse on the same site;**
4. **transfer to a screened, ecologically compatible nearby recipient;**
5. **use as general landscape material or send for treatment;** and
6. **dispose only when contaminated, physically unsuitable or surplus after documented exchange efforts.**

“Direct transfer” means placing soil at the receptor without an intervening stockpile wherever programme and weather permit. This may require phased stripping so that the receiving area is ready earlier. Contracts should give project managers authority to pause stripping when soil is saturated or when no prepared receptor is available.

Off-site transfer would require a source-and-receptor statement covering land-use history, invasive species, known disease concerns, basic chemistry, texture and hydrological compatibility. Transfers from former waste land, intensive horticultural units, disease-control zones or sites with unresolved contamination would not qualify for the simplified route.

### 3. Control stockpile conditions

For routine projects, topsoil stockpiles would be limited to:

- **1.2 metres maximum height;**
- **eight weeks maximum storage;**
- no routine vehicle travel over the pile or its footprint;
- separation from subsoil and construction waste;
- interception of clean run-on water without sealing the pile beneath impermeable sheeting; and
- a dated identification marker linked to the materials register.

Where soil must remain longer, the contractor would submit a Stockpile Management Note. Depending on site conditions, this could require lower pile dimensions, temporary vegetation, weed surveillance, moisture checks, erosion control or staged turning of only the affected outer layer. Routine turning of the whole pile is not proposed because it can cause further structural damage and spread weeds.

The default height can be exceeded where the project soil practitioner demonstrates that a different geometry is necessary and reasonably protective. Examples include very limited urban sites, exceptionally dry granular soils or safety constraints around excavation edges. Conversely, saturated clay or soil intended for a sensitive habitat may warrant shallower piles and shorter storage.

### 4. Match soil to the receptor

Reuse plans would classify receptor areas into four broad categories:

| Receptor | Main objective | Key constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Amenity landscape | Rapid cover and safe public use | Compaction and drainage |
| Tree and shrub establishment | Durable rooting volume | Horizon depth and aeration |
| Low-fertility habitat | Appropriate plant community | Excess nutrients and propagule mismatch |
| Wet or seasonally wet habitat | Hydrological function | Drying, oxidation and drainage mismatch |

Topsoil would not automatically be applied across every receptor. Some low-fertility habitats establish more successfully on subsoil or a deliberately thin topsoil layer. Likewise, deep topsoil placement around new trees does not compensate for compacted subsoil beneath it.

Placement specifications would set maximum trafficking conditions, target horizon depth and a method for loosening the receiving subsoil without indiscriminately blending horizons. Final acceptance would inspect rooting continuity, ponding, visible smearing and bulk density at representative points rather than checking only surface appearance.

### 5. Protect ecological and plant health

Living soil can transport unwanted organisms as well as beneficial ones. Every Soil Reuse Plan would include a proportionate pathway screen. High-risk material would be isolated until identified risks were resolved. Contractors would clean soil-contact equipment before moving from a high-risk source to an uninfected receptor.

The directorate should not promote commercial microbial inoculants as a standard substitute for conserved soil. Such products may have uses in controlled circumstances, but their organisms and carrier materials may not match the receptor, and evidence of field persistence is variable. If an inoculant is proposed, the supplier must state its composition, intended function and comparison with a no-inoculant treatment on projects receiving public funds.

## Implementation

### Phased introduction

The first two years should cover all new directorate-led projects while applying enhanced monitoring to six demonstration sites:

- two road or active-travel schemes;
- one school or civic construction site;
- one flood-storage or drainage project;
- one species-rich grassland receptor; and
- one urban tree-planting scheme.

At least one demonstration site should have heavy clay soil and one should have a freely draining sandy soil. This will not represent every North Vale soil, but it will test the standard across materially different handling conditions.

Projects already in construction would not be required to redesign their earthworks. They should adopt labeling, no-trafficking and horizon-separation measures where these can be introduced without contractual dispute.

### Contracting and accountability

The Capital Commercial Team would insert a Living Topsoil schedule into design-and-build contracts. The client’s project manager would remain accountable for approving exceptions; ecological responsibility should not be transferred entirely to the contractor.

Each qualifying project would maintain a digital soil register recording:

- source area and approximate horizon depth;
- estimated volume;
- date and weather condition at stripping;
- stockpile location and dimensions;
- treatments or incidents;
- receptor location and placement date; and
- reason for any disposal or off-site export.

Payment for soil handling would be linked to completed records and verified receptor preparation. This is preferable to relying solely on penalties after damage has occurred. Persistent noncompliance could still trigger corrective work or withholding under existing contract mechanisms.

Training would consist of a one-day course for project managers and clerks of works, a shorter site briefing for machine operators, and field guidance using photographs and simple decision trees. The directorate would establish a small panel of soil practitioners rather than expecting each project to commission a separate specialist procurement.

### Exceptions

The standard must not create unsafe work or force contaminated material into reuse. Exceptions would be permitted for:

- confirmed or suspected contamination requiring controlled handling;
- emergency works;
- severe space or slope constraints;
- plant-health restrictions;
- saturated conditions where movement itself would cause greater damage; and
- projects where the soil volume is less than 100 cubic metres and the receptor is low sensitivity.

An exception record must state the reason, alternative controls and approving officer. The annual report should publish aggregate exception rates so that repeated use does not become an unexamined norm.

## Monitoring and evaluation

### What success should mean

The policy should be judged on function, not simply whether stockpiles comply with dimensions. Proposed outcomes are:

1. less topsoil sent off site or downgraded to fill;
2. faster and more even vegetation establishment;
3. improved infiltration and aggregate stability;
4. recovery of representative decomposer activity; and
5. lower expenditure on remedial cultivation and replanting.

At the six demonstration sites, sampling would occur before disturbance where access permits, immediately after placement, and at 12, 24 and 36 months. Each site would include an undisturbed local reference and, where design allows, a conventionally handled comparison plot. Sampling would be done in the same seasonal window to reduce, though not eliminate, weather-driven variation.

The proposed field set is deliberately modest: bulk density, infiltration, water-stable aggregates, soil organic matter, surface vegetation cover, litter-bait decomposition and springtail abundance from standardized cores. Earthworm counts would be added where soil pH and moisture make them meaningful. Plant community composition would be assessed for habitat receptors.

Results would feed into a **Soil Function Index** with four equally weighted components: physical structure, water behavior, decomposer activity and vegetation establishment. The index would make project reporting accessible, but raw results must also be retained because equal weighting may conceal trade-offs. For example, vigorous vegetation on nutrient-rich soil could raise the score while undermining a low-fertility habitat objective.

Routine projects outside the six sites would receive visual and physical checks but not full biological sampling. Biological testing is still relatively expensive and sensitive to season, recent weather and laboratory method. The implementation phase should determine whether litter decomposition and springtail counts provide enough repeatability to justify wider use.

The 18-month review should answer four questions:

- Are the 1.2-metre and eight-week defaults achievable without disproportionate land or programme costs?
- Do they preserve function better than current practice across the tested soils?
- Which monitoring measures are sufficiently repeatable for contract assurance?
- Does direct transfer reduce aftercare costs enough to offset additional planning and sequencing?

A three-year ecological report will be needed even though the main policy review occurs earlier. Biological recovery cannot be assessed credibly within one construction season.

## Financial implications

The requested NV£1.15 million comprises NV£310,000 for staff training and guidance, NV£420,000 for surveys and monitoring at demonstration sites, NV£180,000 for the digital register and contract integration, and a NV£240,000 contingency for specialist advice and biosecurity testing.

Project-level costs are expected to arise from earlier surveys, additional stockpile footprints, machinery sequencing and occasional pauses during unsuitable weather. Based on three recent pilot projects, the Land Systems Policy Unit estimates an added construction cost of **NV£0.35 to NV£0.90 per cubic metre of handled topsoil**. This range is provisional. The pilots were medium-sized sites with available land and did not include dense town-center construction.

Potential savings include reduced soil import, disposal, compost, fertilizer, erosion repair and replacement planting. If the standard prevented only 15% of the average annual remedial landscape expenditure observed in the nine-project review, the directorate would avoid roughly NV£300,000 per year. That comparison is indicative rather than a full business case: remedial records do not isolate soil-handling failures, while some benefits, such as improved infiltration or habitat continuity, are not readily monetized.

The policy is therefore justified primarily as better stewardship and risk management, with cost savings to be tested during implementation rather than assumed in advance.

## Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Shallow piles require more land | Pressure on compounds and temporary land | Plan footprints earlier; allow justified exceptions; prioritize direct transfer |
| Weather delays stripping | Construction programme conflict | Include hold points and realistic seasonal allowances in tenders |
| Living soil moves pests or invasive plants | Damage to recipient sites | Source screening, equipment hygiene and restrictions on high-risk transfer |
| Thresholds are treated as guarantees | Poor outcomes despite formal compliance | Retain receptor matching, functional checks and professional judgment |
| Monitoring becomes too complex | Low compliance and unusable data | Limit full sampling to demonstration sites and maintain a simple field protocol |
| Contractor prices rise defensively | Higher capital costs | Standardize clauses, publish handling expectations before tender and review actual cost data |
| Specialist capacity is insufficient | Approval delays | Create a shared practitioner panel and train existing environmental staff |

A particular concern is laboratory capacity for plant-health and biological testing. The proposed budget assumes that most projects will be cleared through desk study and field inspection. A year with several suspected disease or contamination cases could exceed the contingency and delay transfers. Procurement should establish call-off arrangements before the standard takes effect, but the number and turnaround time of suitable providers have not yet been verified.

## Decision requested

The Executive Board is asked to:

1. approve the Living Topsoil Standard for a two-year implementation phase;
2. allocate NV£1.15 million from the Capital Resilience Fund;
3. require qualifying projects entering construction after 1 April 2027 to prepare Soil Reuse Plans and digital soil registers;
4. authorize six enhanced monitoring sites and annual public reporting of compliance, exceptions and soil destinations; and
5. require an 18-month implementation review followed by a three-year ecological outcomes report.

Adoption would not eliminate the ecological damage caused by excavation. It would, however, replace the current assumption that salvaged topsoil remains equivalent regardless of handling. By preserving appropriate living communities where practical, matching soil to its receptor and measuring recovery after placement, North Vale can obtain more value from material it already owns while reducing avoidable restoration failure.
