## Artefact

# The Archive Is Faster Than the River: Measuring Telemetry Delay in Flood Forecasts

## Question

Flood models are often evaluated with a cleaned archive in which every gauge value appears at its nominal observation time. Operationally, however, a 10:00 reading may not reach the forecasting system until 10:20—or at all. This analysis asks how much that distinction matters for short-range forecasts on the 2,140 km² Larkspur catchment, where the target gauge at Bracken Quay sits below two fast-responding tributaries.

We compared forecasts made from a conventional cleaned dataset with forecasts reconstructed from the measurements that would actually have been available at each issue time. The principal result is that archive-based testing understated six-hour stage error by about 18%. Most of the gap occurred during a small number of high-water periods when upstream telemetry was delayed by more than 45 minutes.

## Data

The dataset covers October 2019 through September 2025 at 15-minute resolution. It contains stage from Bracken Quay and three upstream gauges, rainfall from nine tipping-bucket stations, catchment-average radar rainfall, and an indicator for scheduled releases from the small Alder Mere reservoir. Gauge messages retain both an observation timestamp and a receipt timestamp, allowing the information set at each forecast issue to be reconstructed.

Quality control removed physically impossible stage jumps, duplicate messages, and rainfall accumulations produced when blocked gauges emptied. Gaps shorter than one hour were forward-filled, with gap length supplied to the model as a separate feature; longer gaps remained missing. Radar rainfall was retained when ground gauges failed.

Evaluation focused on 58 high-flow episodes with peaks above 2.70 m at Bracken Quay. Thirty-four crossed the 3.20 m local flood-advisory threshold. Each episode ran from 24 hours before the initial rise until 18 hours after the peak. Entire episodes, rather than individual rows, were assigned to folds so that observations from the same flood could not appear in both training and testing data.

## Forecast experiment

A gradient-boosted tree model predicted Bracken Quay stage at three-, six-, and nine-hour horizons. Inputs included current stage, changes over the preceding one and three hours, lagged upstream stages, recent rainfall totals, reservoir-release status, month, and time of day. Missingness indicators allowed the model to distinguish a genuinely steady river from a stale, forward-filled value.

Four versions were evaluated:

1. **Persistence:** future stage equals the latest Bracken Quay observation.
2. **Local-only:** uses Bracken Quay history and catchment rainfall but no upstream stages.
3. **Perfect-archive:** uses all measurements at their observation timestamps, regardless of when they arrived.
4. **As-received:** exposes each measurement only after its recorded receipt time.

Performance was estimated with rolling-origin validation: models trained on earlier water years and were tested on the following year. Tree depth, learning rate, and regularization were selected within each training period. Metrics were pooled from the held-out predictions, while 95% intervals were generated by resampling whole flood episodes 2,000 times.

Stage accuracy was measured by mean absolute error (MAE). Threshold forecasts were assessed using critical success index (CSI), recall, and false-alarm ratio. An advisory was counted when the model first predicted a stage above 3.20 m within the next six hours; lead time was measured against the first observed crossing.

## Results

| Model | 3 h MAE | 6 h MAE | 9 h MAE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Persistence | 0.18 m | 0.32 m | 0.47 m |
| Local-only | 0.15 m | 0.26 m | 0.39 m |
| Perfect-archive | 0.11 m | 0.18 m | 0.29 m |
| As-received | 0.13 m | 0.22 m | 0.34 m |

The as-received model remained substantially better than persistence, reducing six-hour MAE by 0.10 m. Nevertheless, the perfect archive reported an additional 0.04 m improvement that would not have been available operationally. The event-bootstrap interval for this difference was 0.02–0.07 m.

At the six-hour advisory horizon, the perfect-archive model achieved a CSI of 0.74, recall of 0.86, and false-alarm ratio of 0.17. Corresponding as-received values were 0.66, 0.77, and 0.19. Median lead time among correctly forecast crossings fell from 5.7 hours in the perfect archive to 4.4 hours as received. Five of the 34 crossings were missed by the as-received model; three occurred while at least two upstream gauges were more than 45 minutes late.

Latency had a nonlinear effect. When every upstream reading arrived within 15 minutes, six-hour MAE was 0.19 m, close to the perfect-archive result. It rose to 0.24 m with maximum latency of 15–45 minutes and to 0.31 m when latency exceeded 45 minutes. The last category represented only 9% of evaluated timestamps but included 27% of threshold-crossing periods.

An ablation clarified which delayed inputs mattered. Replacing only the upstream stage feeds with instantaneous values reduced as-received six-hour MAE from 0.22 m to 0.19 m. Making rainfall instantaneous reduced it only to 0.21 m. Thus, within this dataset, delayed tributary-stage messages explain more of the archive-to-operation gap than delayed rain reports.

Missingness indicators also helped. An otherwise identical model that forward-filled inputs without reporting their age had a six-hour MAE of 0.24 m and occasionally treated a flat, stale tributary trace as evidence that a flood wave had stabilized. The benefit was concentrated in episodes with communication outages; it was negligible during normal transmission.

Residuals revealed a remaining weakness shared by both full models. For the fastest-rising quarter of episodes, defined by maximum three-hour stage increase, the as-received model underpredicted peak stage by a median 0.16 m. Error was not systematically positive or negative in slower events. This suggests that adding transmission realism does not resolve the scarcity of abrupt floods in the training record.

## Interpretation and limitations

The comparison indicates that cleaned archives can give an optimistic account of operational flood forecasting, particularly for metrics such as warning lead time that depend on exactly when information becomes available. It also shows that telemetry delay is episodic: average latency alone would obscure the coincidence between communication problems and some high-water periods. A practical evaluation should therefore replay message receipt times, retain input-age features, and report performance separately for degraded communications.

The findings come from one catchment with a particular gauge network and should not be transferred directly to basins dominated by snowmelt, tidal backwater, or large reservoir operations. Receipt timestamps show when messages entered the archive, not whether duty forecasters obtained observations by telephone or other informal channels; the reconstruction may therefore be slightly more restrictive than actual practice. Finally, the bootstrap treats flood episodes as independent even though events within the same wet season may share soil and communications conditions, so its intervals may be too narrow.

Even with these qualifications, the operational conclusion is clear: the as-received model is the appropriate headline benchmark. The perfect-archive result remains useful, but mainly as an estimate of the performance recoverable through more reliable upstream telemetry rather than as a description of the forecasting system currently in service.
