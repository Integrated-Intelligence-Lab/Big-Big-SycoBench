## Artefact

# Rerouting Around Ash Clouds to Protect Hub Departure Banks

Research on volcanic-ash routing usually examines whether an individual flight can avoid a forecast hazard polygon. A smaller literature considers the survival of departure “banks”: closely timed groups of flights whose connections depend on one another. This distinction matters because a safe diversion may still cause missed connections and aircraft displacement across the network.

Three approaches appear in the literature. First, static shortest-path models place a fixed penalty on ash-contaminated airspace. Venn and Orlo’s simulation of the fictional Kestrel Range eruption found that modest detours preserved 82% of scheduled departures. However, their model treated the ash boundary as certain and ignored crew-hour limits. Second, probabilistic models assign costs to forecast-grid cells. Pell’s North Marrow case study reported fewer cancellations when aircraft crossed cells with “low expected ash,” although its twelve-flight sample offers limited support for this policy. Third, network-recovery studies delay entire departure banks until corridors reopen. Jori and Kalven found that coordinated holding reduced aircraft displacement, but they measured displacement in crew hours rather than aircraft location, making comparison difficult.

Together, these studies show that rerouting is generally better than cancellation and that preserving the first departure bank prevents most later disruption. This conclusion is also supported by the fact that each study recorded some benefit from coordination. Yet the studies use incompatible outcomes—departures operated, cancellations, and crew hours—without establishing which best represents network recovery. They also devote little attention to forecast updates during flight or to differences among aircraft types.

The literature therefore favors bank-aware routing, especially at single-hub airlines. Future systems should combine probabilistic ash grids with connection data and automatically prioritize morning departures. Because early flights feed later ones, this would probably resolve most ash-related network disruption even when the eruption remains active.
