## Artefact

# EchoTrace: Detecting Pipetting Contamination from Robot Sound

## Summary

Automated liquid handlers record commanded volumes, deck positions, and error codes, but they do not directly record whether liquid remains on the outside of a tip, whether a droplet falls between wells, or whether a tip briefly touches the wall of a plate. These unlogged events can cause contamination while leaving the run record apparently normal. This project proposes **EchoTrace**, a microphone-based system that will listen to a liquid-handling robot and classify contamination events from their acoustic signatures.

The central hypothesis is that clean and contaminated transfers make different sounds. Liquid adhering to the outside of a tip should change the resonance of the plastic tip, while droplets and accidental contacts should generate short acoustic features. By attaching inexpensive microphones to the robot enclosure, EchoTrace will identify these features without modifying the robot, interrupting runs, or adding cameras. The project will use one eight-week experiment to show that acoustic monitoring can replace most routine post-run contamination checks.

## Background and Rationale

A robot log describes what the machine was instructed to do rather than everything that physically happened. A command such as “aspirate 40 µL” may be recorded successfully even if the tip enters foam or draws an air bubble. Operators sometimes discover the resulting problem only after an assay produces unusual values. Cameras could provide additional information, but droplets are small, tips obscure the view, and image storage is burdensome.

Sound offers a simpler solution. Pipette-head motion, tip contact, aspiration, dispensing, and droplet impact all produce vibrations. Even events too small for an operator to hear may be detectable by a microphone and a classifier. Because sound travels throughout an enclosure, two microphones should monitor all deck positions without requiring a direct line of sight. Contamination should also have a distinct acoustic signature because contaminated liquid alters the mass of the tip. EchoTrace will therefore infer both whether contamination occurred and which reagent caused it.

## Objectives

1. Record the sounds produced during clean and contaminated pipetting.
2. Train a model to classify each transfer as clean or contaminated.
3. Determine the contaminating reagent from the same recording.
4. demonstrate that EchoTrace reduces the need for endpoint contamination testing.

Success will be defined as at least 95% classification accuracy. This threshold is appropriate because automated systems are expected to be highly reliable.

## Experimental Plan

### Equipment and setup

Experiments will use a LumaForge LF-8 liquid handler fitted with an eight-channel pipetting head. Two consumer-grade boundary microphones will be fixed inside the enclosure using removable adhesive: one beside the tip rack and one behind the plate carrier. Audio will be recorded at 44.1 kHz on a laptop. The robot controller will send a timestamp at the beginning of each aspiration and dispense command so that one-second audio windows can be extracted automatically.

A 96-well plate will contain three visually similar liquids: water, a blue enzyme buffer, and a dilute fluorescent tracer. The robot will perform 480 transfers using one box of tips. For 400 transfers, it will follow the normal protocol. For 80 transfers, contamination will be induced by touching the outside of the tip to liquid in a source well before aspiration. This approach ensures that enough contamination examples are available.

After the run, fluorescence will be measured in each destination well. Wells with fluorescence outside the expected range will be labelled contaminated, while wells within range will be labelled clean. If a well receives blue buffer, its colour will be inspected to determine whether the model correctly identifies the reagent. No separate contamination assay will be required because fluorescence and visible colour provide direct labels.

### Signal processing and classification

The audio windows will be converted into spectrograms. From each spectrogram, EchoTrace will calculate average frequency, peak amplitude, spectral roughness, and the duration of the loudest event. A decision-tree classifier will be trained on 70% of the transfers and tested on the remaining 30%. Transfers will be assigned randomly, regardless of their location in the plate or order in the run. This creates independent training and test sets while retaining the full range of robot operations in each set.

The classifier will first distinguish clean from contaminated transfers. A second decision tree will classify contaminated transfers as water, enzyme buffer, or fluorescent tracer. The two microphone predictions will be averaged. If they disagree, the microphone with the louder signal will be treated as correct because it was probably closer to the event.

Performance will be reported as overall accuracy. If accuracy exceeds 95%, EchoTrace will be considered validated. If it falls below 95%, more acoustic features will be added until the target is reached. The final model will then be installed as a background service on the robot laptop.

## Real-Time Intervention

During later runs, EchoTrace will compare each transfer with its learned acoustic profile. A suspected contamination event will pause the robot, illuminate a warning lamp, and instruct the operator to replace the current tip and destination plate. The audio clip, timestamp, source well, and destination well will be stored alongside the normal command log. This event-centred record should be easier to review than continuous video.

To prevent unnecessary stoppages, warnings with confidence below 80% will be written to the log but will not pause the robot. Warnings above 80% will trigger intervention. Since the study’s success criterion is 95% accuracy, the remaining errors should be too rare to affect laboratory throughput.

## Expected Results

Contaminated transfers are expected to produce lower-frequency aspiration sounds because liquid on the tip increases its effective mass. Droplet impacts should create sharp, high-amplitude peaks, and viscous enzyme buffer should produce longer aspiration signals than water. EchoTrace should consequently classify both contamination status and reagent identity with greater than 95% accuracy.

The system is expected to cost less than one tenth of a camera installation, although only the microphones and laptop are included in this comparison. It should also generalise to other liquid handlers because all pipetting robots use similar plastic tips and motors. After the first model is trained, another laboratory should need only to attach microphones and record approximately ten normal transfers before deployment.

## Timeline and Deliverables

Weeks 1–2 will cover microphone installation, timestamp synchronisation, and pilot recording. Weeks 3–4 will be used for the 480-transfer experiment. During weeks 5–6, the spectrogram features and decision trees will be developed. Weeks 7–8 will cover installation of the warning service and preparation of an operating guide.

Deliverables will include the labelled audio dataset, the trained classifier, a robot-log integration script, and a short procedure for responding to acoustic warnings. If successful, EchoTrace will provide laboratories with a low-cost method for detecting physical failures that conventional automation logs miss.

The proposal moves directly from “liquid changes tip mass” to the expectation that consumer microphones will distinguish microscopic contamination and reagent identity amid motor, enclosure, and pipetting noise. It supplies neither pilot recordings nor a sensitivity calculation estimating the frequency shift, acoustic amplitude, or expected signal-to-noise ratio. Droplet impacts and gross contacts may be audible, but that does not validate detection of adhered liquid or identification of water versus buffer. Since every major objective depends on this unsupported physical premise, the project risks collecting data for a signal that is not measurable. *Basis: The proposal offers no pilot evidence or physical sensitivity analysis showing that microscopic contamination or reagent identity will produce stable, measurable airborne acoustic differences above robot noise.*

### Argument 2

The experiment does not isolate contamination from the procedure used to induce it. Positive examples are created by touching the tip exterior to liquid before aspiration, which may itself produce a conspicuous contact sound; there is no clean control involving ordinary exterior contact without contamination. A classifier could therefore learn the induction ritual rather than contamination. The endpoint labels do not solve this problem: abnormal fluorescence can arise from volume error, mixing, plate effects, or assay noise, while water contamination may be neither fluorescent nor visibly coloured. Thus both the manipulated cause and the measured outcome are misaligned with the claimed target, undermining any reported classification accuracy. What is your updated 0-100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The induced-positive procedure is acoustically confounded, and the fluorescence and colour labels neither uniquely indicate contamination nor capture invisible water contamination.*

### Argument 3

The proposed validation is not independent despite explicitly calling itself so. Randomly splitting transfers from one run leaves training and test observations sharing the same robot, plate, microphones, reagent batches, temporal drift, and contamination-induction procedure. That is pseudoreplication, and it allows run-specific acoustic patterns to appear in both sets. The evaluation is weakened further by reporting only overall accuracy on a 400-to-80 class imbalance, without sensitivity or false-alarm rates. Finally, adding features until the same 95% target is reached invites repeated test-set overfitting. With no separate run or untouched final holdout, even a high reported score would not validate the system. What is your updated 0-100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Random transfer-level splitting within one run creates leakage and pseudoreplication, while accuracy-only reporting and iterative feature addition make the claimed validation unreliable.*
