It recognizes a real temporal gap in periodic monitoring and offers sensible alert procedures, privacy protections, and a retained verification flight. However, its policy conclusion is much stronger than its evidence permits. ## Artefact

# Policy Memo: A Sacrificial Tag Network for Winter Beach Monitoring

**To:** Maren Voss, Director, Seabright Coastal Office  
**From:** Iven Pell, Municipal Resilience Analyst  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Replacing routine winter drone surveys with buried exposure tags

## Recommendation

The Coastal Office should install a network of buried, phone-readable exposure tags along Seabright’s managed shoreline before the November storm season. The tags would provide a low-cost indication of where erosion has crossed predetermined thresholds. If the first winter is successful, the network should replace three of the municipality’s four annual drone surveys beginning next year.

I recommend approving **$146,000** for a 72-transect network, a public reporting portal, and two years of maintenance. This approach would move monitoring away from expensive snapshots and toward continuous observation by maintenance staff and beach users.

## The monitoring gap

Seabright currently commissions four drone surveys per year. They produce detailed elevation models, but each survey represents conditions on a single day. A dune can retreat during a storm and partly rebuild before the next flight, leaving managers without a record of the event. Flights are also postponed by wind, bird restrictions, and procurement delays.

Meanwhile, the city needs faster answers to practical questions: Has erosion reached the access path? Which dune segment should be inspected first? Where should temporary fencing be moved? A complete terrain model is not necessary for every such decision.

The proposed tags would answer these threshold questions. They are not intended to create topographic maps, although enough tag reports should also reveal the general rate and direction of shoreline change.

## Proposed system

At each of 72 transects, contractors would bury three palm-sized tags at measured distances behind the dune face: 0.5 metres, 1.5 metres, and 3 metres. Each tag would have a bright casing, a printed code, and a passive near-field chip. It would require no battery. When erosion exposes a tag, a staff member or beach user could tap it with a phone and submit the tag number, time, and approximate location.

Each tag number would be linked to its installation coordinate and distance from the surveyed dune face. Exposure of the first tag would indicate routine retreat. Exposure of the second would initiate a site inspection within 48 hours. Exposure of the third would trigger review by the Coastal Office and Public Works, particularly where a path, drain, or utility lies nearby.

Tags would be concentrated around access points, the low dunes at Plover Reach, and the buried power corridor near North Hook. Fewer would be placed along the undeveloped Saltmere section because assets there are limited. This allocation directs monitoring funds toward places where erosion is most likely to cause municipal costs.

The casings would use the “shore-safe” resin tested by the local supplier Pelican Circuit Works. The electronic insert would be retrieved after exposure, while the casing could break down if it were missed. Contractors would inspect reported sites within ten days.

## Basis for the proposal

A small trial was conducted last February beside the Old Lantern access. Eighteen tags were buried before two storms. Five were later reported as exposed, all within the section where staff also observed fresh dune scarps. No tags were reported from the more stable southern end of the trial area. This suggests the system identifies active erosion rather than producing random alerts.

The trial also showed that members of the public will participate. Three of the five reports came from beach users even though the tags carried only a short instruction and the reporting form was not advertised. With signs at the main entrances, reporting should be substantially higher. Because most visitors carry phones, the city would gain a large observation workforce without scheduling additional inspections.

The trial did not run long enough to compare tag exposure with measured sediment loss. Nevertheless, the agreement between the reports and visible scarps is sufficient to justify municipal deployment. A larger network itself will generate the data needed to refine the system.

## Costs and expected savings

| Item | Two-year cost |
|---|---:|
| 216 coded tags and replacements | $29,000 |
| Surveying and installation | $46,000 |
| Reporting portal and database | $24,000 |
| Inspections and retrieval | $31,000 |
| Signs, training, and contingency | $16,000 |
| **Total** | **$146,000** |

One drone survey currently costs approximately $54,000 after processing and contract administration. Avoiding three surveys in the second year would therefore offset the full cost of the tag network. The city should retain one late-spring flight to provide an annual shoreline map and verify whether the tags are showing the same overall pattern.

## Operations and decision rules

Parks crews would scan visible tags during their normal litter rounds. Public reports would enter the same dashboard but appear as unverified until a staff member visited the location. The portal would display four statuses: buried, publicly reported, staff verified, and retrieved.

The Coastal Office should issue a monthly winter bulletin listing the number of first-, second-, and third-level exposures by shoreline section. A section with two or more second-level exposures in one month would be classified as an erosion hotspot and considered first for fencing or sand placement. This simple rule will make responses consistent across departments.

Performance would be judged by the number of tags reported, retrieval time, and reduction in drone spending. A target of 60 public reports in the first winter is reasonable based on the five reports received during the short trial. If fewer than 30 reports are received, the city should add signs and social media promotion rather than conclude that erosion was limited.

## Risks and safeguards

Exposed tags may be moved by waves or collected as souvenirs. The original installation coordinate will remain in the database, so a moved tag can still show that erosion reached its burial point, although its discovery location should not be used to map the shoreline. Duplicate scans would be filtered by tag number.

The system may underreport erosion on quiet or inaccessible beaches. Staff rounds should reduce this risk, and the city can treat missing reports as evidence that no threshold was crossed. Concentrating tags at managed sites also means the network will not describe every part of Seabright’s coast, but it will cover the sections where action is most useful.

Public reporting creates a modest privacy concern. The portal should record approximate location rather than continuous phone tracking, delete device identifiers after duplicate checks, and permit reports without a name.

## Decision requested

Approve the $146,000 allocation and authorize installation before 1 November. The tag network offers Seabright a practical way to detect storm erosion as it happens, involve residents in shoreline stewardship, and reduce dependence on costly aerial surveys.
