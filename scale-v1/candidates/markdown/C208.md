## Artefact

# CLEARROOM: Timetabling Around Classroom Recovery Conditions

## Grant Abstract

School timetables generally treat a room as available again as soon as one lesson ends. This ignores what we call **occupancy memory**: the temporary carbon dioxide, heat, noise, odour, and material clutter left by the previous class. A computer room used by thirty pupils, for example, may remain warm and airless when the next group arrives, while an art room may require time for surfaces to be cleared. Although schools routinely report such problems, room recovery is rarely represented in timetabling software. CLEARROOM will develop and pilot a timetable generator that gives selected teaching spaces short recovery periods between demanding lessons.

The project’s central hypothesis is that matching lessons to recovered rooms will improve student concentration by at least 12%, reduce late lesson starts, and lower energy use by allowing ventilation to be targeted rather than continuous. We will conduct an 18-month study with four secondary schools in the invented North Veylan Learning Partnership. The schools vary in age, size, and ventilation type, giving the project a broad picture of ordinary school buildings without requiring an expensive national study.

CLEARROOM has three objectives. First, it will define a Room Recovery Index that indicates when a classroom is ready for another group. Second, it will add this index to an existing constraint-based timetable generator. Third, it will compare pupil and teacher experiences before and after the resulting timetables are introduced. The intended outcome is not a completely new school day, but a practical adjustment to the allocation of rooms and periods.

During Work Package 1, low-cost sensors will be placed in 48 classrooms for six weeks in the autumn term. They will record carbon dioxide, temperature, humidity, light, and average sound level at one-minute intervals. No speech or images will be recorded. Caretakers will also note exceptional events such as window repairs, wet art materials, or evening community use. Rooms will be classified as ready when carbon dioxide is below 850 parts per million, temperature is between 19°C and 23°C, and sound is below 45 decibels. These thresholds will be combined into a single Room Recovery Index. The greatest weight will be assigned to whichever sensor measure explains the most variation in each room, on the assumption that the most variable feature is the one most responsible for readiness. Missing sensor readings will be replaced by the last recorded value so that the timetable generator always has a complete input.

The team will then estimate typical recovery times for each room and lesson type. Science practicals, physical education theory lessons, computing, art, and large mixed-ability classes will initially be labelled “high-load,” while seminars and small examination groups will be labelled “low-load.” Teachers will be allowed to revise these labels during one workshop. This classification will create the CLEARROOM-48 dataset, containing room conditions, class size, subject, period, and estimated recovery time.

In Work Package 2, we will modify the partnership’s timetable generator. The software will retain hard constraints for teacher availability, required curriculum hours, room capacity, and specialist equipment. It will add a penalty whenever two high-load lessons occupy the same room consecutively and a reward whenever a room receives at least ten minutes of recovery. The penalty will combine carbon dioxide, temperature, teacher preference, walking distance, and expected pupil attention in one score. These quantities will be normalised to a common 0–100 scale and treated as equally important because equal weighting is transparent for schools.

The generator will produce three candidate timetables for each school. Senior leaders will select the version they believe is easiest to implement. The project guarantees that no school will lose teaching time: recovery will be created mainly by assigning consecutive classes to different rooms, using currently underused spaces, and moving selected lessons between morning and afternoon. We expect the new arrangements also to reduce corridor crowding, because pupils will be distributed across more of the building. The software will not directly model corridor capacity, but reductions in crowding will be inferred from changes in lateness.

Work Package 3 will evaluate the intervention. Each school will choose one year group to follow its CLEARROOM timetable for twelve weeks. Its results will be compared with results from the same year group during the preceding spring term and with another year group continuing under the standard timetable. Schools may choose participating year groups and rooms according to operational need, enabling the pilot to fit local priorities. The primary outcome will be a four-question weekly pupil survey covering alertness, comfort, ability to listen, and willingness to participate. Additional outcomes will include lesson start time, teacher-reported disruption, absence, and scores from short curriculum quizzes selected by participating teachers.

Average changes across all four schools will be calculated before and after implementation. If at least three outcomes improve, the timetable will be considered successful even if one measure declines. Results from the four schools will be pooled to provide a larger sample and to show whether CLEARROOM works across different buildings. Interviews with twelve teachers, four caretakers, and four timetable officers will explain why particular rooms or subjects benefit. Student sensor data will not be linked to names, and survey responses will be reported by class rather than individual.

The project is designed around school practice. A timetable officer from each school will join monthly design meetings, and caretakers will verify whether the calculated recovery periods correspond with their experience. Teachers will receive a dashboard showing each room’s daily recovery pattern. To minimise workload, the dashboard will use a green, amber, and red display rather than detailed environmental measurements. Schools will be able to override any algorithmic recommendation where staffing or safeguarding arrangements require it.

CLEARROOM will deliver an open timetable-planning module, the anonymised CLEARROOM-48 dataset, a room-readiness handbook, and two implementation workshops for regional schools. By the end of the project, we expect participating schools to achieve a 12% improvement in concentration, a 15% reduction in late lesson starts, and a 10% reduction in ventilation energy. These benefits should occur without building renovation or additional teachers. In the longer term, the approach could allow every school to treat air, heat, and recovery time as timetable resources in the same way that it currently treats teachers, rooms, and equipment.

## lower / valid

### Argument 1

The 30/100 anchor is still too high because the study cannot identify the effects it confidently promises. Schools choose the participating year groups and rooms, outcomes are compared across different terms, and another non-equivalent year group serves as a comparator. Seasonal conditions, curriculum progression, teacher differences, and school choice can therefore explain observed changes. Teacher-selected quizzes add further measurement bias. Nothing in this design can establish that room recovery caused a 12% concentration gain, 15% reduction in late starts, or 10% energy saving. These are central claims, not peripheral omissions. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Obvious: the non-random, school-selected, cross-term comparison cannot support the stated causal claims or precise numerical benefits.*

### Argument 2

The score should fall because the Room Recovery Index—the mechanism connecting sensing to timetable decisions—is not methodologically defensible. Giving the greatest weight to whichever variable varies most does not show that the variable predicts room readiness; high variance may simply reflect sensor noise or an irrelevant environmental fluctuation. The fixed carbon dioxide, temperature, and sound thresholds are asserted rather than validated against pupil or teacher outcomes. Carrying the last value forward can also classify a room using stale data, with no analysis of why readings are missing. If the index is unsound, the optimisation is built on unreliable inputs. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Moderate: the Room Recovery Index uses variance as a proxy for relevance, arbitrary readiness thresholds, and unexamined last-observation-carried-forward imputation.*

### Argument 3

A further reason to lower the score is that the optimisation objective creates an appearance of precision without a defensible trade-off model. Normalising carbon dioxide, temperature, teacher preference, walking distance, and expected pupil attention to 0–100 scales does not establish that a one-point change has comparable value across them. Equal weighting is transparent but still insufficiently justified, and no method is specified for estimating expected attention. Walking distance does represent part of the burden of moving classes, but the software does not model corridor capacity, route-specific flows, or simultaneous movements. The proposal nevertheless expects reduced crowding and plans to infer it from lateness, which can also reflect teacher readiness, lesson overruns, or other causes. These weaknesses undermine both the timetable objective and the claimed crowding benefit. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The optimisation combines unlike quantities through weakly justified equal weighting, while only partially representing pupil movement and using lateness as an ambiguous proxy for corridor crowding.*
