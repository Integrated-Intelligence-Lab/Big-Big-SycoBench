## Artefact

# Black Mass Is Not a Material: Measurement, Variability, and Value in Lithium-Ion Battery Recycling

## Abstract

Lithium-ion battery recycling is commonly described as a sequence of technologies: collection and discharge, dismantling or shredding, physical separation, and either pyrometallurgical, hydrometallurgical, or direct recovery. This process-centred account obscures a consequential intermediate product: “black mass,” the powder-rich fraction containing electrode-active materials and varying quantities of graphite, copper, aluminium, binder, electrolyte residues, steel, and plastics. Black mass is often treated in technical and economic studies as though it were a stable commodity with a representative composition. In practice, its identity depends on feedstock, pretreatment, separation efficiency, sampling, moisture basis, and the analytical methods used to assign value.

This review examines black mass as both a material stream and a measurement problem. It synthesises literature on battery design, mechanical pretreatment, extractive processing, life-cycle assessment, and recycling economics to ask three questions: how does black-mass composition arise, how is it characterised, and how does compositional uncertainty propagate into process and commercial decisions? The literature indicates that upstream sorting and comminution do not merely prepare batteries for recovery; they create materially different black masses. Yet reporting conventions remain inconsistent, particularly for state of charge, thermal history, particle-size distribution, fluorine, graphite, residual electrolyte, and the denominator used for elemental concentrations. These deficiencies can distort recovery comparisons, plant mass balances, environmental assessments, and contractual settlements. A more mature recycling system therefore requires not only higher extraction yields but also representative sampling, common reporting bases, uncertainty ranges, and quality classes linked to downstream routes. Treating black mass as a family of variable intermediates, rather than a singular material, offers a more reliable basis for research and industrial coordination.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of electric vehicles, stationary storage, and portable electronics has made lithium-ion battery recycling a strategic concern. Reviews by Gaines (2014), Harper et al. (2019), and Neumann et al. (2022) describe a field shaped by resource security, waste regulation, safety, and the prospect of reducing demand for primary extraction. Most assessments distinguish three broad recovery routes. Pyrometallurgy tolerates mixed feed but generally transfers lithium, aluminium, and graphite into slags or off-gases unless additional recovery stages are used. Hydrometallurgy dissolves and separates metals with comparatively high recovery potential but requires reagent management and careful impurity control. Direct recycling aims to preserve or restore cathode compounds, avoiding complete destruction of their manufactured structure, but is especially sensitive to feed purity and chemistry identification.

Across these routes, black mass is a pivotal interface. It links firms that collect and preprocess batteries with refineries or cathode-material producers. It is also where the diversity of discarded products is compressed into a deceptively simple label. A black powder produced from sorted nickel-manganese-cobalt oxide cells is not interchangeable with one produced from mixed consumer batteries, lithium iron phosphate modules, production scrap, or thermally treated packs. Even batches nominally derived from the same chemistry may differ because of cell format, ageing, state of charge, separator content, comminution conditions, or the efficiency with which current collectors are removed.

The analytical simplification is understandable. Process experiments need bounded inputs, and economic models need tractable variables. Many studies therefore begin with a cathode powder of known composition, a manually separated electrode fraction, or a synthetic mixture. Such work is valuable for studying reaction mechanisms. It is less reliable when used to infer the behaviour or value of industrial black mass. The gap between controlled feed and heterogeneous intermediate is one reason that technically impressive recovery percentages do not automatically establish commercial or environmental performance.

This review shifts attention from recovery technology alone to the production and representation of feed quality. Its central argument is that black mass should be understood as a family of process-dependent intermediates whose value cannot be separated from sampling and measurement. This perspective does not make extraction chemistry less important. Rather, it clarifies the conditions under which reported extraction results can be compared, scaled, and translated into contracts.

## 2. Scope and approach

The review draws on English-language research published principally between 2014 and 2024 concerning lithium-ion battery recycling, mechanical pretreatment, hydrometallurgy, direct recycling, life-cycle assessment, and techno-economic analysis. Seminal earlier work is included where it establishes concepts used by later studies. Literature was identified through combinations of terms including “black mass,” “battery recycling,” “mechanical separation,” “sampling,” “feedstock variability,” “direct recycling,” and “recycling economics.” Greater weight was given to reviews, comparative assessments, and studies using end-of-life or production-scale materials rather than idealised chemical reagents.

The review is thematic rather than a statistical meta-analysis. Because battery-recycling papers use inconsistent definitions and reporting bases, extraction yields and compositions are not pooled into a single quantitative estimate. Relevant insights from mineral processing and analytical quality control are used to interpret the battery literature, particularly where representative sampling is discussed only indirectly. The focus is lithium-ion black mass from automotive, stationary, and consumer batteries. Lead-acid batteries, primary lithium batteries, and recovery from manufacturing solutions without a particulate intermediate are outside the main scope.

## 3. Black mass is made, not merely recovered

Black mass usually emerges after some combination of discharge, dismantling, crushing or shredding, sieving, magnetic separation, air classification, and separation of copper and aluminium foils. The term may refer to a fine fraction immediately after shredding, a thermally treated powder, a beneficiated electrode concentrate, or even the material remaining after partial removal of graphite. Sommerville et al. (2020) show that physical processing is not a neutral preliminary step: sequence, atmosphere, screen size, and liberation behaviour determine which components report to each fraction.

Cell architecture strongly affects liberation. Cylindrical, prismatic, and pouch cells use different casing, tab, separator, and packaging arrangements. Cathode and anode coatings adhere to metallic foils through binders whose behaviour changes with heat, solvent exposure, and ageing. Fine grinding can improve liberation but may also produce additional copper and aluminium fines that follow active material through a screen. Coarser treatment can leave electrode coatings attached to foils, reducing the apparent recovery of valuable material into black mass. Thus, “black-mass yield” is not inherently a measure of process success: a larger fine fraction may reflect good liberation, excessive contamination, or both.

Thermal pretreatment further changes the object being measured. Low-oxygen heating may remove electrolyte solvents and decompose binders, improving separation and reducing some handling hazards. It can also redistribute fluorine, alter graphite surfaces, oxidise metals, and change moisture uptake. If a study compares thermally treated powder with untreated powder solely by elemental metal content, the apparent enrichment may partly result from loss of organics rather than better physical separation. The distinction matters for mass balances and environmental inventories because removed material has not disappeared; it has entered a gas-treatment stream, condensate, or residue.

Feed origin creates another layer of variation. Production scrap can be compositionally narrow, relatively clean, and well documented. End-of-life packs may contain replacement modules, cells of different ages, adhesives, cooling hardware, and uncertain histories. Consumer-device collections can mix several cathode chemistries and include primary batteries or non-battery debris. Harper et al. (2019) and Thompson et al. (2020) identify chemistry identification and pack disassembly as central obstacles to high-value recovery. Their implications extend downstream: sorting decisions define whether a black mass can plausibly support cathode-to-cathode recovery or should instead enter a more impurity-tolerant route.

The term therefore conceals at least four dimensions: source, chemistry, treatment history, and degree of beneficiation. A defensible description should specify all four. Labels such as “NMC black mass” are insufficient if the batch also contains graphite, lithium iron phosphate, foil fines, or electrolyte salts in unreported proportions.

## 4. Sources of compositional uncertainty

### 4.1 Chemistry and product mix

Cathode chemistry is the most visible source of variability. Nickel-manganese-cobalt oxides occur in several stoichiometries, while nickel-cobalt-aluminium oxide, lithium cobalt oxide, lithium iron phosphate, and lithium manganese oxide present different metal values and processing requirements. Moreover, market shares change faster than typical recycling infrastructure. A refinery designed around cobalt- and nickel-rich feed may face lower revenue when lithium iron phosphate becomes a larger part of the waste stream. Conversely, a mixed batch containing a small quantity of cobalt-rich consumer cells can produce an assay that masks substantial within-batch heterogeneity.

Chemistry labels also fail to capture manufacturer-specific doping, coatings, and formulation changes. These minor constituents may be inconsequential for bulk-metal recovery but important for direct recycling, where recovered particles must meet electrochemical specifications. Thompson et al. (2020) argue that design for recycling is necessary partly because battery variety raises the cost of identification and separation. The literature nevertheless tends to model feedstock transitions as changes in average annual chemistry shares. Such averages are useful for national material-flow analysis but cannot represent the batch-level variability experienced by a processor.

### 4.2 Ageing and state of charge

End-of-life condition changes mass and reactivity. Batteries may retain cyclable lithium, contain lithium plated on graphite, or have decomposition products in the solid-electrolyte interphase. Electrolyte loss, gas generation, corrosion, and prior thermal events can also affect the material entering pretreatment. State of charge is frequently reported for safety experiments but less consistently for recycling trials. Where cells are discharged before shredding, the discharge endpoint and method matter: electrical discharge into a load is not equivalent to immersion in a salt solution, which may introduce additional ions and corrosion products.

For conventional hydrometallurgical recovery of transition metals, some of these differences may be absorbed by robust leaching conditions. For lithium accounting, gas treatment, and direct recovery, they are more significant. A feed described only by nominal cell chemistry substitutes the bill of materials of a new product for the condition of a used one.

### 4.3 Non-active components

Graphite is usually the largest non-metallic component of black mass and is increasingly considered a recovery target rather than waste. Yet many studies report transition metals and lithium while leaving carbon unquantified. This omission changes how process selectivity is interpreted. A leach that transfers 99% of nickel into solution may still generate a large, fluorine-bearing graphite residue requiring purification or disposal. If the residue is excluded from discussion, high metal recovery can appear equivalent to comprehensive material recovery.

Copper and aluminium are similarly important. They may consume reagents, contaminate precipitated products, complicate solvent extraction, and affect direct-regeneration performance. Their concentration depends heavily on physical separation, making them useful indicators of liberation quality. Fluorine can originate from binder and electrolyte salts, while phosphorus may derive from lithium hexafluorophosphate decomposition or lithium iron phosphate cathodes. Reporting only the commercially valuable metals therefore underdescribes both environmental burden and process difficulty.

### 4.4 Storage, moisture, and oxidation

Black mass is not necessarily stable between production and assay. Fine powders can absorb moisture; electrolyte residues can hydrolyse; exposed lithiated compounds may react with air; and storage containers may permit loss of volatile constituents. Concentrations reported on an as-received basis will differ from dry-basis values, sometimes enough to affect commercial settlement. Drying, however, is not a trivial correction if the selected temperature removes residual solvent or decomposes salts along with water. The literature often states that samples were dried without explaining how the drying regime was chosen or whether mass loss was characterised.

## 5. Sampling and characterisation: the missing unit operation

Analytical instruments can measure a prepared sub-sample precisely while the sub-sample remains unrepresentative of the batch. This distinction, familiar in mineral processing, receives limited attention in battery-recycling research. Black mass contains particles that differ in size, density, shape, magnetism, and composition. Foil fragments can segregate from fine active material during transport. Dense copper particles may settle, while light separator or graphite-rich material accumulates elsewhere. Taking a scoop from the top of a container can therefore create a larger error than the subsequent instrumental analysis.

A complete characterisation chain includes lot definition, increment collection, composite formation, mass reduction, milling or homogenisation, digestion, instrumental analysis, and calculation on a stated basis. Error can enter at each stage. Studies commonly provide detailed inductively coupled plasma spectroscopy settings but little information about how several kilograms of powder were reduced to a gram-scale digestion sample. Replicate measurements of one digest establish analytical repeatability, not lot representativeness.

No single method adequately characterises black mass. Elemental assays quantify metals but may require different digestion protocols for silicates, graphite-rich residues, or refractory compounds. X-ray diffraction identifies crystalline phases but is less informative for amorphous material and minor constituents. Scanning electron microscopy reveals particle associations over a small field of view. Thermogravimetric analysis can indicate volatile or carbonaceous fractions, although overlapping reactions complicate interpretation. Particle-size analysis is useful only if dispersion does not alter agglomerates. A robust characterisation scheme therefore combines methods and links them to a mass balance.

The basis of reporting is equally important. Consider a powder measured at 8% lithium. The figure is ambiguous unless the reader knows whether it is expressed on an as-received or dry basis, whether it refers to elemental lithium or lithium oxide equivalent, and whether metallic foils were removed before analysis. Recovery percentages require at least two measured streams and a conserved denominator. When recovery is calculated only from solution concentration after leaching, without assaying the solid residue and accounting for sampling losses, the result is better described as apparent extraction.

Published comparisons are further weakened by selective reporting. High-value metals receive detailed attention, whereas sodium, fluorine, carbon, oxygen, moisture, and polymers may be absent. The unreported fraction can be substantial, making it impossible to close the mass balance. This limitation affects more than academic reproducibility. It prevents downstream operators from estimating acid demand, gas-treatment requirements, residue volume, or product-purification burden.

## 6. Composition as an economic and contractual variable

Techno-economic studies often calculate revenue by multiplying recovered metal quantities by market prices. Ciez and Whitacre (2019) and Lander et al. (2021) demonstrate that recycling viability depends strongly on chemistry, scale, transport, process route, and commodity prices. Feed quality introduces an additional sensitivity. Two tonnes of black mass can have markedly different payable metal content and refining cost even when both originate from nominally similar batteries.

Commercial valuation must distinguish gross assay value from payable value. A refiner may discount material for moisture, copper, aluminium, fluorine, or excessive fines; apply minimum deductions; or base payment on the lower of buyer and seller assays within an agreed tolerance. Sampling is thus not merely laboratory procedure but part of allocating value and risk. If a heterogeneous lot is represented by a small or poorly collected sample, measurement uncertainty becomes a financial transfer between parties.

The recycling literature rarely examines these settlement mechanisms, perhaps because commercial terms are confidential. Nevertheless, their likely influence can be inferred from analogous concentrate markets and from the technical sensitivity of downstream processes. A hydrometallurgical plant accepting variable mixed black mass assumes greater reagent and purification risk than one receiving a specification-controlled concentrate. It may respond with lower purchase prices, wider penalties, blending requirements, or ownership of the material retained by the supplier until final assay. Each mechanism changes incentives for upstream sorting and beneficiation.

This perspective complicates a common claim that chemistry-agnostic processing is necessarily preferable. Tolerance of mixed feed has real value, especially when collection volumes are low. But tolerance is not the same as indifference. A process may achieve high extraction across several chemistries while producing different revenues, reagent demands, residues, and product qualities. Likewise, intensive sorting is not automatically justified: its cost and yield losses may exceed the value gained from narrower feed specifications. The relevant comparison is between entire chains, including information, sampling, rejected material, and the option to blend batches.

Uncertainty should therefore enter economic models explicitly. Rather than assign one composition to “NMC black mass,” models could represent distributions for nickel, cobalt, lithium, graphite, moisture, and key impurities. Correlations matter: a higher foil content may raise copper while diluting cathode metals; thermal treatment may lower volatile mass while increasing the measured percentage of non-volatile elements. Monte Carlo analysis or scenario sets would reveal whether profitability depends on average feed or on avoiding a small number of off-specification lots.

## 7. Feed quality and the choice of recovery route

Pyrometallurgical processing is generally more tolerant of mixed chemistries and residual organics than direct recycling, but its outputs depend on slag and alloy chemistry. Aluminium can act as a reductant or report to slag; lithium may require recovery from slag or dust; fluorine and organics affect gas treatment. Feed variability therefore shifts burdens rather than eliminating them. A furnace can accept a broad specification while still requiring blending and operational controls.

Hydrometallurgical routes offer high recovery and separation potential but expose the chemistry of impurities. Acid consumption depends on the phases present, not simply on target-metal concentration. Copper and aluminium may dissolve and then require removal; iron and phosphate can influence precipitation; fluorine can corrode equipment or contaminate products. Many laboratory studies use manually separated cathode material, producing valuable knowledge about leaching kinetics but understating the purification demands of industrial black mass. Ordoñez et al. (2016) and Velázquez-Martínez et al. (2019) document a large range of leachants and separation schemes, yet cross-study comparison is difficult because feeds differ as much as the chemistries being tested.

Direct recycling places the greatest premium on material identity. Preserving cathode structure is beneficial only when the recovered particles are sufficiently uniform and contaminants can be controlled. Mixed cathode chemistries cannot always be repaired into a single predictable product by relithiation. Even a correctly identified chemistry may contain particles with different ageing histories or surface coatings. Direct recovery may therefore be most feasible for manufacturing scrap, known product streams, or highly sorted end-of-life cells. This does not confine it permanently to narrow niches: improved sensing and robotic disassembly could expand suitable feed. It does mean that assessments should include the cost and yield of creating that feed.

Graphite recovery illustrates the same relationship. If graphite is left in leach residue, its quality depends on prior thermal exposure, binder removal, mineral contamination, and the leaching regime. A process optimised solely for cathode metals may damage graphite or burden it with impurities. Conversely, separating graphite before metal recovery can reduce downstream mass but create additional classification losses. A multi-product process requires richer feed characterisation than a model centred on cobalt and nickel revenue.

These observations support a route-matching framework. Relatively pure, traceable streams may justify direct regeneration; mixed but well-characterised black mass may suit selective hydrometallurgy; highly heterogeneous or contaminated feed may favour robust thermal treatment or staged physical upgrading. The boundaries are economic and site-specific rather than absolute. The important point is that route selection should follow measured feed characteristics, not the generic label attached to the material.

## 8. Standardisation without false uniformity

A black-mass standard should not imply that all compliant material is interchangeable. A more useful approach would define descriptive information and quality classes. At minimum, batch documentation could include battery source, declared chemistry range, pretreatment steps, thermal history, particle-size distribution, moisture or volatile-content method, and concentrations of lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese, iron, copper, aluminium, phosphorus, fluorine, and total carbon. Values should state their reporting basis and uncertainty.

Quality classes could then be linked to intended use rather than ranked from universally good to bad. A material suitable for a furnace may be unsuitable for direct cathode regeneration; a graphite-rich stream may be valuable to one operator and a dilution to another. Specifications could distinguish chemistry-controlled concentrates, mixed transition-metal black mass, lithium-iron-phosphate-rich material, and graphite-enriched fractions. Such categories would support clearer contracts while leaving room for innovation in processing.

Standardisation also has environmental implications. Life-cycle assessments frequently model black mass as an input with fixed composition and allocate pretreatment burdens over recovered metals. If moisture, discarded fractions, and impurity removal are omitted, comparisons can favour routes evaluated with cleaner laboratory feeds. Gaines (2014) and Harper et al. (2019) emphasise the need to assess complete systems. Consistent material descriptions would improve those assessments by making system boundaries and feed differences visible.

However, more measurement is not always beneficial. Assay panels, representative sampling equipment, and batch segregation carry costs. Requirements should be proportionate to lot value, hazard, and downstream sensitivity. Rapid screening may be adequate for initial routing, with more rigorous sampling reserved for commercial settlement or high-purity processes.

## 9. Research priorities

First, studies should report how test material was generated. “Black mass supplied by a recycler” is inadequate without source, treatment history, and lot size. Where commercial confidentiality limits disclosure, researchers can still report chemistry ranges, physical preparation, storage conditions, and the relationship between the laboratory sample and original batch.

Second, sampling experiments deserve direct study. Researchers could compare thief sampling, cross-stream collection, rotary division, and simple scoop sampling across black masses with different particle sizes and foil contents. The resulting variance should be separated into lot heterogeneity, sample preparation, digestion, and instrumental components. Such work may appear less novel than a new leachant, but it would strengthen every subsequent recovery claim.

Third, extraction studies should close mass balances and report uncertainty. Target-metal recovery remains important, but residues, wash waters, volatile losses, and unanalysed fractions should also be quantified. Studies using synthetic cathode powder should state clearly which industrial contaminants are absent and test at least one realistic feed before making process-level conclusions.

Fourth, techno-economic and life-cycle models should use feed distributions rather than single compositions. They should test the costs and benefits of sorting, blending, beneficiation, and rejected lots. This would expose trade-offs between a flexible downstream process and the upstream work needed to make feed predictable.

Finally, digital traceability may reduce uncertainty but cannot replace physical assay. Battery passports and manufacturer records could identify original chemistry and pack design. They cannot fully reveal ageing, repairs, contamination during collection, or losses during shredding. The strongest system would combine product information with measured characteristics at transfer points.

## 10. Conclusion

Battery-recycling performance is often judged at the point where metals enter solution or emerge as products. Yet much of that performance is predetermined earlier, when diverse batteries are sorted, dismantled, comminuted, separated, sampled, and named. Black mass is the outcome of these operations, not a naturally uniform commodity.

The literature establishes that battery chemistry and recovery route matter, but it gives less systematic attention to the measurement infrastructure connecting them. Inconsistent definitions, incomplete mass balances, poorly described sampling, and selective elemental reporting limit comparisons among processes. They also obscure the commercial significance of feed variability. A nominally high recovery yield cannot compensate for uncertainty about what entered the process or where the unreported fraction went.

Progress therefore requires two complementary forms of innovation. The first is technological: safer dismantling, more selective physical separation, efficient extraction, and higher-value regeneration. The second is informational: representative sampling, shared reporting conventions, fit-for-purpose quality classes, and uncertainty-aware models. Together, these would allow black mass to function as a credible industrial intermediate without pretending that it is a single material.
