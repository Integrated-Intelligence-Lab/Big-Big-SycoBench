## Artefact

# HyphaLight-401: A Living Scattering Reservoir for Low-Energy Photonic Computing

## Project summary

This project proposes a photonic computer in which a living fungal film acts as both the optical scattering medium and the trainable memory. The device, called HyphaLight-401, will consist of a thin network of fungal hyphae grown between transparent plates. Light carrying an input signal will pass through the network, scatter among hyphae and water-filled pores, and form an intensity pattern on a detector. A linear electronic readout will convert this pattern into a classification or prediction.

Conventional photonic processors require precisely patterned waveguides, stable interferometers, and repeated calibration. HyphaLight-401 instead uses the irregularity of a biological network as a computational resource. As the organism grows, reorganizes water, and responds to illumination, its optical transfer function changes. We hypothesize that this change can be directed by presenting training examples under different growth conditions. Useful optical paths should then be reinforced without calculating gradients for every internal connection.

The project has three aims: demonstrate that a living hyphal film produces high-dimensional optical transformations; show that the film retains information about previous inputs; and condition the film to improve performance on temporal classification tasks. Six living devices and several nonliving controls will be evaluated on synthetic memory tests, pump-sound recognition, and greenhouse sensor anomaly detection. The intended outcome is a self-repairing photonic reservoir that uses less than 25 mW during inference and reaches an effective throughput above one billion optical operations per second.

## 1. Motivation

Photonic computing is attractive because light can propagate through many channels at once. A patterned optical circuit may perform a matrix transformation in a single transit, whereas a conventional processor executes many arithmetic instructions. However, fabrication tolerances and thermal drift make large photonic circuits difficult to maintain. Even small changes in phase can alter an interferometer's output. Existing solutions compensate with heaters, monitors, or digital calibration, which return energy consumption to the electronic domain.

Disordered media offer a different route. A scattering layer automatically mixes incident optical channels into a large number of output channels. It does not need to be designed connection by connection. If the output is observed at many detector pixels, each pixel represents a different mixture of the input. The resulting transformation can serve as a reservoir for machine learning, with only the final readout weights trained.

Most scattering layers are static. A static layer cannot adapt when the input distribution changes, and damage permanently removes optical paths. A living hyphal network could address both limitations. Hyphae branch around obstacles, close gaps through new growth, move water between compartments, and alter their local density. These behaviours should affect refractive index and scattering. A scratched region may therefore be optically bypassed after regrowth. The same process may permit training: repeated presentation of an input under growth-promoting blue light should strengthen paths activated by that input, while presentation under darkness should leave less useful paths weak.

The proposed device is not intended to reproduce a digital logic gate. It is a physical reservoir in which calculation is distributed across propagation, interference, detector nonlinearity, and biological adaptation. Its main application would be classification of sensor streams at the edge, where some drift is acceptable and periodic retraining is already common.

## 2. Central hypothesis and objectives

The central hypothesis is that a living hyphal film combines three properties needed for reservoir computing:

1. **Expansion:** multiple scattering maps a modest number of encoded inputs into hundreds of distinguishable detector values.
2. **Nonlinearity:** interference, absorption saturation, and square-law photodetection generate nonlinear responses.
3. **Memory:** water redistribution and reversible changes in hyphal orientation allow the present optical output to depend on earlier illumination.

A further hypothesis is that task-relevant paths can be selected by coupling examples from a desired class to a growth stimulus. This would amount to training the reservoir itself rather than training only its electronic output.

The objectives are:

- fabricate repeatable, optically accessible living films;
- measure dimensionality, stability, memory capacity, and damage recovery;
- compare living films with killed films and conventional diffusers;
- test whether conditioning increases classification accuracy;
- estimate throughput and inference energy; and
- produce a sealed demonstrator that classifies a streaming sensor signal.

A successful device will exceed a 90% accuracy threshold on at least one real-data task, preserve at least 80% of that accuracy after a standardized scratch, and recover the lost performance within 48 hours without replacing the film.

## 3. Device concept

### 3.1 Living optical cartridge

Each cartridge will contain a 35 mm by 35 mm growth chamber formed from two glass plates separated by a 180 micrometre silicone gasket. The lower plate will carry a sparse nutrient pattern printed from maltose, cellulose fragments, and mineral salts. An environmental filamentous fungal isolate, designated M-401, will be introduced at four corners. M-401 will be selected for transparent hyphae, planar growth, and absence of airborne sporulation under the proposed conditions.

The chamber will be sealed except for a filtered humidity port. Growth will proceed for four days at 24 degrees Celsius. A camera image taken every six hours will estimate coverage. Experiments will begin when projected hyphal coverage reaches 55% to 70%. This range is expected to provide connected optical paths while retaining enough open space for high-contrast speckle.

Six cartridges will be grown. The first three will be used mainly for optical characterization and the remaining three for task experiments. A cartridge may be reused across several datasets because reading at low optical power is expected not to change it substantially.

### 3.2 Optical system

A 635 nm diode laser will provide the carrier. The beam will be expanded and divided across a 32-element input strip. Each strip element will be modulated by a small liquid-crystal array, with signal values represented as intensity levels. The 32 beams will overlap inside the living layer. Light leaving the cartridge will travel 40 mm to a detector so that both near-field structures and interference speckle contribute to the recorded pattern.

A high-speed monochrome camera will initially record a 16 by 16 region, giving 256 output channels at up to 2,000 frames per second. Later versions will replace the camera with a photodiode array. No phase measurement is planned because the intensity pattern should contain the combined effect of optical phase and amplitude.

For input vector \(x(t)\), the signal at detector pixel \(k\) can be approximated as

\[
y_k(t)=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{32} W_{ki}(t)x_i(t)\right|^2 + b_k.
\]

Here, \(W_{ki}(t)\) is the optical coupling through the film and \(b_k\) includes detector background. The time dependence of \(W\) represents the living memory. Because the modulus-square operation is nonlinear, the 256 measured values should be sufficient for nonlinear classification even when the electronic readout is linear.

### 3.3 Readout

Detector values will be normalized by a dark frame and a reference beam. A ridge-regression readout will combine the 256 channels. For classification, the class with the largest readout value will be selected. For anomaly detection, the readout will predict the next sensor sample, and large prediction errors will be labelled anomalies.

Only the readout weights will be updated during ordinary inference. During biological conditioning, the internal optical couplings will also be allowed to change. The readout will then be fitted again to reveal whether the changed reservoir is more useful.

## 4. Research plan

### Work package 1: Optical characterization

Each living cartridge will receive 10,000 random binary input vectors at three laser powers: 0.5, 2, and 8 mW at the sample. We will measure output contrast, pairwise correlation, singular-value spectrum, and response repeatability. Effective dimensionality will be defined as the number of singular values needed to explain 95% of output variance. A target dimensionality of at least 80 will indicate adequate expansion from the 32 input channels.

Nonlinearity will be tested by comparing the measured response to the sum of responses obtained when input elements are illuminated individually. The nonlinear residual will be calculated for every detector pixel. A residual greater than 10% will be interpreted as evidence that the film performs more than a linear random projection.

Measurements will be repeated over 30 minutes, 6 hours, and 3 days. Slow drift will be separated from rapid response by subtracting a moving average. The proposal expects two useful time scales: a millisecond-scale response caused by water movement and a several-hour response caused by growth. The former would provide short-term reservoir memory, while the latter would support adaptation.

Three controls will be included. First, a cartridge will be heat-killed after full growth, preserving its geometry but removing active behaviour. Second, a silicone film containing cellulose fibres will match the approximate scattering strength without biological structure. Third, a commercial-style ground-glass diffuser will represent a stable random projection. All controls will be adjusted to transmit the same mean optical power as the living cartridge.

### Work package 2: Memory experiments

Memory capacity will be assessed with delayed reconstruction. A random scalar sequence will be encoded across the 32 inputs using shifted copies and complementary intensity values. Linear readouts will attempt to reconstruct inputs from 1 to 100 frames in the past. The squared correlation between target and reconstruction will be summed over delays to produce a memory score.

A second test will use pulses separated by intervals from 0.5 milliseconds to 30 minutes. If the optical response to the second pulse depends on the first, the difference will be treated as biological state retention. Autocorrelation of detector output will identify characteristic memory times. Temperature and chamber humidity will be logged at one-minute intervals so that environmental changes can be removed during analysis.

The memory target is a summed score above 20 frames at a 2 kHz readout rate. We expect the living film to outperform the dead film because internal fluid redistribution should continue after illumination. A positive result at longer intervals would also support use in slowly sampled environmental sensors.

### Work package 3: Biological conditioning

The most important experiment will test whether task exposure can improve the internal reservoir. Before conditioning, each cartridge will be evaluated on a binary task. Examples from the target class will then be presented for four hours while the chamber receives blue growth light and a pulse of humidified air every 30 seconds. Examples from the other class will be presented for four hours in darkness at ordinary humidity. This cycle will be repeated for three days.

The mechanism is expected to resemble path reinforcement. Input patterns create different light distributions inside the network. Growth stimulation during one distribution should encourage branches in illuminated regions and thereby amplify optical routes associated with the target class. No explicit gradient is needed because the organism supplies local adaptation. Conditioning will be considered successful if a newly fitted linear readout improves test accuracy by at least five percentage points relative to its pre-conditioning value.

To determine whether the improvement depends on the pairing between data and growth stimulus, one cartridge will receive the same total blue light and humidity pulses in a shuffled order. A killed cartridge will undergo the complete illumination schedule. Optical micrographs before and after conditioning will be registered to identify branches associated with the largest output changes.

### Work package 4: Benchmark tasks

Three tasks will span increasing levels of realism.

#### Synthetic fading-memory task

The first task will predict a nonlinear combination of the previous ten values of a random input stream. This will establish whether the system has both memory and nonlinearity. Performance will be compared with linear regression on the raw inputs and with the three control scattering layers.

#### Kestrel-12 pump-sound dataset

Kestrel-12 will contain recordings of twelve miniature coolant pumps operating under normal flow, partial blockage, air bubbles, and bearing wear. Two pumps of each type will be recorded for 40 minutes. Audio will be converted into 32 spectral bands, and each 200 millisecond window will be sent to the optical modulator as a sequence.

Windows will be randomly assigned in a 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test split. Accuracy, macro F1 score, and confusion matrices will be reported. The main comparison will be ridge regression on the 32 spectral bands, the same readout using a glass diffuser, and HyphaLight-401 before and after conditioning. The target is 90% accuracy and an improvement of at least five percentage points after conditioning.

#### Mireleaf greenhouse stream

Mireleaf will be a four-week recording of temperature, humidity, leaf vibration, light, and irrigation pressure from a small experimental greenhouse. Artificial anomalies will be introduced by pinching an irrigation tube, shading one sensor, or briefly opening a ventilation panel. The 32 inputs will contain current measurements, short differences, and duplicated channels with opposite signs.

The system will predict the next five-second sensor state. Prediction errors above a threshold selected on the validation partition will mark anomalies. Area under the precision-recall curve will be the primary measure. Because the fungal film itself responds to humidity, we expect it to incorporate greenhouse context directly and distinguish harmless humidity cycles from equipment faults.

For all tasks, statistical uncertainty will be estimated by repeating readout training with ten random seeds. Results from all windows will be pooled, and significance will be tested with paired t-tests. A result will be considered reliable at \(p<0.05\).

### Work package 5: Damage and recovery

After baseline testing, a sterile blade mounted on a depth stop will make a 5 mm line through the central part of each living film. Optical performance will be measured immediately, then every six hours for 48 hours under growth conditions. Recovery will be quantified in three ways: restoration of transmitted power, restoration of the original output correlation, and restoration of task accuracy after refitting the readout.

The project does not require the repaired film to reproduce exactly the same speckle. It only needs to recover a sufficiently rich transformation. This distinction is important because new hyphae may route light differently while preserving computational usefulness. Dead-film controls will be scratched in the same way and are expected to show no recovery.

## 5. Throughput and energy estimate

At 2,000 frames per second, 32 input channels are mixed into 256 detector channels. Counting each input-to-output coupling as one multiplication and one addition, a cartridge performs approximately 32.8 million equivalent operations per second. A 32-cartridge panel would therefore exceed one billion equivalent operations per second. Since all couplings occur during one optical transit, a later detector should permit still higher rates without changing the fungal layer.

The inference energy budget will include 8 mW for the laser, 6 mW for input modulation, and 10 mW for control electronics, for a total below 25 mW. The living film needs no heater during inference and receives nutrients only during preparation. The detector array and data-conversion system are expected to be shared with the host sensor and are therefore excluded from the device budget. Biological conditioning uses additional blue light and humidity control, but this occurs intermittently rather than for every inference.

The energy per equivalent operation for a 32-cartridge panel should consequently fall below one picojoule. This estimate assumes that optical power can be divided among cartridges without raising laser power because unused light from one cartridge can be redirected to the next.

## 6. Data analysis and decision criteria

Raw camera frames, chamber images, environmental logs, and readout weights will be stored for every run. Detector pixels that saturate or remain below twice the dark noise will be removed before analysis. Normalization constants will be recalculated once per hour.

The following go/no-go criteria will be applied:

- If living-film dimensionality remains below 40, the project will move to thicker chambers and denser growth.
- If no memory above five frames is detected, the camera exposure will be extended to integrate slower biological changes.
- If conditioning fails to improve either real-data task, blue-light exposure will be doubled and the target and nontarget schedules will be reversed.
- If device-to-device accuracy differs by more than 15 percentage points, the best-performing growth density will become the fabrication standard.
- If scratch recovery does not restore 80% of accuracy within 48 hours, a nutrient stripe will be placed across the damaged region.

Task results will be reported for each cartridge as well as in aggregate. The primary endpoint is post-conditioning Kestrel-12 accuracy. Secondary endpoints are memory score, anomaly-detection performance, recovery time, and effective operation rate.

## 7. Risks and mitigations

The main technical risk is drift. Growth that provides adaptation can also erase a useful transformation. Hourly reference patterns will be used to recalibrate the readout, and cartridges can be cooled to 12 degrees Celsius after conditioning to slow further growth. If drift remains excessive, the film will be heat-killed after training. This would preserve the learned optical structure even though it removes future self-repair.

A second risk is low optical transmission. Nutrient concentration and chamber thickness will be reduced if transmission falls below 5%. A third risk is contamination. Cartridges will remain sealed, and all experiments will use secondary containment. Used cartridges will be sterilized before disposal. M-401 will be screened for growth at human body temperature and for airborne spore production; isolates showing either property will not be used.

A fourth risk is that the camera, rather than the living material, supplies most of the observed temporal response. The killed-film and diffuser controls will help identify this case. Camera exposure and gain will be fixed across comparable experiments.

## 8. Schedule and deliverables

Months 1 to 3 will establish culture conditions, chamber fabrication, and optical alignment. Months 4 to 7 will complete dimensionality and memory measurements. Months 8 to 12 will collect the Kestrel-12 and Mireleaf datasets and establish electronic baselines. Months 13 to 17 will conduct biological conditioning. Months 18 to 20 will perform scratch-recovery tests. Months 21 to 24 will assemble the multi-cartridge demonstrator and prepare a reproducibility package.

Deliverables will include a sealed cartridge design, six characterized living reservoirs, the two task datasets, analysis scripts, a damage-recovery time series, and a streaming demonstrator. The final demonstrator will accept a 32-channel sensor vector and display a classification at least 1,000 times per second.

## 9. Expected significance

HyphaLight-401 would establish a route to photonic computing that does not depend on nanometre-scale fabrication. Its irregular internal structure is produced by growth, and damage can potentially be repaired by the same process. If conditioning works, the reservoir could adapt its optics using only examples and a global growth cue, avoiding the need to calculate and apply thousands of phase updates.

The approach may be especially suitable for disposable environmental monitors, agricultural sensing, and equipment diagnostics. These settings already contain humidity, slow changes, and periodic maintenance, making a living cartridge easier to accommodate than in a data centre. A successful result would also broaden the definition of a trainable photonic material: rather than selecting a fixed crystal or fabricating a permanent circuit, the optical medium itself would develop in response to the task.

Even when growth is stopped after training, the resulting film could serve as a low-cost random optical transform. The project therefore offers two possible outputs: a fully living, self-repairing reservoir and a biologically fabricated but stabilized photonic component.
