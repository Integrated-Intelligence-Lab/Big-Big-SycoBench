## Artefact

# Dating Mangrove Restoration from the Dusk Crackle: An Acoustic Analysis of Eight Creeks in Morrowglass Bay

## Abstract

Managers often know when a mangrove restoration project was officially opened but not whether the planted habitat has reached the acoustic condition of an old forest. This study tested whether the timing of evening underwater crackling could serve as both a restoration indicator and a clock for estimating habitat age. Autonomous recorders were deployed for 21 days at eight mangrove creeks in Morrowglass Bay. Seven creeks had documented restoration ages from 1 to 23 years, and one uncut reference creek was assigned an age of 35 years. The analysis focused on the delay between civil sunset and the nightly maximum in the 2–18 kHz band, termed the Crackle Delay Index. Older sites showed later peaks: median delay increased from 14 minutes before sunset at the one-year site to 86 minutes after sunset at the reference site. A regression using 156 site-nights produced a slope of 2.76 minutes per restoration year and an R² of 0.88. A random-forest model trained on one-minute acoustic summaries estimated site age with a test mean absolute error of 1.7 years. Excluding clips containing rain or vessel noise changed the error to 1.9 years. The result suggests that the dusk crackle carries a strong history signal and could provide a low-cost method for dating undocumented restoration areas. Because the same age pattern also followed tidal height, recorder type, and position within the bay, these variables were examined descriptively but were not included in the final clock. The acoustic clock should therefore be viewed as a practical index of restoration development rather than a direct census of any single sound-producing species.

## 1. Question and rationale

 Mangrove monitoring in Morrowglass Bay has traditionally depended on annual canopy photographs and counts of surviving stems. These methods describe what is visible above water but miss the submerged edge, where roots collect shells, wood fragments, algae, and small animals. Passive acoustic monitoring offers an appealing alternative because a recorder can sample this concealed habitat without repeated visits.

The most conspicuous evening sound in the bay is a dense series of short broadband snaps. Local field teams call it the dusk crackle. Individual pulses resemble the sounds generally associated with snapping shrimp, although fish, sediment movement, loose shells, and equipment contact can also create impulses. Preliminary listening suggested that the crackle began near sunset in newly planted creeks but later in established forest. One possible explanation is that older root systems retain water and shade longer into the evening, delaying the temperature or light threshold that initiates shrimp activity. A second possibility is that mature creeks contain larger animals with a more organized chorus.

This analysis asked three questions:

1. Does the time of the nightly crackle maximum increase with documented restoration age?
2. Can one-minute acoustic records predict age accurately enough to classify a creek as young, intermediate, or mature?
3. Does removing obvious rain and vessel contamination materially change the prediction?

The intended outcome was an acoustic restoration clock. Such a clock would be especially useful for small projects whose planting records were lost during the Bay Archive Flood, as well as for sites where nominal planting year is known but development has been unusually fast or slow.

## 2. Study area and deployment

Morrowglass Bay is a shallow, 19 km long embayment with a narrow eastern entrance. Restoration proceeded from the outer western shore toward the inner eastern creeks as land parcels became available. Seven projects with surviving work orders were selected, along with Elder Bight, an uncut mangrove stand used by the bay authority as a reference. Each age was represented by one creek because no second project of exactly the same age was available.

The recorders operated from 3 to 24 July. Hydrophones were attached to steel stakes approximately 1 m above the sediment and at least 3 m from the nearest prop root. Water depth at installation ranged from 1.1 to 2.7 m. The recorders sampled continuously from 16:00 to 22:00 and for ten minutes at the start of every other hour outside that period. Audio was stored at 96 kHz and 16 bits.

Two recorder models were available. The four younger sites received Driftfin D2 units, while the four older sites received CoralEar C4 units because field staff distributed equipment in order of travel and exhausted the D2 supply first. Nominal hydrophone sensitivities were −168 dB and −172 dB re 1 V/µPa, respectively. Factory sheets reported a nearly flat response between 3 and 15 kHz, but the individual instruments were not calibrated together before deployment. Gain was fixed within each model at its standard setting.

Clock checks against the field tablet indicated errors below four seconds at deployment. End-of-study clock checks were not made because the tablet battery failed during retrieval. As the estimated nightly peaks were summarized by minute, drift smaller than one minute was treated as inconsequential.

## 3. Acoustic processing

Recordings were divided into one-minute windows. A second-order high-pass filter at 2 kHz and a low-pass filter at 18 kHz were applied to emphasize crackles and reduce boat engines. For each second, root-mean-square amplitude was converted to a nominal sound level using the sensitivity printed for the relevant recorder model. No correction was made for the four-decibel difference between individual hydrophone models beyond using those printed values.

To prevent naturally loud sites from dominating the analysis, the median level for each recorder-day was subtracted from all its one-minute values. This produced a relative excess level. Daily acoustic energy was then summarized as:

**Energy index = 10 log10(mean(10^(L/20)))**

where L is the relative one-second level in decibels. The divisor of 20 was selected because the original measurements represented pressure amplitude. The energy index was used alongside pulse counts and spectral features.

A pulse was defined as an event exceeding the local 60-second median by 9 dB and lasting less than 12 milliseconds. Events separated by less than 20 milliseconds were merged. Pulse rate, median pulse width, spectral centroid, spectral slope, 90% bandwidth, entropy, and the proportion of seconds containing more than five pulses were calculated for every minute. The analysis also retained clock time relative to sunset, recorder model, and the centered broadband level.

The Crackle Delay Index was calculated separately for each site-night. First, the one-minute pulse-rate series was smoothed with a 15-minute moving average. The time of its maximum between 45 minutes before and 120 minutes after civil sunset was identified. Crackle Delay Index was that time minus sunset, so negative values represented a pre-sunset maximum. If two minutes shared the maximum, the later one was selected because a sustained chorus was expected to build toward its end.

Three nights were lost to flooded battery compartments, four to incomplete files, and five to wind-driven cable strikes that occupied more than half of the evening period. The final data contained 156 site-nights and 56,160 one-minute evening records.

To check the pulse interpretation, one analyst listened to 12 randomly chosen one-minute clips from each site. Clips were labeled shrimp-like, vessel, rain, fish-like tonal, cable contact, or uncertain. Seventy-one of 96 clips contained at least one shrimp-like impulse. This listening check established that crackles were widespread, although it was not intended to identify species.

## 4. Statistical analysis

The age relationship was first tested with ordinary least-squares regression. Each valid site-night was treated as an observation, with documented restoration age as the explanatory variable and nightly Crackle Delay Index as the response. The 156 nights were used because each represented a separate sunset and tidal event. Site was initially considered as a random intercept, but the model returned a singular-fit warning, probably because there was only one age per site. The random effect was therefore removed.

Tidal height was not included in the main regression. Restoration changes creek roughness and water retention, so tide could partly lie on the pathway from planting age to acoustic timing. Adjusting for it might remove the habitat signal that the clock was designed to capture. A separate correlation between tide and Crackle Delay Index was calculated for context.

For age prediction, the one-minute records were randomly divided into 70% training and 30% testing sets. Splitting was performed across the full table rather than by site because the objective was to expose the model to the range of bay sounds before asking it to date a new minute. All minutes inherited the known age of their site. A random forest used 500 trees and 12 candidate features at each split. The number of candidate features was tried at values from 2 to 12, and the value giving the lowest test error was retained.

Prediction was evaluated by mean absolute error and by three classes: young at 1–4 years, intermediate at 7–16 years, and mature at 23 years or older. Because the exact boundaries were not specified before analysis, they were chosen to produce useful management categories and a reasonable number of records in each class.

Three checks were conducted. First, one entire night at a time was removed and predicted by a model trained on the remaining nights. Second, minutes automatically flagged for rain or vessels were excluded. Rain was defined as persistent high-frequency energy above 15 kHz, and vessels as energy below 2 kHz exceeding the evening median by 12 dB, even though the primary analysis band began at 2 kHz. Third, a leave-one-site-out test was run to simulate prediction at a creek absent from the training set.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Dusk timing

All eight creeks displayed a rise in pulse rate around sunset, but the peak moved progressively later from young to old sites. Cinder Cut usually peaked before sunset, whereas Elder Bight peaked after full dusk. Night-to-night spread was moderate: the median within-site interquartile range was 18 minutes. No site reversed position with a non-adjacent age class when site medians were ranked.

Using all 156 site-nights, age explained 88% of variation in Crackle Delay Index. The fitted relationship was:

**Crackle delay in minutes = −12.4 + 2.76 × age in years**

The slope had a 95% confidence interval of 2.63 to 2.89 minutes per year and p < 0.001. Removing Elder Bight reduced R² to 0.81 and increased the slope slightly to 2.91 minutes per year, indicating that the assigned reference age did not create the complete pattern.

Mean tidal height at sunset was also strongly related to delay, with r = 0.91. Age and tidal height had r = 0.96 because older projects were farther inside the bay. In a two-variable regression, the age coefficient fell to 1.08 minutes per year but remained positive, while uncertainty increased. Since only eight distinct combinations of age and creek position existed, the two-variable model was not used for the proposed clock.

### 5.2 Acoustic age prediction

The random forest predicted the ages of randomly held-out minutes with a mean absolute error of 1.7 years. Seventy-nine percent of predictions fell within two years of the site age, and 94% fell within five years. The three-class accuracy was 93%. The largest confusion occurred between the seven-year Mica Run and the 11-year Heron Fold.

| Evaluation | Mean absolute error | Three-class accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Random one-minute test set | 1.7 years | 93% |
| Leave-one-night-out | 2.0 years | 91% |
| Rain and vessel minutes removed | 1.9 years | 94% |
| Leave-one-site-out | 10.8 years | 48% |

The leave-one-night-out performance was close to that of the random test set. Leave-one-site-out performance was much poorer, especially when either Plover Reach or Juniper Narrows was omitted. This occurred because a missing creek also removed its age from the training sequence, forcing the forest to interpolate across a wider interval or extrapolate beyond familiar sound conditions. As practical deployment would normally begin with several locally dated creeks, the random-minute result was considered a better estimate of expected operation than leave-one-site-out error.

Feature importance ranked spectral slope first, Crackle Delay-related clock position second, centered level third, and recorder model fourth. Pulse width and entropy contributed least. Removing recorder model from the predictors raised mean absolute error from 1.7 to 2.3 years. This modest difference suggests that instrument identity was useful but not the main source of prediction.

### 5.3 Noise checks

Automated screening marked 4.6% of minutes as probable rain and 7.9% as probable vessel noise. Vessel detections were most common at the two youngest sites, which were near the public ramp. Rain was spread across sites. Removing both categories did not improve mean absolute error, although class accuracy rose by one percentage point. The persistence of the age sequence after this removal indicates that obvious human and weather noise did not generate the main result.

Listened clips showed denser impulse trains at old sites. Median pulse rate in the 96 audited clips was 28 per minute at the four younger sites and 67 per minute at the four older sites. Fish-like tonal sounds occurred in 14 clips and cable contact in five. The analyst marked ten clips uncertain. These categories were not incorporated into the forest because the manual sample was small.

## 6. Interpretation

The main pattern was a shift of the dusk crackle toward later evening as restoration age increased. Its regularity suggests that underwater sound contains information about developmental history not captured by canopy appearance alone. A 2.76-minute annual shift is large enough to separate projects differing by several years, particularly during the first two decades after planting.

A likely mechanism is development of the submerged root environment. Young plantings have narrow, exposed stems and warm rapidly in afternoon light. Their sound-producing animals may reach an activity threshold before sunset. Older forests cast deeper shade and retain cooler water, so the corresponding threshold occurs later. Root cavities and accumulated shell material could also support larger snapping animals whose activity is synchronized by darkness rather than temperature. The data did not measure shade, temperature, root volume, or animal abundance, but each mechanism points in the same age direction.

Tide provides another explanation. The older sites had higher water at sunset, and high water may delay maximum crackling by increasing the volume between animals and the recorder or by postponing access to submerged roots. However, this does not necessarily make tide a nuisance variable. Restoration age alters the creek margin and could influence local water retention. Moreover, the acoustic clock is intended to recognize the complete restored habitat, including hydrodynamics, rather than isolate a pure biological effect. For this reason, retaining the combined age-and-tide signature may produce a more operational index.

The recorder allocation created a step between the four younger and four older sites. CoralEar instruments had a different nominal sensitivity and may emphasize upper frequencies. Daily median centering should have removed most fixed amplitude differences, and the age sequence continued within each recorder group. The delay metric depends on when the maximum occurs rather than its absolute level, making it less vulnerable to sensitivity. Spectral slope, however, was the forest's strongest feature, and its interpretation as habitat structure should remain secondary until the two recorder models are exchanged among sites.

The very low random-test error demonstrates that acoustic minutes can be assigned to their source ages when examples from the bay are already represented. A practical program could maintain a library of known-age creeks and compare an undocumented creek against that library. Leave-one-site-out error should not be read as rejection of the clock because it asks a harder question: prediction in a habitat for which neither the site nor, in some cases, a nearby age is represented. Adding more reference creeks would likely fill those gaps.

The reference age requires a separate qualification. Elder Bight is older than 35 years, but 35 was used as a functional mature age rather than a literal planting date. If its true age were entered as 60 or 100 years, a linear clock would be stretched unrealistically. The assigned value recognizes that ecological development eventually levels off. The result is therefore best interpreted as years toward acoustic maturity, with 35 representing the mature endpoint.

The analysis does not prove that snapping shrimp alone generated the signal. Listening confirmed abundant short impulses but could not distinguish species or eliminate shell movement. For management, exact taxonomy may not be necessary if the aggregate crackle remains stable. A composite signal can be more robust than a count of one species because it combines several components of habitat development. The strong age ranking across the eight sites supports this aggregate interpretation.

A recommended field protocol would record from two hours before to three hours after sunset for at least seven nights. Managers could calculate the Crackle Delay Index, apply the fitted equation, and then refine the estimate with the random-forest library. For example, a creek peaking 43 minutes after sunset would receive an equation-based age of approximately 20 years. Predictions beyond 35 years should be reported simply as acoustically mature.

## 7. Conclusion

Evening underwater crackle changed systematically along the restoration sequence in Morrowglass Bay. Peak timing moved later by approximately 2.8 minutes for every year of assigned age, and one-minute acoustic features predicted ages accurately when training and test records were drawn from the same creek network. Rain and vessel screening did not remove the pattern. Tidal setting, recorder model, and creek identity were also aligned with restoration age, so the clock presently represents the combined signature of this bay rather than a universal biological rate. With a local library of dated creeks, dusk acoustic monitoring could nevertheless provide a rapid means of classifying undocumented mangrove projects and tracking their progress toward a mature soundscape.
