## Artefact

# Computing Before Equilibrium: Cold-Start Compensation for Microring Photonic Accelerators

## Summary

Photonic matrix processors are usually evaluated after their lasers, heaters, and resonators have reached thermal equilibrium. That assumption is poorly matched to intermittently powered systems, such as battery-limited sensor nodes and accelerators that are power-gated between bursts. A microring array may perform a multiplication in nanoseconds yet spend substantially longer warming, calibrating, and locking its weights. The resulting delay and energy are absent from most throughput figures.

This project proposes **Kestrel**, a cold-start compensation method that permits useful matrix multiplication during thermal settling. Rather than forcing every resonator immediately to its equilibrium state, Kestrel will infer the array’s evolving optical operator from a small set of pilot wavelengths. It will then apply a low-rank electronic correction to each optical output. The central premise is that startup drift is high-dimensional at the device level but low-dimensional at the matrix level because many rings share heaters, substrate paths, and ambient conditions.

The project will test whether this structure can reduce time-to-first-valid-result and total energy per valid multiply without requiring continuous per-ring locking.

## Research questions and hypotheses

The primary question is: **Can a resonator-based photonic matrix engine compute accurately before thermal equilibrium, once startup drift is represented as a low-dimensional operator?**

Three hypotheses will be tested:

1. Across repeated power cycles, at least 95% of the variance in the array-level weight error can be represented by six or fewer calibrated drift modes.
2. Four pilot wavelengths, observed at eight monitor ports, can estimate the coefficients of those modes well enough to keep normalized matrix error below 2% during most of the startup interval.
3. Including pilot generation, monitor detection, conversion, and digital correction, Kestrel will reduce energy to the first 1,000 valid matrix-vector products by at least 40% relative to waiting for equilibrium.

These hypotheses separate physical structure, observability, and system benefit. A low-rank drift model alone would not establish an advantage if estimating or correcting it consumed excessive energy.

## Proposed architecture

Let the programmed equilibrium operator be $W_0$. During startup, the realized operator is modeled as

$$W(t) = W_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} z_k(t) B_k + R(t),$$

where the matrices $B_k$ are drift modes learned during calibration, $z_k(t)$ are time-varying coefficients, and $R(t)$ is unmodeled residual error. Each $B_k$ will be factorized as $P_kQ_k^T$ with rank at most two. After the photonic core produces $y_p=W(t)x$, a small electronic unit will calculate

$$y_c = y_p - \sum_k z_k(t)P_k(Q_k^Tx).$$

This correction scales with the number and rank of drift modes rather than with the full matrix size. Pilot tones placed between data channels will traverse the same distribution network as the computational wavelengths. Their monitor-port intensities will be mapped to $z_k(t)$ by a ridge-regression estimator initially, followed by a compact nonlinear estimator only if residual analysis shows a clear need.

Kestrel differs from conventional locking in its objective. It does not attempt to return every ring to its nominal resonance at every instant. It estimates the aggregate computational error and corrects only the components that materially affect the output.

## Experimental plan

### Phase 1: Drift characterization

A 64-by-64 wavelength-multiplexed microring test array will be instrumented with heater telemetry, eight optical monitor ports, and synchronized output capture. Two dies from the same fabrication run will be tested. Each die will undergo 1,000 startup cycles at ambient set points of 15, 25, and 40 degrees Celsius, with three input-power levels and randomized idle durations.

During each cycle, 256 probe vectors will be repeated from laser turn-on until equilibrium. The instantaneous matrix will be reconstructed from the outputs, and principal-component and structured low-rank analyses will identify recurring drift modes. Calibration and evaluation cycles will be separated by day to prevent the model from merely interpolating adjacent traces.

The principal endpoint is the fraction of matrix-error variance captured as a function of mode count. Secondary analyses will determine whether mode shapes transfer between temperatures, power levels, and dies.

### Phase 2: Pilot-based state estimation

Candidate pilot sets will be selected by maximizing sensitivity to the calibrated drift modes while minimizing spectral interference with data channels. Estimators will be trained on 60% of startup cycles, tuned on 20%, and evaluated on the remaining 20%, with complete cycles—not individual timestamps—assigned to each split.

Ablations will vary the number of pilots, monitor ports, modes, and temporal history samples. To distinguish genuine state observation from a learned startup clock, one control will provide only elapsed time since power-on. Further controls will perturb ambient temperature midway through startup and introduce randomized laser ramp profiles. Success requires the pilot estimator to outperform the clock-only model under both perturbations.

### Phase 3: End-to-end computing tests

Kestrel will be evaluated on three workload classes: dense random matrices spanning several condition numbers, convolutional layers extracted from a compact acoustic event classifier, and a recurrent layer used for vibration-anomaly detection. For each workload, results will be labeled valid when they meet a task-specific output-error threshold established using equilibrium operation.

Comparators will include: waiting for passive equilibrium; static one-time trimming; heater feedback using all available monitors; and a time-indexed correction without pilots. Measurements will report time-to-first-valid-result, valid results per joule over burst lengths from 10 to 100,000 operations, normalized matrix error, task accuracy, and the 99th-percentile error across startups.

Energy accounting will include lasers, thermal tuning, pilot generation, photodiodes, data converters, and the correction unit. Both measured board-level power and a component-normalized projection will be reported so that an inefficient laboratory controller is not mistaken for an architectural cost.

## Decision criteria and failure analysis

Kestrel will be considered successful if it achieves less than 2% median normalized matrix error from 50 microseconds after turn-on until equilibrium, limits 99th-percentile error to 5%, and reduces energy to the first 1,000 valid operations by at least 40% on both dies. Results that meet accuracy targets but lose the energy advantage after accounting for conversion and correction will be treated as a negative system result.

If drift is not low-rank, spatially partitioned models will be tested by assigning separate modes to quadrants of the array. If pilot observability is inadequate, pilots will be moved from shared monitor ports to sparse taps within the mesh. These alternatives increase hardware overhead and will therefore be evaluated against the same energy criteria rather than accepted solely for improving accuracy.

## Expected contribution

The project will produce a cycle-resolved dataset of photonic startup behavior, a method for determining whether thermal transients are computationally compressible, and an experimentally grounded comparison between settling, locking, and error correction. More broadly, it reframes startup as an operating regime rather than dead time. If successful, Kestrel would make photonic acceleration more credible for bursty workloads, where equilibrium throughput is a poor predictor of delivered efficiency.
