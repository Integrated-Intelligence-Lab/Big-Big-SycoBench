## Artefact

# Stop the Compliance Clock: Why Tax Agencies Should Be Accountable for Taxpayer Waiting Time

Tax compliance systems are built around clocks. A return is due on one date, a payment on another, and a response to an information request perhaps thirty days later. Miss a deadline and the system can impose interest, penalties, collection action, or the loss of an appeal right. These consequences are justified on familiar grounds: public revenue depends on timely compliance, and deadlines are easier to administer when they are firm.

Yet the same systems often become strangely indifferent to time once a file passes into government hands. A taxpayer may submit an amended return and wait nine months for it to be processed. A business may answer an auditor’s questions, only to hear nothing while interest continues to accrue on a disputed balance. An automated portal may reject a document without explaining why, beginning a cycle of calls, resubmissions, and new notices. Although these delays can be as consequential as a missed filing deadline, they are usually treated as background inconvenience rather than as part of the tax burden.

That distinction is increasingly untenable. A modern compliance system should operate a **dual clock**: one clock for periods in which the taxpayer is responsible for acting, and another for periods in which the tax administration controls the next meaningful step. When the administration’s clock is running, taxpayer deadlines connected to the unresolved matter should pause, disputed-balance interest should ordinarily stop accumulating, and prolonged delay should trigger a modest service credit. Every case should also have a visible event ledger showing which clock is active and why.

This is not merely a customer-service proposal. It is a reform to the substantive allocation of compliance costs. Tax law cannot eliminate disagreement or administrative delay, but it can stop pretending that only one side’s time has value.

## Waiting is a form of compliance cost

Conventional accounts divide taxation into the amount legally owed and the expense of calculating and paying it. The latter category includes recordkeeping, software, professional advice, and staff time. Waiting rarely receives the same attention because it does not always look like an activity. Nevertheless, uncertainty immobilizes resources.

Consider a small manufacturer, Alder Forge, that claims a refundable equipment credit of 48,000 crowns. The tax authority flags the claim for review. Alder Forge responds within ten days with invoices, bank records, and photographs of the machinery. For the next seven months, the file remains unassigned. The company cannot know whether the refund will arrive, whether another question is coming, or whether the credit will be denied. It can borrow against the expected refund, postpone hiring, or maintain a larger cash reserve. Each option has a cost produced not by the underlying tax rule but by unresolved administration.

The burden is not confined to businesses expecting refunds. Suppose an individual receives a notice asserting an underpayment, contests it promptly, and then waits while interest accumulates. If the challenge eventually fails, the individual owes more because the authority took longer to decide. If it succeeds, the individual has still spent months under the possibility of collection. In effect, the agency controls both the duration of uncertainty and, in some cases, the size of the final bill.

Not all waiting should be blamed on the administration. Taxpayers submit incomplete records, change representatives, request postponements, or raise new issues late in an examination. Complex transactions genuinely take time to understand. A fair system therefore cannot impose a single processing deadline for every file. It can, however, distinguish between periods when the administration has enough information to proceed and periods when it is waiting for the taxpayer. Current systems already record notice dates, document uploads, calls, assignments, and decisions. The missing step is to connect those events to legal consequences.

This matters for distribution as well as efficiency. A large corporation can assign a tax manager to monitor a dormant case and may have sufficient credit to tolerate a delayed refund. A household or a five-person firm cannot spread the burden as easily. Formal equality—everyone waits in the same queue—therefore produces unequal practical effects. A compliance regime that ignores waiting time quietly favors taxpayers with liquidity, expertise, and organizational slack.

## The asymmetry weakens voluntary compliance

Tax authorities often describe prompt filing as a civic obligation rather than a mere response to enforcement. That appeal depends on reciprocity. The state asks taxpayers to keep records, disclose accurately, and respond on time; in return, it should administer the rules predictably and correct mistakes without avoidable delay.

A one-sided deadline system undermines that bargain. The problem is not simply that taxpayers dislike waiting. It is that the system sends a message about whose errors count. If a taxpayer is twelve days late, an automated charge may appear immediately. If the agency is twelve weeks late, the case may show only “in process.” The first delay is individualized, priced, and legally visible. The second disappears inside the institution.

That asymmetry can damage compliance even when each delay is explicable. People judge procedures partly by whether constraints apply consistently. A taxpayer who sees no consequence for administrative inaction may infer that deadlines are instruments of power rather than neutral coordination devices. Some will respond by becoming less cooperative: answering only what is strictly demanded, contesting routine requests, or postponing payment until compelled. Such reactions are not necessarily rational in a narrow financial sense, but compliance systems rely heavily on habits and perceived legitimacy.

There is a managerial consequence too. What is not measured is easy to defer. Agencies typically report total inventories and average processing times, but averages blur responsibility. A case waiting ninety days for taxpayer records is different from one waiting ninety days for internal assignment. Without that distinction, managers can reduce reported backlogs by closing simple files while difficult, fully documented cases continue to age. A dual clock would expose where delay is actually occurring.

The likely behavioral effect should not be exaggerated. Most taxpayers will not become enthusiastic about paying tax merely because a portal displays an honest timeline. Tax rates, complexity, enforcement, and social norms matter more. Still, procedural credibility is one element over which an administration has direct control, and it is wasteful to erode it through preventable asymmetry.

## How a dual-clock system would work

The reform would begin with a case-level event ledger accessible to both the taxpayer and the administration. The ledger would not display every internal note. It would show the events that determine responsibility for the next action: return received, information requested, response due, response received, file assigned, additional information required, preliminary decision issued, objection filed, and final decision issued.

Each unresolved issue would carry one of four statuses:

| Status | Clock running | Typical situation |
|---|---|---|
| Taxpayer action | Taxpayer | Requested records have not yet been provided |
| Administrative action | Agency | Complete response received; review or decision pending |
| Joint scheduling | Neither | Mutually agreed conference or temporary hold |
| External dependency | Special rule | Outcome depends on a court case or another public body |

The statuses would attach to issues rather than entire taxpayers. If a business has answered questions about payroll withholding but has not supplied records for a depreciation claim, the agency clock could run for the first issue while the taxpayer clock runs for the second. This prevents a missing document on one subject from excusing inactivity across the whole file.

The legal consequences should be graduated. First, when the agency clock is active, any taxpayer response deadline that depends on the pending decision should be suspended. A person should not have to preserve appeal rights against a position the administration has not yet explained. Second, interest on a genuinely disputed assessment should pause after a reasonable administrative interval, such as thirty days after a complete response. Interest could resume when a reasoned decision is issued. Third, if agency-controlled delay exceeds a longer threshold—perhaps 120 days—the taxpayer should receive a small service credit.

The credit should not be framed as damages. Its purpose is to make delay visible in the agency’s budget and to recognize the taxpayer’s carrying cost. A possible formula is 0.25 percent of the amount in dispute or the refund claimed for each full month beyond the threshold, subject to a modest floor and cap. For cases with no monetary amount, such as a delayed registration decision, a fixed credit could apply. Credits should be paid automatically rather than requiring a separate claim, since a remedy that generates another compliance process defeats much of its purpose.

The design must avoid rewarding taxpayers for manufacturing delay. The agency clock would start only after a response passes an objective completeness check. If essential items are absent, the ledger would identify them and remain on the taxpayer clock. If the agency later discovers that a response was materially misleading, it could correct the classification retrospectively, with reasons and a right of review. Repeated or vague demands for “anything else relevant,” however, should not allow the administration to keep the taxpayer clock running indefinitely. An information request must identify the issue and the records reasonably needed to address it.

A status designation should be challengeable through a fast, low-stakes process. The first review could be conducted by a service-resolution officer outside the case team and completed within ten working days. Only disputes with significant financial consequences would proceed to a tribunal. Otherwise, a reform intended to reduce uncertainty might produce a new layer of clock litigation.

## What a pilot suggests—and what it does not

The Kestrel Revenue Directorate tested a limited version of this model in its commercial registration unit. Over twelve months, 6,200 applicants could see whether their files were awaiting applicant documents or directorate action. Managers received weekly lists of files that had remained on the directorate clock for more than twenty working days. The pilot did not pause statutory interest because registration cases carried none, and it offered no service credit.

Median completion time fell from forty-four to thirty-six days. More revealingly, repeat calls asking for status fell by about one quarter. Staff interviews indicated that the visible clock helped supervisors find files that had been transferred between teams without clear ownership. Applicants also submitted missing documents more quickly when the portal named the exact item preventing progress.

These results support the modest proposition that responsibility can be made visible without paralyzing administration. They do not establish that clock-based interest relief will work in audits, objections, or high-value refund reviews. The registration cases were relatively standardized, the observation period was short, and the directorate did not publish a comparison group. Some of the improvement may have come from extra managerial attention rather than the clock itself.

Even so, the pilot illustrates an important mechanism. Transparency was useful not because it shamed individual officers but because it converted an amorphous backlog into a set of attributable states. Applicants knew when to act, and managers knew where files had stalled. The same operational clarity is likely to be valuable in tax administration, even if the financial remedies require more careful testing.

## Objections to pricing administrative delay

The strongest objection is fiscal. Stopping interest or paying credits could reduce revenue at precisely the time agencies are struggling with old systems and staff shortages. It might seem perverse to penalize an under-resourced authority for being under-resourced, leaving it with still less capacity to improve.

This concern is real, but it can be overstated. Continuing to charge taxpayers for time controlled by the agency does not create administrative capacity; it shifts the cost of scarcity onto people with unresolved cases. Moreover, status calls, premature appeals, duplicate submissions, and complaints consume staff time. A clearer process could recover some of the cost. Service credits could initially be capped at a small share of each unit’s operating budget so that they create pressure without destabilizing the agency.

A cap, however, weakens the remedy when delay is widespread. Once a unit reaches its annual limit, later taxpayers would receive less protection through no fault of their own. The better long-term answer is legislative funding tied to workload, but that answer depends on budget decisions outside the compliance system. The dual-clock proposal cannot by itself solve chronic underinvestment.

A second objection is that tax cases are too complex for binary responsibility. An auditor may possess the requested records but reasonably need clarification. A taxpayer may provide thousands of poorly indexed files that are technically complete but practically unusable. Legal uncertainty may require internal consultation. If the clock forces officials to decide prematurely, accuracy could suffer.

This is why the system needs more than a stopwatch. Officials should be able to return a file to taxpayer action when they identify a concrete deficiency, and external-dependency status should cover genuinely controlling litigation or interagency information. But complexity cannot become an all-purpose exemption. Reading evidence, consulting specialists, and resolving uncertain law are core administrative work. They may justify a longer service standard; they do not make the taxpayer responsible for the time consumed.

A third objection concerns strategic behavior. Taxpayers expecting to lose a dispute might flood the agency with material, hoping to start the government clock and stop interest. Others might deliberately choose paper channels that are harder to process. The completeness check answers only part of this risk, because officials may not immediately recognize evasive or disorganized submissions.

Anti-abuse rules would therefore be necessary. Deliberate document dumping, false statements, and refusal to use an available secure format could suspend interest relief. Yet these exceptions should require recorded reasons. If “poor organization” is left entirely to the case officer’s discretion, the protection will be least reliable for unrepresented taxpayers—the very people least able to package records professionally. The system should provide standard document categories and free indexing tools before penalizing disorganization.

A fourth objection is that pausing interest gives an advantage to taxpayers who dispute assessments over those who pay immediately and seek a refund later. The former retain cash without financing cost, while the latter surrender it to the government. That difference could encourage weak objections.

One answer is to pay equivalent interest on amounts deposited pending review. Another is to permit taxpayers to make a reversible suspense payment that stops ordinary interest but remains segregated until a decision. Neither solution is administratively simple. The proposal therefore risks replacing one asymmetry with another unless payment and refund rules are redesigned alongside the clock.

Finally, some will argue that deadlines should be enforced through managerial targets, not individual financial entitlements. Service standards can account for case complexity and avoid satellite disputes over status classifications. But purely internal targets have repeatedly proven easy to revise, average, or suspend. An individual entitlement changes incentives because failure has a consequence that cannot be erased from a performance dashboard. The sensible approach is not to choose between management and rights: internal targets should govern ordinary work, while legal remedies should address prolonged, agency-controlled delay.

## A practical route to implementation

Implementation should begin where event data are already reliable: amended returns, routine refund reviews, and administrative objections involving a defined assessment. It should not begin with the most complex anti-avoidance investigations, where issue boundaries and information sufficiency are hardest to determine.

For the first year, the ledger could operate without service credits. The administration would publish monthly figures showing time on each clock, transfers between statuses, and the distribution of delay rather than only the average. An independent review panel should examine a sample of status decisions, with particular attention to whether unrepresented taxpayers are disproportionately classified as incomplete.

The second phase should introduce automatic deadline suspension and interest relief. These are more closely connected to fairness than a cash credit: they prevent the government from creating procedural default or increasing a disputed debt through its own inaction. The legislation should specify a standard grace period but permit longer periods for defined categories, such as valuations involving overseas assets. Exceptions should expire unless periodically justified with processing data.

Only after those rules are stable should service credits begin. The initial amounts ought to be deliberately modest. If credits are too large, the administration may become overly defensive, issue premature denials, or devote excessive resources to easy cases with high disputed amounts. A floor is nevertheless needed so that low-income taxpayers and small claims do not become invisible. A fixed 30-crown monthly minimum and a 1,500-crown annual cap per issue would be a plausible starting point, though those figures should be adjusted for local incomes and borrowing costs.

The agency’s public reporting must also resist a predictable distortion: optimizing the clock rather than the service. Officials might issue unnecessary requests to move files back to taxpayer action or close cases with cursory decisions before the credit threshold. Audits should therefore track not only elapsed time but also repeated requests, reopened cases, successful appeals, and decisions issued shortly before a deadline. Speed without accuracy is not compliance improvement.

None of this requires pretending that every delay has a villain. Files stall because software fails, staff leave, evidence conflicts, and legislation creates difficult boundaries. The point of the ledger is not to assign moral blame to individual employees. It is to allocate the practical cost of time to the institution that controls the next step. Governments routinely ask taxpayers to build systems that remain compliant during holidays, staff turnover, and technical problems. Tax administrations should be expected to design for the same foreseeable disruptions.

## Time should be part of tax legality

Tax systems confer coercive powers that ordinary creditors do not possess. They can require disclosure, calculate interest by statute, seize assets after prescribed steps, and make taxpayers prove entitlement to deductions or refunds. Such authority may be necessary, but it strengthens the case for reciprocal discipline.

The dual clock would not make every decision fast. Nor would it settle how much tax is owed. It would do something narrower: identify who controls the next action and prevent administrative waiting from remaining legally invisible. That change would improve case management, give taxpayers clearer instructions, and reduce the inequity between those who can absorb uncertainty and those who cannot.

The central principle is simple. When a taxpayer controls the next step, the taxpayer should bear the consequences of delay. When the administration controls it, the administration should bear at least part of those consequences. A compliance system that measures only the citizen’s lateness is not truly organized around timeliness. It is organized around one-sided power. Stopping the compliance clock would be a practical way to replace that asymmetry with accountable administration.
