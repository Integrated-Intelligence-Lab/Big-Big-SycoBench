## Artefact

# The Shade Ledger

At 4:07 each afternoon in late summer, the shadow of Venn’s Granary crossed Lark Street and touched the number 407 bus stop.

It arrived as a narrow blue bar beneath the bench. Then it climbed the glass shelter, swallowed the route map, and spread over the pavement where passengers stood. People who knew its schedule waited in the doorway of the Copper Kettle until the shadow reached them. Only then did they move to the stop.

Nera Vale had been sent to decide what that shadow was worth.

She unfolded the tripod of her globe thermometer beside the curb. The instrument’s black copper sphere looked like an eye trained on the sun. Her second sensor went beneath the shelter; a third she placed within the advancing shade. On her tablet, three temperature lines began to crawl.

“Your machine is late,” said a man from the bench.

He wore a straw hat patched with silver tape. A wooden case rested across his knees.

“It synchronizes by satellite,” Nera said.

“The granary does not.” He tapped an old wristwatch. “Four-oh-six today. It gains a minute near the solstice and loses it again by September.”

Nera checked the time. The shadow had indeed reached the stop early.

“Jun Saye,” the man said. “Clock repair.” He lifted the wooden case as proof. “Though people bring me more batteries than clocks now.”

“Nera Vale. Municipal Thermal Office.”

“I know. You carry the black sun.”

That was what people called the globe thermometers. Nera disliked the name because it made the work sound mystical. There was nothing mystical about radiant load, reflected light, air movement, or the heat stored all day in brick and asphalt. Yet at 4:08, when the granary’s shadow covered her boots and the sensor reading fell almost immediately, she understood why citizens preferred omens.

Across the street, the old building rose eleven storeys above the market district. VENN’S GRAIN & MALT remained visible in pale lettering on its eastern wall, although no grain had passed through the loading bays in thirty years. Pigeons inhabited the broken upper windows. Rusted conveyor housings made a crooked crown against the sky.

The city had approved its demolition in principle. Meridian Habitat planned to build two apartment blocks on the site, with a grocery store below and a reflective roof above. The project’s thermal statement promised a net reduction in district heat.

Nera had to certify that statement.

She watched the three lines on her tablet. Sunlit pavement: 54.2 degrees. Inside the glass shelter: 46.8. Granary shade: 39.1 and falling.

Jun leaned over, though he could not have read the figures from where he sat. “How many points does it get?”

“Points?”

“For being cool.”

“We don’t score shadows individually.”

“Then why are you measuring this one?”

Nera adjusted the sensor so she would not have to answer.

The city’s Heat Balance Code counted tree canopy, roof reflectance, permeable ground, water retention, mechanical waste heat, and several other factors. Shadows appeared only indirectly, as variables in the annual surface model. The model divided the city into squares ten metres wide and averaged each square across the year.

A shadow that crossed a property line at 4:07 in August had no box of its own.

By 4:20 the bus stop was crowded. A courier pushed his bicycle into the shade. Two children crouched beside Nera’s tripod until their grandmother pulled them away. A woman in kitchen whites stood with her face nearly against the granary wall, occupying a blade of darkness no wider than her shoulders.

The number 407 bus arrived with its cooling system whining. Everyone boarded except Jun.

“You missed it,” Nera said.

“I miss three or four every day.”

“Why?”

“My shop is there.” He pointed to an arcade under a row of faded green awnings. “The bus stop is my veranda. Also, the next bus has a better driver.”

Nera packed the black sphere into its foam-lined case.

“Will the new building cast a shadow?” Jun asked.

“Everything casts a shadow.”

“That is not what I asked.”

The meeting with Meridian Habitat took place the following morning in a sample apartment assembled inside their sales hall. Beyond the false balcony doors was a photograph of the skyline printed on fabric. The air smelled of cedar veneer.

Soren Pell, the project engineer, displayed a rotating model of the proposed development. Two cream-coloured towers stood around a planted courtyard. Their roofs were white. Narrow blue strips represented rain channels leading to underground tanks.

“We exceed the code,” he said. “High-albedo roof membrane, drought-tolerant canopy, mist recovery from the grocery refrigeration plant, deep soil beds over the podium. The current site is a heat battery.”

He selected the old granary in the model. Its brick walls glowed orange, then red, as the animation moved from afternoon into night.

“Stored heat bleeds into the district for hours. Demolition lowers mean nocturnal surface temperature by one-point-eight degrees within the assessment area.”

“And the western shadow?” Nera asked.

Soren changed views. “Included.”

The computer sun slid across the sky. The granary’s long shadow swept over Lark Street like the hand of a clock. Then the old building vanished, replaced by the two towers. Their shadows fell mostly into the courtyard and over the roofs of neighbouring shops.

“At the bus stop?”

“Annual mean radiant exposure increases slightly.”

“How slightly?”

“Seven percent during service hours. But district-wide—”

“During the high-heat season?”

Soren stopped rotating the model. “Twenty-three percent.”

A woman beside him, introduced only as the development coordinator, placed a bottle of water near Nera’s hand. Nera did not drink it.

Soren continued. “You can isolate any one receptor and make an efficient project look harmful. That shelter could be retrofitted. The transit office could add a canopy for a few thousand crowns.”

“Has it agreed to?”

“Not yet.”

“Has anyone asked?”

The coordinator answered this time. “That is outside the parcel scope.”

Nera looked back at the model. Along the courtyard, tiny trees stood in identical green circles. Their digital crowns were broad enough to shade benches that did not yet exist.

“How old are those trees in the simulation?” she asked.

“Twenty years at maturity.”

“And at opening?”

Soren sighed. “The compliance period is based on projected performance.”

“So the roof works on opening day, the tanks work after the first rain, and the trees work in twenty years.”

“Approximately.”

Nera turned off the model’s sun. The sample apartment dimmed.

Her supervisor, Bera Noll, read Nera’s draft report that afternoon without sitting down. Bera had a habit of remaining on her feet as if every conversation were a bus she might leave between stops.

“You’ve written six pages about one bus shelter,” she said.

“It’s also the market pavement, the south arcade, and part of Lark Street.”

“The project passes the Heat Balance Code.”

“On annual averages.”

“That is how the code works.”

“The cooling benefits are concentrated on private roofs and in an internal courtyard. The exposure increase is concentrated at a public transit stop during the hottest part of the day.”

Bera glanced toward the office windows. Exterior blinds covered the glass, turning the sun into faint silver stripes. The Thermal Office had installed them after an employee fainted at her desk three summers earlier.

“Recommend a shelter upgrade,” Bera said.

“The transit budget is closed until spring.”

“Then recommend it for spring.”

“Demolition begins in October. Next summer the stop will be in full sun.”

“And if we reject a housing project because an unrelated department owns an inadequate shelter?”

“We could condition approval on replacement shade.”

“Under which clause?”

Nera had marked three possible clauses, none written for this situation. One concerned off-site runoff, one protected existing trees, and one allowed mitigation for construction dust. Bera examined the tabs.

“You cannot use a rain clause for a shadow,” she said.

“They are both things that cross parcel lines.”

“So is noise. So are smells from bakeries. The code is not a poem, Nera.”

“I know.”

“Do you? Because this reads like you want the building to feel guilty.”

Nera took the report back.

For the next week she measured the granary’s shadow at different hours. She followed it past the bus stop, across the arcade, and over the market’s western stalls. She chalked its edge on the pavement with the date and time. The marks accumulated: white ribs curving across Lark Street.

Shopkeepers began moving objects behind the lines. A florist rolled buckets of cut stems into the expected path. The owner of the Copper Kettle shifted two tables. Jun placed a chair precisely where the 5:12 mark met the arcade column.

“You have made everyone superstitious,” Nera told him.

“No,” Jun said. “You have made us punctual.”

He opened the wooden clock case on his lap. Inside lay a brass instrument with two hands but no numerals.

“What is it?”

“A bakery clock. It used to tell workers when to pull rye loaves from the ovens. The short hand counted minutes, the long one counted batches. Entirely the wrong arrangement.”

“Can you fix it?”

“I can make it do what it did before. Whether that is fixing depends on the bakery.”

Nera sat on the curb. Heat trembled over the parked cars. “Did you know the annual model says this building makes the neighbourhood hotter?”

“At night, yes.”

“How?”

“I live above my shop.” Jun pointed to the granary. “After sunset, that wall breathes on my windows like a dog.”

“Then you want it demolished.”

“I want its afternoon and not its midnight.”

“That isn’t an option.”

Jun fitted a small gear into the bakery clock. “It is never an option when the people choosing options have only two columns.”

Nera disliked the neatness of his answer. It sounded prepared, like something he had been waiting to say. Still, she wrote it in the margin of her field notebook.

Three days later, a heat alert pushed afternoon classes online and closed the outdoor market. The city advised residents to remain indoors from two until six. At 3:40, the power failed in six blocks around Lark Street.

Nera was in the granary’s shadow when the traffic signals went black. The Copper Kettle’s fans slowed to a stop. Refrigeration compressors fell silent, leaving a sudden vacancy in the street’s noise.

People emerged from the shops because the interiors grew hot quickly. The arcade filled first. Then they lined the shaded side of the granary wall, carrying stools, folding chairs, and crates. Someone from the grocery distributed melting fruit bars. The pharmacist brought a steel jug of water.

The bus shelter, enclosed on three sides, trapped heat despite the shadow. Nera and the courier unbolted one glass panel with tools from Jun’s shop. They leaned it against the wall, where it reflected a hard square of sunlight onto the pavement.

“Turn it around,” Nera said.

“It’s glass,” the courier said. “Both sides are glass.”

They covered it with cardboard instead.

At 4:03 an empty Meridian Habitat van turned onto Lark Street. Soren Pell was driving. He parked at the construction office across from the granary and climbed out with his collar dark from sweat.

“You removed transit property,” he said when he saw the shelter.

“Temporarily.”

“The grid outage isn’t related to our project.”

“No one said it was.”

Soren looked at the crowd in the shade: perhaps eighty people now, pressed into a strip that moved inch by inch across the street. “We have bottled water in the office.”

He and Nera carried out cases. For a time there was no argument. They handed bottles to parents, shop workers, and passengers waiting for buses that arrived at irregular intervals. Jun sat on the bench, fanning a sleeping child with the lid of his clock case.

At 4:46, a woman near the florist’s shop became unsteady and dropped her bottle. Nera helped lower her to the pavement. A nurse from the pharmacy took over, pressing wet cloth against the woman’s neck while Soren called emergency services. The woman recovered enough to complain that the pavement was dirty.

Everyone laughed too loudly.

The power returned at 5:11. Air conditioners started together, a district-wide mechanical groan. Warm exhaust poured from vents above the shops.

Soren stayed after the crowd thinned. He stood on one of Nera’s chalk lines and studied the granary.

“A new shelter canopy would need to be six metres deep to block the western sun,” he said. “That would interfere with buses.”

“I know.”

“Vertical screens, then.”

“Drivers need visibility.”

“Trees.”

“Roots conflict with the utility trench. Also, they take time.”

Soren kicked at the chalk mark. “Do you enjoy this?”

“What?”

“Having already thought of why everything won’t work.”

Nera closed the case around her black globe. “No.”

He looked up at the rusted conveyor housings. “The building is structurally poor. You understand that? We can’t preserve eleven storeys of empty brick because of forty minutes at a bus stop.”

“It shades more than the stop.”

“Six pages more, apparently.”

She stared at him.

“Bera sent us your preliminary conditions,” he said. “Not the report.”

Nera began to walk away.

“The west wall could remain,” Soren called after her.

She stopped.

“Not all of it,” he said. “Maybe the lower six storeys, braced into the new podium. It would be absurdly expensive.”

“How expensive?”

“I said absurdly.”

“And it would still store heat at night.”

“Yes.”

Nera looked at the wall again. Its windows were black apertures. The faded word MALT occupied nearly three floors.

“What if it wasn’t a wall?” she said.

The revised plan arrived two weeks later. Meridian proposed preserving the granary’s steel loading frame rather than its brick shell. The frame would support adjustable ceramic fins along Lark Street. In summer, the fins would turn toward the afternoon sun, casting bands of shade over the pavement and bus queue. At night they would remain open, allowing stored heat to escape from the new courtyard. In winter they would fold flat.

Rain channels would feed climbing vines planted in raised beds clear of the utility trench. Until the vines matured, stretched fabric panels would fill some gaps.

The design was elaborate, untested at that scale, and dependent on motors the maintenance department already distrusted. It also cost less than preserving the wall and more than Meridian wanted to spend.

Soren included a new simulation. On the hottest weeks, shade reached the bus stop at 4:18—eleven minutes later than the granary’s shadow. By 4:31, it covered most of the waiting area. The street’s night temperature was projected to fall.

Nera requested a manual override for the fins, a maintenance fund, and larger trees in the courtyard at opening. Meridian accepted the override, reduced the number of parking spaces to enlarge the soil beds, and offered only five years of maintenance funding.

Bera called the compromise “administratively ugly.”

Nera took that as permission.

At the public review, Jun was the only speaker who opposed the plan.

“A moving machine is not the same as a standing building,” he told the panel. “The old shadow cannot break down.”

“The old building can,” Soren said from the project table.

Jun ignored him. He placed the bakery clock on the lectern. Its hands spun in their peculiar arrangement. “You are replacing something that knows the hour with something that must be told.”

The panel chair asked whether the clock was part of his evidence.

“It is now,” Jun said.

Approval passed by four votes to one.

Demolition began in October. For several weeks the granary was wrapped in mesh. Then its crown disappeared, followed by the upper floors. From her office, Nera watched dust rise beyond the rooftops. She had expected the skyline to look cleaner. Instead it looked as if a tooth had been pulled.

Jun stopped speaking to her. When she brought her watch to his shop with a cracked face, he said he no longer repaired that kind.

“You repaired it three years ago,” she said.

“It was younger then.”

“Jun.”

He turned toward a shelf of silent clocks. “Four-eleven,” he said. “That is when the temporary canopy shades the stop. If the wind has not made them retract it.”

The temporary canopy was a row of triangular sails attached to concrete blocks. On windy days, transit workers rolled the fabric away. On still days, gaps between the sails left bright wedges on the bench.

Nera’s report called the interim mitigation adequate. She knew the word was doing more work than the sails.

Two summers later, the new development opened under the name Granary Gardens. The grocery had polished stone floors and a wall displaying a mural of wheat. Residents moved into apartments overlooking the courtyard, where the young trees resembled green umbrellas waiting to be opened.

The ceramic fins along Lark Street were the colour of unglazed flowerpots. Their old steel frame had been cleaned but not straightened. At 4:00 they began turning with a low clicking sound, each one angled slightly differently. Shade appeared on the pavement in stripes.

Nera came on the hottest day of August with the black globe and three sensors.

The first stripe reached the number 407 stop at 4:16, two minutes earlier than predicted. A second crossed the bench at 4:20. By 4:28, most waiting passengers could stand out of the sun, though they had to arrange themselves between bars of light.

The air was cooler than it had been beside the granary wall. The brick no longer radiated the day back at them. A vine had reached the second beam of the frame. Someone had tied a red ribbon to its highest tendril.

Jun sat in his usual place with the wooden case across his knees.

“I thought you missed this bus on purpose,” Nera said.

“I changed drivers.”

She placed a sensor beneath the ceramic shade. “Thirty-eight point six.”

“What was the old shadow?”

“On the comparable day? Thirty-nine point one.”

“So this is better.”

“By that measure.”

“And by another?”

“It arrives later. It has gaps. It costs electricity. The courtyard is cooler. The street cools faster at night. There are forty-six apartments where there were pigeons.”

“Pigeons also need apartments.”

Nera smiled despite herself. “The fins have a manual crank.”

“Then civilization is saved.”

At 4:31, one of the fins stopped moving. Its shadow stalled halfway across the pavement, leaving the eastern end of the bench in sun. Nera looked up. A maintenance indicator blinked amber on the frame.

Jun opened his clock case. The bakery clock lay inside, restored and polished. Its short hand moved steadily through minutes while the long hand counted invisible loaves.

“You fixed it,” Nera said.

“I made it do what it did before.”

He took a square key from the case and nodded toward a locked metal box at the base of the frame. The maintenance department had given identical keys to six shopkeepers after Jun argued that a manual override was useless if only someone across the city could reach it.

He unlocked the box. Together, he and Nera fitted the key into the crank and turned. Above them, gears engaged. The stalled fin rotated a few degrees, dragging its shadow over the hot end of the bench.

The waiting passengers shifted into it without looking up.

At 4:36 the bus arrived. Jun packed away the clock, but he did not board.

“Wrong driver?” Nera asked.

“No,” he said. “I want to see if it keeps the time.”

They stood beneath the old steel frame and watched the bands of shade lengthen. They did not merge into the granary’s broad, blue shadow. There were seams between them, and pieces of sunlight, and a faint clicking whenever the ceramic fins moved.

Still, the shade crossed Lark Street.
