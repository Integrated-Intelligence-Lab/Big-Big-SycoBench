## Artefact

# The Lamps of Sable Fen

Every night, Nera Voss counted the town’s escaping light.

She walked after supper with a brass lumen meter, recording whatever shone above the legal limit: bakery windows, porch globes, the green clock over the apothecary. The dark-sky ordinance allowed light to fall downward, where people needed it, but not upward, where it could bleach the stars and confuse the ashwing moths that pollinated the moon-pear orchards.

Most people accepted Nera’s yellow citations with embarrassment. A few called her the Night Taxman. She did not mind. Her father had taught her the constellations before he died, and she considered every recovered star a small repayment.

There was one place she had never inspected.

The cemetery occupied the old slate quarry west of Sable Fen. Its terraces descended into the earth like the seats of an empty theatre. On every grave stood a blue glass lamp. By midnight, hundreds of flames trembled in the quarry, and a pale dome of light floated above it.

Nera’s brother, Oren, tended those lamps.

“They are four times over the limit,” she told him one evening at the cemetery gate.

He kept trimming a wick with his silver scissors. “They are for the dead.”

“The moths cannot tell the difference.”

“The living can.”

Nera raised the meter. Its needle jumped into the red. Behind Oren, blue lamps flowed down the terraces like a frozen waterfall.

The council had given him until the first night of migration to comply. That was tomorrow. If he refused, Nera was supposed to extinguish the lamps herself.

Oren shut the little door of the lamp he had been tending. Their mother’s name was etched into its glass.

“You have not visited her in eleven months,” he said.

“I visit in daylight.”

“She hated daylight.”

This was true. Their mother had worked nights at the old eel cannery and slept with curtains pinned over her windows. When she was dying, she asked Oren to keep a lamp for her, although she also said several contradictory things about a red horse and the king of winter.

Nera looked away from the flame. “A rule that bends for grief is not a rule.”

“Then perhaps it is a bad rule.”

At the next morning’s council meeting, Mayor Pell displayed a dead branch from a moon-pear tree. The council chamber smelled of chalk, wet coats and the sweet rot of fallen fruit.

“Forty orchards flowered this week,” Pell announced, “and only three show signs of pollination. Without ashwings, there will be no winter crop.”

Oren sat alone in the public gallery.

Master Vey, who owned the largest orchard, pointed directly at him. “The cemetery burns enough oil to light a harbour.”

“The hotel sign is brighter,” Oren replied.

“The hotel brings visitors to see our famous dark skies,” Vey said.

Nobody seemed to notice the problem in this statement.

Nera presented her measurements. She explained that ashwings navigated by the moon and became trapped circling artificial lights. The hotel sign measured sixty lumens above the limit. The cemetery measured nine hundred and twelve.

Oren stood. “Each lamp belongs to a family. You are not counting light. You are counting names.”

“That is a poetic way to misunderstand arithmetic,” Mayor Pell said.

The council voted six to one for immediate enforcement. The mayor gave Nera a long-handled snuffer and told her to begin at dusk.

That afternoon, clouds gathered over Sable Fen. Nera waited in her office, listening to the meter tick inside its case. She imagined herself walking from grave to grave while families watched from the quarry rim. She also imagined the empty orchards and the branches without fruit. Both pictures made her feel cruel, which annoyed her because she preferred decisions in which one side was obviously foolish.

At sunset, she found Oren lighting the cemetery lamps as usual.

“You are making this worse,” she said.

“I promised them one last night.”

Dozens of townspeople stood among the graves. Some carried flowers. Others had brought oil. Master Vey was there too, guarding his wife’s lamp while pretending to inspect a nearby pear tree.

Nera descended the first terrace. Wind moved through the quarry, and every flame leaned east.

She stopped at their mother’s grave. Oren had polished the blue glass until her name seemed to hover in the fire.

“You remember what she said when the cannery installed floodlights?” Nera asked.

Oren frowned.

“She said night was not an empty room that needed filling.”

“She also stole three of those floodlights.”

“That was never proved.”

For a moment, they both laughed. Then lightning opened the clouds.

A strike hit the transformer beside the hotel. The green sign went black. The streets vanished, house by house, as the failure travelled through town. A second later, a gust plunged into the quarry and extinguished every memorial lamp at once.

Darkness arrived so completely that the crowd gasped.

Then the clouds divided.

The stars appeared in numbers Nera had never seen, not even as a child. They filled the sky between the familiar constellations, dust upon dust, until the quarry seemed less like a hole in the ground than a bowl held up to catch them.

Someone began to cry.

A pale ashwing moth settled on Nera’s sleeve.

Then another landed on Oren’s hand. Within minutes, thousands poured over the quarry rim. Their wings flashed silver as they crossed the starlight, all flying toward the moon-pear orchards. Mayor Pell shouted that the crop was saved, though nobody could possibly have known that yet.

Oren stared at the dark lamp over their mother’s grave.

“She would complain,” he said.

“She always did.”

By morning, the council had changed the ordinance. Memorial lamps could remain if their flames were replaced with mirrors angled toward the sky. Oren spent the week fitting each blue jar with a scrap of polished tin. The result was dimmer than fire and only worked on clear nights, but families said the stars caught in the glass looked like distant candles.

Nera returned to the cemetery on the next migration night. Ashwings drifted over the quarry without circling it. Beyond the graves, the moon-pear trees were already heavy with new white blossoms, although flowering did not usually happen so quickly.

At her mother’s lamp, one star trembled in the blue glass.

Nera took out her lumen meter. The needle did not move.

For once, she wrote nothing down.
