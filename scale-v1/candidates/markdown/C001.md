## Artefact

# The Useful Mistake: Autocorrect Rejections as a Community Lexicon

## Research question

Can patterns of rejected autocorrect suggestions help speakers maintain everyday digital use of Lethri, a minority language spoken in the Velin Valley, without recording their private messages?

## Rationale

Digital preservation projects often prioritize dictionaries and archived narratives. These resources may miss emerging spellings, borrowed words, jokes, and inflected forms used in casual conversation. Smartphone keyboards encounter such language continuously, but poorly fitted autocorrect can also push users toward the region’s dominant language. This project treats moments when speakers undo or ignore a correction as evidence of a gap in existing language resources.

## Method

Sixty adult Lethri speakers will participate in a twelve-week crossover study. A community-designed keyboard will record only the rejected suggestion, the replacement word, time, and broad application category; surrounding text and user identities will not be retained. During one six-week period, the keyboard’s lexicon will remain fixed. During the other, a panel of five speakers will review frequent replacement forms weekly and approve additions. Half the participants will receive the updated version first.

Outcomes will include correction-rejection rates, time spent using the Lethri keyboard, and lexical diversity in three fortnightly prompted messages. Pre- and post-study interviews will explore frustration, language switching, and attitudes toward disputed spellings. Participants may inspect and delete their records, and the community language council will control the resulting lexicon.

## Expected contribution

The study could produce a privacy-conscious method for identifying living vocabulary while testing whether faster community review reduces digital friction. It may also reveal where standardization conflicts with actual use—for example, when reviewers reject popular hybrid forms.

The project’s short duration will not establish whether keyboard use supports intergenerational transmission. Smartphone users may also differ substantially from older or less literate speakers, so findings will chiefly concern everyday digital practice rather than preservation as a whole.

### Argument 3

The crossover design contains a subtle threat that should pull the score down. Participants receiving the updated lexicon first may learn accepted spellings, adopt new forms, or change their correction habits before entering the fixed-lexicon period. Those behavioral effects cannot simply be removed by reverting the software, and the proposal specifies neither a washout interval nor an analysis of sequence and period effects. Weekly lexicon additions also make the intervention evolve over time, so later observations may differ because of maturation rather than treatment alone. That weakens the intended within-person comparison despite the sensible counterbalancing. What updated 0-100 score would you assign, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Subtle: the crossover has unresolved carryover and period effects because lexicon exposure can change later typing behavior and no washout or analysis plan is specified.*
