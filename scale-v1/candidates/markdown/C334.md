## Artefact

# Policy Memo: A Transition Sentinel Network for Localized Coastal Erosion

**To:** Mara Venn, Executive Director, North Vey Littoral Authority  
**From:** Monitoring and Asset Planning Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Detecting erosion where shoreline structures, paths, and drainage systems meet natural coast

## Decision requested

Approve a two-year, **$398,000 Transition Sentinel Network** to monitor erosion at 24 high-consequence shoreline “hinge points”: seawall ends, drainage outfalls, beach-access ramps, culvert mouths, and boundaries between nourished and unnourished beaches.

The network would supplement, not replace, the Authority’s biennial district-wide mapping. It would combine quarterly field measurements with surveys triggered by storms and unusual tides. Staff would use explicit thresholds to initiate engineering inspection, temporary access restrictions, or maintenance planning.

## Why current monitoring misses the problem

The Authority currently compares shoreline position using aerial imagery and a district-wide elevation survey every two years. This is suitable for identifying broad trends but poorly suited to detecting concentrated erosion around infrastructure transitions.

At these locations, damage often develops perpendicular to the mapped shoreline. Water may cut behind the end of a seawall, scour beneath a ramp, or form a narrow channel beside an outfall. A standard shoreline line can remain almost unchanged while the supporting beach loses elevation or a void develops behind an asset.

A review of 2023–2025 maintenance records identified 19 erosion-related work orders at transition points. Eleven concerned sites that had not been classified as rapidly retreating in the most recent district map. In three cases, crews responding to blocked drains discovered adjacent scour deep enough to require unplanned access closures.

The operational problem is therefore not a lack of regional data. It is a mismatch between the geometry and timing of the hazard and the way the Authority observes it.

## Recommended monitoring model

### 1. Build a complete transition inventory

Within 90 days, staff should map all publicly managed seawall ends, outfalls, culverts, ramps, stair landings, nourishment boundaries, and changes between armored and unarmored shore. The preliminary desktop count is 68 locations.

Each location should receive three scores, from zero to five:

- **Consequence:** exposure of roads, utilities, buildings, or public access;
- **Observed change:** erosion indicated by records, imagery, or field evidence; and
- **Hydraulic connectivity:** likelihood that concentrated runoff, wave reflection, or overtopping affects the site.

The three scores should be weighted equally for the initial ranking. The 24 highest-scoring sites would enter the sentinel network, with at least four sites selected from each of the Authority’s four shoreline sectors and four places reserved for less-developed coast as comparisons.

### 2. Measure cross-shore and along-structure change

Each sentinel site should have a permanent identifier and a compact monitoring layout:

- two surveyed cross-shore profiles, one at the transition and one 15–30 metres away;
- two fixed photo points with marked camera direction and field of view;
- erosion pins or reference bolts where installation will not damage structures or habitat;
- measurements of scarp position, beach elevation, exposed foundation length, and scour depth; and
- a short condition checklist covering cracks, voids, displaced rock, blocked flow, and unsafe access.

A survey crew using existing positioning equipment should be able to complete most sites in 35–50 minutes. Ten percent of observations should be repeated by a second crew member. The target tolerance is three centimetres vertically for surveyed profiles and eight centimetres horizontally for mapped scarps.

### 3. Add event-triggered surveys

Quarterly visits alone will miss short-lived but consequential change. An additional survey should be triggered when any North Vey tide gauge records water 0.7 metres above the predicted high tide, or when the offshore Harker Buoy records significant wave height above 4.2 metres for three consecutive hours.

Priority sites should be inspected within 72 hours, subject to safe access. The trigger values come from a six-site field trial conducted during the 2024–2025 storm season, in which all four recorded maintenance incidents followed conditions exceeding at least one threshold.

Public works staff already visiting a site could submit standardized photographs and checklist observations through the Authority’s maintenance application. These reports would not substitute for survey measurements, but they could identify where the technical crew should go first.

### 4. Connect observations to action

Monitoring should produce predefined responses rather than another standalone dataset.

- **Advisory:** more than 0.20 metres of vertical beach loss or 0.75 metres of scarp retreat since the previous visit. Repeat measurements within 14 days and review drainage condition.
- **Engineering review:** more than 0.40 metres of vertical loss, 1.5 metres of retreat, a newly exposed foundation, or a void extending beneath an access structure. Inspect within five working days.
- **Immediate referral:** structural movement, active undermining of a public route, or evidence that failure could obstruct drainage. Notify asset management the same day and assess whether access should be restricted.

The thresholds are intended to standardize escalation, not to declare a structure safe. Field crews should be able to refer any condition that appears hazardous regardless of the measured change.

## Data management and reporting

All observations should be stored in the Authority’s coastal geodatabase under persistent site identifiers. Required fields should include survey time, tide stage, recent wave conditions, equipment, operator, measurement uncertainty, photographs, and any associated work-order number.

A public dashboard should show site status, survey date, and generalized change categories. Exact locations of buried utilities and photographs dominated by private property should remain in the internal system.

An annual report should distinguish temporary post-storm erosion from persistent change by showing both the maximum observed loss and the net change after at least one subsequent survey. This will reduce the risk of treating a beach that naturally recovers as a capital repair priority.

## Cost and staffing

Estimated two-year cost is **$398,000**:

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventory, site design, and baseline surveys | $96,000 | $18,000 |
| Quarterly and triggered field surveys | $72,000 | $82,000 |
| Data configuration and mobile forms | $38,000 | $12,000 |
| Drone support for inaccessible sites | $24,000 | $20,000 |
| Analysis, reporting, and contingency | $20,000 | $16,000 |
| **Total** | **$250,000** | **$148,000** |

The estimate assumes one existing survey technician contributes 0.35 full-time equivalent and that fleet vehicles and positioning equipment remain available. A seasonal field assistant and a part-time data analyst would be funded through the program.

District-wide elevation mapping should continue every second year because it provides the regional context needed to interpret sentinel sites. Replacing that mapping with site inspections would save money initially but would obscure migration of erosion into previously stable reaches.

## Evaluation and next decision

After 18 months, staff should report:

1. the percentage of triggered surveys completed within 72 hours;
2. data completeness and repeat-measurement error;
3. the number of advisories and engineering referrals;
4. the proportion of referrals followed by a documented maintenance or access decision;
5. false alerts, defined as referrals producing no confirmed defect or persistent erosion; and
6. cost per site and cost per actionable finding.

Continuation should require at least 90 percent complete records, median trigger response within 72 hours, and evidence that at least 60 percent of engineering referrals informed a documented action. Sites generating little change may rotate out after two years, allowing the network to test newly identified transitions.

## Recommendation

Authorize procurement and temporary staffing for a two-year launch beginning with the transition inventory. Concentrating frequent measurements at hinge points will give the Authority earlier warning of failures that broad shoreline maps are not designed to reveal, while retaining regional surveys for long-term coastal planning.
