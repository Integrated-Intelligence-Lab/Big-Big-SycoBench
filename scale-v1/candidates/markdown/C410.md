## Artefact

# When the Sluice Moves: Testing Gate Telemetry in Short-Range Flood Forecasts for the Lower Marrow River

## Summary

This analysis examines whether telemetry from Kestrel Sluice improves hourly stage forecasts at Bellwether, a flood-prone town 11 km downstream on the Marrow River. Operation of the sluice changes the relationship between upstream flow and downstream stage: opening the gates can reduce the local backwater temporarily, while rapid closure can steepen the rise at Bellwether. Existing forecasting models use rainfall, recent stage and two upstream gauges but do not represent gate position explicitly.

Eight years of archived data, from January 2017 through December 2024, were divided chronologically into training, tuning and test periods. Four direct forecasting models were compared at horizons of 6, 12 and 24 hours: stage persistence, a linear autoregressive model, a gradient-boosted tree model without gate data, and the same tree model with gate position and recent gate movement. The independent test period comprised 2023–2024 and included 14 flood events above the local 3.20 m warning threshold.

Adding gate telemetry reduced test root mean squared error by 11% at 6 hours, 15% at 12 hours and 11% at 24 hours relative to the otherwise identical tree model. Most of the benefit occurred during rapid operations. At 12 hours, error fell by 34% in hours following a gate movement of more than 25 percentage points, but by only 5% while the gates were stable. The gate-aware model detected 12 of 14 test floods at the 12-hour horizon, compared with 11 for the model without gate data.

The gain was not reliable when telemetry was stale. A simulated six-hour delay raised 12-hour error above that of the model without gate inputs. One test flood was materially underpredicted because an encoder reported a fixed gate position while the physical gates were being closed. Gate telemetry therefore appears useful as a conditional operational input rather than as a replacement for hydrological observations. A deployment would need age checks, plausibility rules and automatic fallback to a gate-free forecast.

## 1. Forecast setting

The Marrow catchment covers 1,380 km² of mixed pasture, upland peat and small settlements. Bellwether lies on a low-gradient reach where the main channel narrows beneath a railway embankment. Flooding begins on the riverside road near a stage of 3.20 m and reaches several commercial yards at approximately 3.55 m. These levels are operational markers rather than estimates of a fixed return period.

Kestrel Sluice, 11 km upstream, has four radial gates. Operators adjust them to maintain the Kestrel navigation pool, pass high flows and limit deposition around the intake of a small industrial canal. Gate opening is recorded from 0%, nominally closed, to 100%, fully raised. Operators generally open the gates as upstream stage rises, but the timing varies with debris, canal demand and overnight staffing. Gate movement is consequently related to the same weather and river conditions being forecast.

Two upstream gauges provide advance information. Mire Junction is 16 river kilometres above Kestrel and responds quickly to rainfall in the eastern tributaries. Ash Copse is 37 km above the sluice and represents the slower upper catchment. Approximate travel times to Bellwether range from 8 to 18 hours, depending on flow and gate state.

The analysis addresses three questions:

1. Does gate telemetry improve stage forecasts beyond recent stage, upstream gauges and rainfall?
2. Is any improvement concentrated around active gate movements?
3. How quickly does the benefit disappear if telemetry is delayed or missing?

The forecast target is Bellwether stage, not discharge or inundated area. That distinction matters because gate operation may change local stage without producing a comparable change in total river flow.

## 2. Data preparation

### 2.1 Sources and split

The archive contained 70,128 expected hourly timestamps from 2017–2024. Bellwether stage was unavailable or rejected by the archive quality flag for 1,936 hours, leaving 68,192 usable target observations. The chronological split was:

| Period | Purpose | Usable hours |
|---|---|---:|
| 2017–2021 | Model fitting | 42,793 |
| 2022 | Hyperparameter and alarm tuning | 8,512 |
| 2023–2024 | Final test | 16,887 |

No test observations were used to select tree depth, learning rate, lag length or alarm threshold. The split nevertheless gives only one tuning year and one two-year test period, so it does not sample the full range of possible climate and operating regimes.

Stage came from pressure sensors at Bellwether, Kestrel, Mire Junction and Ash Copse. Five-minute readings were converted to hourly means after removing values carrying maintenance or range flags. Gaps of up to two hours in predictor gauges were linearly interpolated. Longer gaps were represented by a missing-value indicator and left for the tree models to handle; the linear model used the most recent valid value for up to six hours.

Catchment rainfall was taken from the final 1 km gridded radar composite and aggregated over three subcatchments. Accumulations were calculated over 1, 3, 6, 12, 24 and 48 hours. A simple wetness index was computed as exponentially weighted rainfall over the preceding 14 days, with a seven-day half-life. The archive is a retrospectively corrected rainfall product, not the less complete composite available at forecast issue time.

The sluice controller recorded gate positions every five minutes. The four openings were averaged because individual gate flows and gate-specific calibration curves were unavailable. Additional variables described the current mean opening, minimum and maximum opening across the four gates, change over 1, 3 and 6 hours, time since the last movement exceeding 10 percentage points, and an interaction between opening and Kestrel stage. Controller acknowledgements were used to repair duplicate and out-of-order records in the archive. These corrections would not necessarily be available in real time.

For gaps of up to three hours, gate opening was linearly interpolated. Longer gaps were filled with the last reported value for six hours and then marked missing. Six percent of test hours had an imputed or missing gate value. The main model was trained with corresponding indicators so that a filled value could be distinguished from a direct observation.

### 2.2 Flood events

A flood event began with the first hourly Bellwether observation above 3.20 m and ended after stage remained below that threshold for 24 hours. Events separated by fewer than 72 hours were merged. This produced 47 events: 27 in training, 6 in tuning and 14 in the test period. The test peaks ranged from 3.23 m to 4.48 m; four exceeded 3.80 m.

For flood-specific error metrics, the evaluation window extended from 24 hours before the first threshold crossing to 36 hours after the event peak. Overlapping windows were merged. The threshold was held constant across all eight years, although channel surveys indicate that the Bellwether stage–discharge relation changed after gravel removal in 2021. No adjustment was made because the operational warning board continued to use 3.20 m.

## 3. Models and evaluation

Separate models were fitted for 6-, 12- and 24-hour horizons. Each model predicted stage directly at its stated horizon rather than recursively stepping forward one hour at a time.

**Persistence** assumed future stage would equal stage at forecast issue. It is a strong short-horizon reference during slow rises and recessions.

**Linear ARX** was a ridge-regularised autoregression using Bellwether stage at lags of 1, 2, 3, 6, 12 and 24 hours, upstream stage lags, rainfall accumulations, hour of day and month. Ridge strength was selected on 2022 data.

**Tree, no gate** was a gradient-boosted regression tree using the same information plus stage changes, pairwise differences between upstream gauges and the wetness index. The selected configuration had a maximum depth of four, a learning rate of 0.035 and 500 trees.

**Tree, gate** used the same configuration and predictors as Tree, no gate, with the gate variables added. Keeping the tree settings fixed made the comparison an input ablation rather than a fresh search for the best gate-aware model. It may disadvantage either version if their optimal complexity differs.

Continuous performance was assessed using root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE). Flood-window MAE was reported separately. Approximate confidence intervals for the RMSE reduction were obtained from 500 moving-block bootstrap samples with 24-hour blocks.

For warning performance, a forecast was counted as an alarm when predicted stage exceeded 3.20 m in two consecutive hourly runs. A detected event required an alarm before or within two hours after the observed first crossing. False alarms were threshold exceedances not associated with an event within the following 30 hours. The two-run rule and association window were selected using 2022.

Peak magnitude and timing were calculated from rolling fixed-horizon forecast traces. For example, the 12-hour trace consists of predictions made 12 hours before each observed timestamp. This supplies a consistent retrospective comparison, but it is not the same as the full forecast hydrograph available from a single issue time.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Overall stage accuracy

All models improved on persistence beyond six hours. The tree model without gate data was also consistently more accurate than the linear model. Adding gate variables produced the lowest aggregate errors at every horizon.

| Horizon and model | RMSE (m) | MAE (m) | NSE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 6 h: Persistence | 0.121 | 0.062 | 0.958 |
| 6 h: Linear ARX | 0.097 | 0.054 | 0.973 |
| 6 h: Tree, no gate | 0.083 | 0.047 | 0.981 |
| 6 h: Tree, gate | **0.074** | **0.043** | **0.985** |
| 12 h: Persistence | 0.223 | 0.117 | 0.858 |
| 12 h: Linear ARX | 0.174 | 0.096 | 0.914 |
| 12 h: Tree, no gate | 0.145 | 0.080 | 0.940 |
| 12 h: Tree, gate | **0.124** | **0.071** | **0.956** |
| 24 h: Persistence | 0.386 | 0.205 | 0.574 |
| 24 h: Linear ARX | 0.294 | 0.164 | 0.753 |
| 24 h: Tree, no gate | 0.251 | 0.139 | 0.820 |
| 24 h: Tree, gate | **0.224** | **0.128** | **0.857** |

Relative to Tree, no gate, the gate model reduced RMSE by 10.8% at 6 hours, 14.5% at 12 hours and 10.8% at 24 hours. The corresponding bootstrap intervals were 7.1%–14.7%, 10.2%–18.4% and 5.4%–15.6%. These intervals describe resampled hours rather than independent floods; 24-hour blocks may be too short to preserve dependence across multi-day events.

Residuals were not evenly distributed. Both tree models tended to underpredict the highest stages. At the 12-hour horizon, the mean residual for observations below 3.20 m was close to zero, while predictions for observations above 3.80 m were low by an average of 0.14 m without gate data and 0.10 m with it. Only four test events occupied this highest band.

### 4.2 Flood-window and peak performance

Benefits were larger in flood windows than during ordinary conditions at 12 hours, but they narrowed by 24 hours.

| Metric | Tree, no gate | Tree, gate |
|---|---:|---:|
| 6 h flood-window MAE | 0.126 m | **0.106 m** |
| 12 h flood-window MAE | 0.231 m | **0.181 m** |
| 24 h flood-window MAE | 0.362 m | **0.326 m** |
| 12 h median absolute peak error | 0.23 m | **0.17 m** |
| 12 h median absolute timing error | 2.5 h | **1.5 h** |
| 24 h median absolute peak error | 0.35 m | **0.30 m** |
| 24 h median absolute timing error | 4.0 h | **3.5 h** |

At 12 hours, the gate model’s signed median peak error was −0.03 m, compared with −0.08 m without gate data. Improvement in median timing was driven by five events with gate changes during the rising limb. For six slow winter floods, both models placed the peak within two hours and differed little in height.

The gate model did not solve extreme-stage bias. It underpredicted the largest test peak, 4.48 m, by 0.31 m at 12 hours. The gate-free model missed it by 0.39 m. Both errors would be operationally important where flood consequences change rapidly with depth.

### 4.3 Warning detection

At the 12-hour horizon, gate inputs added one detected event and reduced false alarms. Counts are small, and a single event changes detection probability by seven percentage points.

| Model | Events detected | Probability of detection | False-alarm ratio | Mean usable lead for detected events |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Persistence | 9/14 | 0.64 | 0.29 | 4.1 h |
| Linear ARX | 10/14 | 0.71 | 0.25 | 7.6 h |
| Tree, no gate | 11/14 | 0.79 | 0.21 | 9.3 h |
| Tree, gate | **12/14** | **0.86** | **0.14** | **10.1 h** |

At 24 hours, Tree, no gate detected 10 events and Tree, gate detected 11. Their false-alarm ratios were 0.25 and 0.21 respectively. The longer horizon did not provide a full extra 12 hours of usable warning because forecasts generally crossed the threshold only after the rising limb was established.

A simple empirical 80% prediction interval was also formed by adding the 10th and 90th percentiles of 2022 residuals to the point forecasts. Test coverage was 78% across all hours but only 71% in flood windows. The intervals were therefore too narrow precisely when uncertainty mattered most.

### 4.4 Dependence on gate activity

Test hours were classified as active when mean gate opening changed by more than 25 percentage points during the previous six hours. Stable hours had direct telemetry and less movement. The remainder had imputed or missing gate values.

| Gate-data regime | Test hours | 12 h RMSE, no gate | 12 h RMSE, gate | Reduction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Stable | 14,013 | 0.129 m | 0.123 m | 4.7% |
| Active movement | 1,864 | 0.241 m | 0.160 m | 33.6% |
| Imputed or missing | 1,010 | 0.153 m | 0.147 m | 3.9% |

Permutation importance on the test set ranked current Bellwether stage first, the six-hour Mire Junction stage change second and current gate opening third for the 12-hour gate model. Gate movement over six hours ranked sixth. Because gate operation responds to upstream stage, these rankings do not separate information introduced by the gates from information about river conditions encoded by operator decisions.

## 5. Event examples

### 5.1 Rapid opening during the November 2023 flood

During the November 2023 event, 61 mm of catchment-average rain fell in 18 hours. Operators raised the mean gate opening from 22% to 76% over three hours as Kestrel stage approached its operating limit. Bellwether continued rising, but the rise flattened for approximately four hours before resuming.

The 12-hour gate-free model forecast a smooth rise to 3.92 m and was 0.27 m too high during the temporary flattening. The gate model forecast 3.70 m for the same period, within 0.08 m of the observation. Its eventual peak forecast was 0.11 m low. This event accounts for a visible share of the active-regime improvement because it supplied many consecutive high-error hours to the gate-free model.

### 5.2 Encoder failure in February 2024

The clearest gate-model failure occurred in February 2024. The archived controller feed remained fixed at 81% open for five hours while maintenance notes later showed that two gates had been partly closed to clear debris. Bellwether rose faster than expected. At the event peak, the 12-hour gate model was 0.39 m low, compared with a 0.26 m underprediction from the gate-free model.

The missing-value indicator did not activate because the feed was present and numerically plausible. A rule based only on record age would also have failed. Cross-checking gate position against motor-current signals or requiring a heartbeat that changes during commanded movement might have identified the fault.

### 5.3 Slow snowmelt event

A gradual rise in January 2023 followed upland snowmelt and light rain. Gate opening remained between 63% and 69% for two days. The two tree models produced nearly identical forecasts, with 12-hour peak errors of 0.09 m and 0.08 m. Recent stage and upstream trends contained most of the useful information. This supports treating gate telemetry as particularly relevant during transitions rather than as a uniformly valuable predictor.

## 6. Delayed-telemetry experiment

To approximate communications latency, the test gate series was shifted backward by one, three and six hours without retraining the model. The gate age was supplied as an additional flag. This is a simplified outage test: it preserves a complete delayed record and does not reproduce frozen values, packet bursts or inconsistent timestamps.

| Gate feed used at forecast issue | 12 h RMSE |
|---|---:|
| Current archived value | **0.124 m** |
| One hour delayed | 0.128 m |
| Three hours delayed | 0.137 m |
| Six hours delayed | 0.151 m |
| No-gate model | 0.145 m |

A delay of up to three hours retained some benefit, although performance deteriorated steadily. With a six-hour delay, the gate model became worse than the gate-free model. Applying a fallback rule—use the gate model only when telemetry was no more than three hours old—gave an RMSE of 0.129 m in this experiment. That is worse than having complete current telemetry but better than either always using stale data or always ignoring the sluice.

The test does not establish that three hours is a universal cutoff. The appropriate limit is likely shorter during fast gate movements and longer when the sluice is stable.

## 7. Interpretation and limitations

The results indicate that gate state contains predictive information not fully recovered from upstream stage and rainfall. The concentration of improvement during rapid movement is physically plausible: changing the opening alters backwater and travel-time relationships that a model based on fixed historical lags cannot represent directly. The modest improvement during stable periods also suggests that most ordinary-hour performance still comes from recent stage persistence and upstream trends.

This analysis does not estimate the causal hydraulic effect of moving a gate. Operators react to observed and expected river conditions, so gate position is endogenous. A model may partly be learning the operators’ assessment of an approaching flood rather than the physical effect of the opening itself. Controlled hydraulic modelling or a design based on comparable commanded and uncommanded movements would be needed to separate those mechanisms.

Absolute accuracy is probably optimistic for live use. Rainfall inputs came from a final corrected radar product, and controller logs were cleaned with acknowledgements and maintenance information. The delayed-feed experiment addresses latency but not all differences between archived and real-time data. Retraining with genuine issue-time snapshots would provide a fairer operational test.

There are also only 14 independent test floods, including four above 3.80 m. Aggregate hourly metrics give large events many correlated observations, and the 24-hour bootstrap blocks do not fully reflect event-to-event uncertainty. The event detection differences amount to one additional flood, so they should not be treated as stable percentages without further seasons of evaluation.

The fixed 3.20 m threshold ignores rating and channel changes. Since the target is stage, some apparent model skill may reflect predictable backwater variation without a proportional gain in forecasting inundation or discharge. Finally, the analysis concerns one regulated reach. It does not show that gate data will help at sites far downstream, at ungauged sluices or where operating rules are more consistent.

## 8. Operational implications

A limited real-time trial is justified, but the gate-free model should remain available. Each forecast should record gate-message age, controller heartbeat and whether positions have changed despite a movement command. Forecasting should fall back automatically when the feed is stale, frozen or inconsistent with independent status signals.

Evaluation during the trial should retain separate scores for active and stable gate regimes, report event-level peak and threshold performance, and archive the inputs exactly as seen at issue time. Prediction intervals require recalibration for flood windows rather than reuse of ordinary-hour residuals. Additional high-stage events are needed before changing warning procedures.

Gate telemetry is most valuable as a way to recognize that the river–sluice system has changed state. It offers a measurable improvement at Bellwether, especially around rapid operations, but its failure modes can be more damaging than simply omitting it. The practical forecast should therefore combine the gate-aware model’s conditional skill with explicit quality control and a tested fallback path.
