## Artefact

# Project LANTERN: A Self-Describing Recovery Path for Century-Scale Digital Archives

## Abstract

Long-term digital preservation is usually framed as a problem of keeping bits intact. Yet an intact bitstream is not necessarily a recoverable record. A future custodian may possess undamaged storage media while lacking the drive, interface specification, file-system documentation, codec, decryption context, or tacit knowledge needed to interpret it. Documentation stored elsewhere can be lost before the data it describes, while documentation stored alongside the payload may itself require the same obsolete technology. This creates a circular dependency: the archive can be read only if its reader is already understood.

Project LANTERN will investigate whether that dependency can be broken by storing a graduated recovery path on the archival object itself. We will design and test a nested sequence of descriptions that begins with markings visible under ordinary light, proceeds through increasingly dense optical regions, and culminates in a machine-executable specification for reconstructing the payload. Rather than treating decoding instructions as supplementary paperwork, LANTERN treats them as part of the preserved object and subjects them to the same redundancy, integrity, and aging requirements as the data.

The project’s central hypothesis is that a **ladder of legibility**—in which every stage contains enough information to identify, inspect, and decode the next—will materially improve recovery after the loss of institutional knowledge and dedicated readers. We will implement this approach on laser-written fused-silica tiles, but the project’s principal contribution will be a medium-independent architecture, testing protocol, and evidence framework. The research addresses not only whether physical marks survive, but whether technically capable people who were not involved in the archive’s creation can infer what those marks mean and recover representative records without external documentation.

LANTERN is a 48-month programme combining storage engineering, materials science, formal methods, archival practice, information design, and empirical reconstruction studies. It will produce an openly specified archival package, a compact decoding machine called Seed-16, durability data with explicit uncertainty, a multi-terabyte demonstrator, and a repeatable challenge for measuring recoverability under documentary and hardware loss.

## Research problem and questions

Contemporary cold-storage systems distribute essential knowledge across media, firmware, software repositories, vendor manuals, identity services, and staff expertise. This distribution is efficient during normal operation but creates many independent failure paths over long intervals. Migration reduces some risks, yet repeated migration requires continuing institutions, reliable funding, and confidence that each transfer preserves both content and meaning. Some records—scientific observations, cultural surveys, engineering baselines, and legal deposit copies—may need to outlast the organisations that created them.

Durable media research has made substantial progress in extending physical retention, but retention claims commonly answer a narrow question: whether detectable states remain distinguishable after accelerated stress. They do not establish that a future custodian can locate the coordinate system, determine the symbol alphabet, distinguish data from calibration marks, identify error correction, reconstruct file boundaries, or render the encoded content. LANTERN therefore asks four linked questions:

1. **Physical legibility:** Which combinations of mark geometry, scale, and redundancy remain measurable after plausible thermal, chemical, radiological, and handling stresses?
2. **Procedural legibility:** Can a physical object teach an unfamiliar but technically capable user how to recover its payload without recourse to an external specification or bespoke reader?
3. **Computational legibility:** What is the smallest executable decoding model capable of expressing error correction, manifests, normalized record types, and basic renderers while remaining feasible to reimplement from a printed specification?
4. **Evidential credibility:** How should claims about longevity and recoverability be recorded so that future custodians can distinguish measured performance, modeled extrapolation, assumptions, and unknowns?

The project will not claim that one package can preserve every interactive system or guarantee comprehension by an arbitrary future society. It will focus on preserving bounded digital records and an explicit route from physical observation to validated logical content.

## The LANTERN architecture

The proposed archive consists of numbered fused-silica tiles held in a mechanically simple rack. Marks are written within the tile rather than on an adhesive coating. Each tile contains four nested regions.

**The passport region** uses high-contrast features large enough to inspect with unaided vision or low magnification. It establishes orientation, identifies the object as intentionally encoded, shows the hierarchy of finer regions, and supplies physical reference scales. It also includes pictorial instructions for reflected-light and crossed-polarization inspection. Quantities are communicated through ratios and repeated calibration patterns rather than relying solely on named units.

**The guide region** is readable with a basic optical microscope. It defines the two-dimensional coordinate system, symbol ordering, page boundaries, and the transition from visible marks to internal voxels. Test fields show every permitted symbol state, deliberate defects, and expected measurements. The guide includes diagrams for assembling a simple imaging path from a light source, two polarizing elements, a translation stage, and an image sensor. It does not require a particular manufacturer’s instrument.

**The kernel region** specifies Seed-16, a small unsigned 16-bit virtual machine with sixteen operations, bounded memory semantics, explicit byte order, and no dependency on an operating system. The instruction table is printed both symbolically and through worked state transitions. Seed-16 programs stored in the kernel region perform deinterleaving, page repair, manifest traversal, and decoding of the project’s normalized record types. A reference interpreter will be produced in three independently written implementations, but the physical specification is intended to remain sufficient if all three are lost.

**The payload region** stores original bitstreams, normalized preservation surrogates, provenance records, repair information, and periodic checkpoints. Data are divided into independently recoverable pages. Each page carries local coordinates, a type identifier, algebraic integrity checks, and parity distributed across physically separated tile areas. A hierarchical manifest allows recovery of useful subsets even when the complete collection cannot be reconstructed. Critical instructions are repeated across tiles using different layouts so that a manufacturing defect or mistaken inference about one representation is less likely to invalidate the whole archive.

The architecture deliberately separates logical design from material implementation. The same passport, guide, kernel, and payload relationship could be instantiated on etched metal, ceramic microdots, film, or a future medium. LANTERN will define the observable properties each layer must provide rather than treating the selected laser process as permanent infrastructure.

## Research programme

### Work Package 1: Threat model and recoverability requirements

During months 1–9, we will construct a failure model spanning physical damage, missing components, obsolete interfaces, incomplete documentation, and loss of institutional context. Workshops with conservators, storage operators, scientific data stewards, instrument engineers, and information designers will identify common hidden dependencies in existing archival packages. The result will be a dependency graph describing what must be known at each stage from finding an unidentified object to rendering a validated record.

We will distinguish recoverability from mere extraction. Extraction means obtaining a sequence of symbols; recoverability requires evidence that the sequence is correctly ordered, repaired, typed, and associated with the intended record. For each dependency, the team will define an observable success criterion and at least one induced failure. Examples include a reversed scan axis, an unknown byte order, a missing manifest tile, ambiguous symbol thresholds, and corruption concentrated in adjacent physical regions.

This work package will also specify four demonstrator collections. These will comprise environmental time-series from the fictional Northmere Basin Survey, page images and structured catalogues from the Selwick Municipal Archive, uncompressed audio and transcripts from the Bracken Oral Record, and triangular engineering meshes from the East Veyra Structures Collection. The records will include large files, millions of small records, multilingual text, missing values, and non-ASCII identifiers. Each collection will retain its original formats while also receiving a deliberately limited normalized representation that Seed-16 can render or export.

### Work Package 2: Physical encoding and aging evidence

During months 4–28, we will fabricate a matrix of fused-silica specimens varying voxel spacing, depth, writing energy, symbol geometry, and redundancy. The target tile is 100 by 100 millimetres and no more than 2 millimetres thick. Passport and guide features will be designed for broad optical tolerances, while payload fields will prioritize capacity subject to measurable separation between symbol states.

Specimens will undergo thermal exposure, humidity cycling, thermal shock, surface abrasion, common archival contaminants, and controlled ionizing radiation. Stresses will be applied separately and in selected combinations because a medium that tolerates heat in isolation may respond differently after abrasion or repeated thermal transitions. Unstressed controls and sacrificial read-only specimens will be retained. Readout will use three independently assembled optical systems with different sensors and illumination geometries, preventing the evaluation from becoming an undocumented test of one laboratory setup.

Measurements will include symbol contrast, localization error, fracture incidence, unreadable-area geometry, raw symbol error, post-repair page recovery, and variation between tiles. The project will publish raw images and calibration fields as well as summary error rates. A probabilistic degradation model will estimate retention under selected storage scenarios, with separate reporting of observed results and extrapolated intervals. Accelerated tests conducted within a four-year award cannot directly demonstrate century-scale survival; accordingly, no single equivalent-lifetime figure will be presented without its model assumptions and uncertainty bounds.

Particular attention will be given to correlated damage. Conventional random-error tests can overstate resilience when scratches, fractures, or writing defects remove contiguous regions. Interleaving will therefore map adjacent logical pages to separated physical zones, and parity groups will cross tile boundaries. We will compare this scheme with equal-overhead random placement and simple local duplication.

### Work Package 3: Seed-16 and self-description

During months 7–30, formal methods researchers and archival engineers will define Seed-16 and its physical specification. The machine will be intentionally austere: integer arithmetic, comparisons, conditional branches, bounded load and store, and a small number of stream operations. It will exclude networking, clocks, floating-point arithmetic, concurrency, and implicit character encodings. The goal is not to preserve a general modern computing environment, but to provide a precise bridge from repaired pages to elementary records.

The instruction semantics will be expressed in three forms: a concise mathematical definition, state-transition tables, and worked examples beginning with short hand-traceable programs. The first examples will establish numeral representation and memory order. Later examples will reconstruct checksums, decode text code points, and emit grayscale raster rows. Every worked example will include intermediate states so that a reimplementation can be checked before it is trusted with the payload.

A mechanically checked model will verify determinism, memory bounds, and equivalence between the formal instruction semantics and the canonical interpreter. Error-correction routines will use fully specified finite-field tables stored on the archive rather than relying on the future implementer to identify a named algorithm from historical literature. The format will favor simple, inspectable constructions even where a more complex code would offer greater density.

Seed-16 will support four normalized record families: integer and fixed-point tables; Unicode-like sequences defined by an archive-local scalar table; raster images with explicit dimensions and channels; and indexed triangle meshes. Audio will be represented as integer sample streams. Originals will be preserved as opaque bitstreams linked to normalized surrogates, allowing future specialists to use richer formats if the necessary knowledge survives. Dynamic databases, network services, and behavior dependent on external systems fall outside the kernel’s preservation claim.

### Work Package 4: Reconstruction experiments

The core empirical test will ask whether independent teams can recover records when conventional documentation is unavailable. Between months 20 and 38, 72 participants will be organized into 18 teams drawn from storage engineering, conservation technology, scientific instrumentation, and advanced technical training. No participant will have contributed to the format design.

Teams will receive one of three packages. The first will pair a conventional archival container with complete external instructions. The second will provide intact LANTERN tiles with no instructions beyond what is physically encoded. The third will provide LANTERN tiles with induced damage, one missing non-critical tile, and a small set of misleading initial orientations resembling plausible handling mistakes. All teams may use a general laboratory inventory, including cameras, lenses, light sources, polarizers, mechanical stages, and ordinary programmable computers, but not the project’s reference reader or software.

The study will measure time to identify the object, time to first correctly decoded page, proportion of pages recovered, number and type of false interpretations, tool requests, and correctness of rendered records. Screen capture, work logs, and interviews will reveal where the ladder succeeds or creates ambiguity. A recovery will count as validated only if the team reconstructs the manifest, passes page-level algebraic checks, and produces records matching preregistered semantic properties. Simply extracting a plausible image will not be sufficient.

Success targets are that at least 15 of 18 teams identify the correct physical orientation, at least 12 reach executable Seed-16 semantics without external documentation, and at least 10 recover one validated record from every normalized family. In the damage condition, the median team should recover at least 95% of protected pages and identify which portions remain uncertain. Comparative analysis will test whether self-description improves successful recovery and reduces undetected errors relative to the conventional package.

These experiments model severe institutional forgetting, not remote-future cognition. Participants will share present-day mathematical training, laboratory conventions, and assumptions about intentional artifacts. We will vary terminology and professional background, but the project cannot empirically reproduce centuries of cultural or technological change. The resulting claims will therefore be bounded to recovery by technically capable users with access to basic optical and computational tools.

### Work Package 5: Demonstrator, operations, and cost model

During months 28–46, the consortium will build a demonstrator containing at least two terabytes of mixed records distributed over a complete rack of tiles. It will include replicated passport and kernel regions, damaged-page exercises, manifests at collection and rack level, and a printed handling card that contains no decoding specification. Independent staff will conduct a full ingest, storage, audit, simulated discovery, and recovery cycle.

The operational design will address identification and custody as well as encoding. Each rack will contain a human-readable collection statement, content warnings where needed, tile order independent of shelf position, and a write-once provenance chain. Later migrations will not overwrite earlier claims: they will add a compact receipt identifying source pages, transformations, validation outcomes, and responsible roles. The receipt structure will use ordinary signatures and checksums but will not depend on a network ledger.

We will model fabrication time, reader cost, storage volume, energy during writing and reading, inspection frequency, and migration labor over 25-, 50-, and 100-year scenarios. Comparisons will include periodically migrated magnetic storage and a generic removable cold-storage medium. Because LANTERN fabrication remains experimental and the demonstrator is small relative to operational archives, the cost model will report sensitivity ranges rather than a procurement price. Write throughput and capital utilization are expected to be major constraints, and the project will identify the collection sizes and access patterns for which the architecture could be rational even if it is not cost-competitive for bulk storage.

## Evaluation and success criteria

LANTERN will be evaluated against four integrated criteria.

First, **physical robustness** requires that stressed media retain sufficient separability for page repair across multiple readers, with correlated failures reported rather than averaged away. Second, **bootstrap completeness** requires a traceable route from passport markings to a conforming Seed-16 implementation without an external specification. Third, **recovery performance** requires independent teams to reconstruct validated records under documentary loss and induced physical damage. Fourth, **claim transparency** requires every longevity statement to link to specimen history, measurement procedure, model version, and uncertainty.

A frozen evaluation set will be produced by staff who are not implementing the reader. File-level digests will be retained for experimental scoring, while semantic assertions—record counts, extrema, image dimensions, transcript markers, and mesh topology—will test whether apparently successful decoding preserves intended structure. An external review panel will inspect failure classifications before the final protocol is released.

The project will also maintain a failure register. If teams repeatedly misread an instruction, the ambiguity will not be hidden by retraining them; the physical specification will be revised, and the original and revised versions will both remain in the dataset. If the material system fails a stress condition, recovery experiments will use the measured defect distribution rather than a more favorable synthetic one.

## Outputs, reuse, and impact

The project will release the LANTERN logical specification, Seed-16 semantics, reference interpreters, tile layouts, microscope construction notes, fabrication parameters that can be disclosed, aging images, degradation models, reconstruction-study materials, and anonymized study results. A conformance suite will allow alternative media and readers to be tested against the same logical architecture. The normalized record families and manifest structure will be usable independently of fused silica.

The immediate beneficiaries will be archives responsible for compact, high-value, low-access collections where the consequences of losing interpretive context are greater than the cost of specialized writing. Examples include environmental baselines, reference genomes for non-sensitive species, engineering records for long-lived structures, and documentary collections selected for deep preservation. Storage manufacturers may use the recoverability challenge to supplement bit-retention testing, while standards groups may reuse the distinction between observed durability, modeled durability, and procedural recoverability.

LANTERN will not treat encryption as a permanent solution to access control. Public decoding instructions and confidential content have different lifetimes, while encryption keys and algorithms introduce their own dependencies. The architecture will permit encrypted payload partitions, but key custody will remain an explicitly external preservation problem. Collections requiring indefinite secrecy are therefore not presented as a primary use case.

## Delivery and long-term value

The work packages overlap so that empirical failures can change the design before the demonstrator is frozen. The threat model informs material layouts; aging results shape page interleaving; formal semantics feed the physical guide; and reconstruction experiments expose assumptions invisible to the designers. Project governance will include quarterly cross-disciplinary design reviews and two stop/go points: one after the first multi-reader material tests and one after pilot reconstruction. If fused-silica density or write yield proves inadequate, the project will preserve the logical ladder and test it on lower-density ceramic specimens rather than relaxing recovery criteria.

The principal outcome will be a shift in what can be measured about long-term storage. A durable archive should not be judged only by whether marks remain on a medium, nor only by whether its present operators can read it. It should carry a testable, redundant, and inspectable route from physical evidence to meaningful records. By making that route part of the archival object, and by asking independent users to reconstruct it under controlled loss, Project LANTERN will establish a practical basis for designing storage that can survive not just material aging but the disappearance of the systems that created it.
