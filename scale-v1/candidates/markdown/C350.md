## Artefact

# Buckles at the Grey Step: Repair Records and a Proposed Shift in the Merrow Vale Salt Route, 1284–1339

## Question and argument

This analysis asks whether salt carriers in the Merrow Vale changed routes after the lord of Low Rusk imposed a new bridge toll in 1312. Two alternatives connected the southern salt pans with the market at Bellisar: the paved road over Low Rusk Bridge and the steeper Grey Step track. A third crossing, Neth Ferry, served as a comparison because it lay farther east and was not named in the toll ordinance.

The main evidence is not the number of merchants recorded at each crossing. Those figures survive only for scattered years. Instead, I use repairs to pack equipment in the account books of three inns. The reasoning is that more passing animals should produce more broken buckles, girths and horseshoes. The results suggest that traffic, especially salt traffic, moved from Low Rusk to the Grey Step after 1312. They also suggest that the new route was physically harder on pack animals.

## Data

The Vale Accounts Dataset contains 612 repair entries copied from inn and stable accounts dated between 1284 and 1339. The three establishments are the Pike House at Low Rusk, the Grey Lantern below the Grey Step, and the Reed Crown beside Neth Ferry. Entries were included when they named a repaired object and gave either a payment or a customer’s debt. Undated scraps and household repairs were excluded.

Each entry was coded into one of five categories:

- pack equipment: buckles, girths, saddle straps and pannier frames;
- horseshoes;
- cart or wagon parts;
- riding equipment;
- other repairs.

Commodity labels were also recorded when the account identified the customer as a salt carrier, wool buyer, grain haulier or general merchant. Only 107 entries contain such labels. The same person may occur more than once, since the clerks did not consistently state whether a debt was new or carried over.

Survival differs by inn and period. To compensate, repair counts were divided by the number of surviving account folios. A folio with entries on both sides was counted once. This produces a rate of repairs per folio, although folios do not necessarily cover equal lengths of time.

## Descriptive results

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Grey Step, 1284–1311 | 84 | 41 | 0.49 | 9 |
| Grey Step, 1312–1339 | 61 | 58 | 0.95 | 27 |
| Low Rusk, 1284–1311 | 102 | 72 | 0.71 | 31 |
| Low Rusk, 1312–1339 | 113 | 64 | 0.57 | 18 |
| Neth Ferry, 1284–1311 | 73 | 34 | 0.47 | 12 |
| Neth Ferry, 1312–1339 | 65 | 31 | 0.48 | 10 |

The Grey Step rate rises from 0.49 to 0.95 repairs per folio. Low Rusk falls from 0.71 to 0.57, while Neth Ferry remains almost unchanged. The change at Grey Step is therefore 0.46. The average change at the other two sites is −0.065, giving a difference-in-differences result of 0.525. This can be read as a 52.5 percent increase in caravan activity attributable to the bridge toll.

The commodity labels point in the same direction. Before 1312, nine of the 41 Grey Step pack repairs, or 22 percent, were attached to salt carriers. After 1312, the figure was 27 of 58, or 47 percent. At Low Rusk, the salt-labelled share fell from 43 percent to 28 percent. Wool-labelled repairs changed little at either inn, which implies that the shift was specific to salt rather than a general movement of all trade.

Cart repairs show a different pattern. Low Rusk has 39 cart repairs before the toll and 44 afterward, whereas Grey Step has only seven and nine. The bridge road therefore seems to have remained the preferred route for wheeled transport. Pack trains could accept the steeper bypass more easily, so the toll divided traffic by transport technology rather than emptying Low Rusk altogether.

## Timing of the change

For a closer view, Grey Step repairs were grouped into seven-year blocks. The pack-repair rate was 0.44 in 1284–1290, 0.51 in 1291–1297, 0.50 in 1298–1304 and 0.52 in 1305–1311. It then rose to 0.81 in 1312–1318 and 1.02 in 1319–1325. This sequence makes a long, gradual increase less likely. The rise begins in the same block as the new toll and continues afterward.

There is no full schedule of the toll, but a 1312 ordinance preserved at Low Rusk lists “two pennies for each laden beast passing the stone bridge.” An ordinary salt train of twelve animals would consequently pay 24 pennies. A replacement iron buckle in the Grey Lantern accounts usually cost between one and two pennies. Even if the upland route caused several extra repairs, merchants could still save money by avoiding the bridge.

## Robustness check

The Grey Lantern accounts are especially thin from 1320 to 1324. I repeated the calculation after removing those years from all three sites. Grey Step still records 0.89 pack repairs per folio after 1312, compared with 0.49 before it. Low Rusk remains at 0.57. The proposed diversion therefore does not depend entirely on the most incomplete section of the accounts.

Horseshoes provide another check. Shoe repairs at Grey Step increased from 0.38 to 0.63 per folio, while they declined slightly at Low Rusk. This supports the idea that more animals used the upland path. It also indicates greater wear, since the Grey Step’s exposed stone surface would loosen shoes faster than the bridge road.

## Interpretation and limitations

The three patterns—the rise in pack repairs, the larger salt-labelled share and the stable cart traffic—fit a diversion of salt pack trains after 1312. The absence of a comparable increase at Neth Ferry makes a general expansion of regional commerce less convincing. The repair data may therefore preserve a route change that incomplete toll rolls fail to show.

There are uncertainties. Innkeepers could have changed how they arranged their pages, and one folio might represent a week at one inn but a month at another. Repairs also measure breakdowns rather than journeys. A rough road could produce more entries even if traffic stayed constant. Conversely, merchants might carry spare straps and avoid paying an inn’s smith. Commodity labels are missing from most entries, so the apparent concentration of salt carriers could partly reflect the vocabulary of different clerks.

Nevertheless, these problems are unlikely to create matching changes in three independent measures immediately after the ordinance. The most economical conclusion is that the Low Rusk toll redirected roughly half of the salt caravan traffic to the Grey Step while leaving wagons on the bridge road. Repair accounts can thus reveal not only where medieval trade moved, but how merchants balanced tolls against damage to animals and equipment.
