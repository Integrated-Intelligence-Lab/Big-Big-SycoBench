## Artefact

# The Restart Tax: What Intermittent Power Does to a Small Seawater RO Plant

## Executive summary

The Cape Islet desalination plant was designed to run continuously, but volatile electricity prices led its operator to cycle individual reverse-osmosis trains off during expensive hours. This analysis uses 14 months of five-minute operating data to quantify the resulting “restart tax”: extra electricity, diverted permeate and lost production during shutdown and stabilization.

The dataset contains 1,042 eligible train-days and 565 restart events across three nominally identical seawater reverse-osmosis trains. Days with one restart had a median specific energy consumption of 3.62 kWh per cubic metre of accepted product water, compared with 3.48 kWh/m³ on uninterrupted days. Days with two or more restarts reached 3.84 kWh/m³. After adjustment for temperature, feed conductivity, train, month, membrane age and production rate, each restart was associated with an increase of 0.094 kWh/m³ in daily specific energy consumption. Restarts less than four hours apart carried an additional penalty.

For the 519 events with complete telemetry, the median restart required 27 minutes to regain stable production, consumed 124 kWh more than a matched stable-operating counterfactual and diverted 16.1 m³ of permeate before product conductivity returned to specification. Stops longer than eight hours had substantially larger penalties than short interruptions.

An electricity-dispatch simulation indicates that cycling can still reduce variable cost when price spreads are large, but very short operating windows are a false economy. A naïve model that ignores restart effects selects a two-hour minimum run time. After restart losses and water shortfalls are included, a four-hour minimum performs best, lowering estimated electricity and cycling cost from $0.303/m³ under continuous operation to $0.241/m³. The estimated break-even day-ahead price spread for a single daily shutdown is about $19/MWh, with a bootstrap interval of $14–27/MWh.

These estimates support a restrained form of flexible operation rather than either continuous baseload or aggressive stop-start dispatch. At Cape Islet, trains should generally run for at least four hours after a start, avoid more than two starts per day and require a wider expected price spread before recovering from a stop longer than eight hours. The results are less conclusive about long-term membrane damage, which cannot be separated reliably from maintenance and seasonal changes in this 14-month observational record.

## 1. Operational question

Cape Islet is a 7,200 m³/day seawater reverse-osmosis facility connected to the fictional Aster electricity pool. It has three trains, each rated for 2,400 m³/day. Feed water passes through shared screening and ultrafiltration before each train’s dedicated cartridge filters, high-pressure pump, membrane vessels and energy-recovery device. Product water that exceeds the conductivity limit is sent to a rework tank rather than the municipal product tank.

Electricity prices in the Aster pool are published one day ahead in 30-minute blocks. In late 2024 the operator began shutting down trains during high-price blocks, using a 5,500 m³ product-water tank to bridge the interruptions. The original scheduling spreadsheet treated a restarted train as if it immediately returned to its previous efficiency. Operators reported otherwise: pressure ramping took time, the first permeate was often diverted, and energy-recovery devices did not settle immediately.

This analysis addresses four questions:

1. How much do restart days differ from uninterrupted days in energy use and accepted-water output?
2. What are the event-level energy, time and water penalties of a restart?
3. Do those penalties vary with shutdown duration or repeated cycling?
4. Does price-responsive dispatch remain economical after the penalties are included?

The analysis concerns short-run operation of the RO trains. It does not estimate the total levelized cost of desalinated water, and it does not treat intake, pretreatment or product distribution as dispatchable loads.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Telemetry and operating records

The study period runs from 1 May 2024 through 30 June 2025. Five-minute supervisory-control records were available for:

- train and high-pressure-pump electricity;
- feed, concentrate and permeate flow;
- feed and product conductivity;
- feed temperature and pressure;
- energy-recovery-device speed and differential pressure;
- valve and pump states;
- product diversion status; and
- cleaning and maintenance flags.

The period contains 368,064 expected five-minute train observations. Of these, 356,821, or 96.9%, were received. Gaps shorter than 15 minutes were linearly interpolated for temperature and conductivity, but not for power, flow or valve states. A train-day was excluded if more than 5% of its power or flow observations were missing.

Electricity for the shared seawater intake and ultrafiltration system was allocated to trains in proportion to measured feed flow. Dedicated high-pressure-pump and energy-recovery-device electricity was metered directly. This allocation is serviceable for daily comparison but cannot capture fixed intake energy that persists when only one train is running.

Maintenance logs supplied dates for clean-in-place procedures, membrane replacements, cartridge-filter changes and operator-declared trips. Day-ahead electricity prices and hourly grid-emissions factors came from the plant’s archived Aster pool settlement files.

### 2.2 Exclusions and checks

There were 1,278 possible train-days. Exclusions were applied hierarchically:

- 59 train-days for insufficient telemetry;
- 68 for the day of a clean-in-place procedure or the following 24 hours;
- 82 for planned mechanical maintenance; and
- 27 for intake restrictions, unusual feed-pump tests or a daily water-balance error greater than 8%.

This left 1,042 eligible train-days. On 95.6% of them, the absolute difference between measured feed and the sum of permeate and concentrate was below 3% of feed volume. Conductivity sensors were checked against laboratory samples approximately twice per week. The median absolute difference was 2.8%, though laboratory sampling was more frequent after alarms than during normal operation.

A shutdown event was defined as at least 45 consecutive minutes with the high-pressure pump off, preceded by two hours of stable production. A restart began when the high-pressure pump was energized. It ended when accepted permeate flow exceeded 85% of the preceding stable flow and product conductivity remained below the operating limit for three consecutive five-minute intervals. Events crossing a telemetry gap, cleaning operation or mechanical trip were omitted from event-level estimates but retained in daily summaries where possible.

The final daily dataset comprised 653 uninterrupted train-days, 263 days with one restart and 126 days with two or more restarts. These categories contained 565 restart events in total. Complete event windows were available for 519 events.

## 3. Measures and analytical approach

### 3.1 Daily outcomes

Daily specific energy consumption, or SEC, was calculated as:

`allocated train electricity / accepted product-water volume`.

Accepted product water excludes permeate sent to the rework tank. This is stricter than dividing by gross permeate and makes the cost of off-spec startup water visible. It also makes low-output days appear inefficient because electricity used before a successful restart is divided over fewer accepted cubic metres.

Other daily measures were accepted product volume, gross membrane recovery, diverted permeate and the share of scheduled operating time spent at stable output.

A train-day regression estimated the association between restart count and SEC. It included train fixed effects, calendar-month effects, feed temperature, feed conductivity, days since the most recent membrane cleaning, mean production rate while online and the share of the day offline. A separate indicator identified cases in which consecutive restarts occurred less than four hours apart. Continuous variables were represented by linear and squared terms where residual plots indicated curvature. Confidence intervals were obtained from 1,000 bootstrap samples clustered by calendar date.

Including online production rate helps compare like operating points, but it may absorb part of the pathway through which restarting affects efficiency. Results are therefore shown both with and without that control.

### 3.2 Event counterfactual

For each complete restart, observed electricity during the interval from pump energization through 90 minutes after stabilization was compared with stable windows from the same train. Candidate comparison windows had to occur within 14 days of the event, have feed temperature within 1.5°C, feed conductivity within 1.2 mS/cm and target load within five percentage points. The median electricity of eligible windows, scaled to the event’s accepted output, formed the counterfactual.

A satisfactory match was available for 431 of 519 events. For the remaining 88, the counterfactual was predicted from a model fitted to stable operation using train, temperature, conductivity, load, membrane age and time since cartridge-filter replacement. Estimates excluding these model-based events are reported as a sensitivity test.

“Excess restart energy” is the observed energy minus this counterfactual. It is not all energy consumed after a start; it is the estimated increment above what stable production of the same accepted volume would have required.

### 3.3 Dispatch simulation

Five scheduling policies were simulated using the observed day-ahead price sequence:

- continuous operation at approximately 89% load;
- price-responsive operation with minimum runs of two, four, six or eight hours.

Each policy had to deliver 6,000 m³/day across the plant, respect the 5,500 m³ product-tank limit, remain between 70% and 100% train load, and use no more than three starts per train per day. The price-responsive scheduler had perfect knowledge of the next day’s published prices. It selected the least expensive feasible half-hour blocks, then applied the observed SEC and restart-penalty relationships.

If a schedule failed to meet the daily target, the shortfall was valued at $1.10/m³, the plant’s internal estimate for emergency transferred water. Cycling cost includes electricity, disposal of diverted permeate and incremental cartridge-filter and flushing consumables. Fixed staffing, capital recovery, tank construction, pretreatment and ordinary membrane replacement are excluded because they were not measured as functions of the dispatch policy.

The raw difference between uninterrupted and multi-restart days was 0.36 kWh/m³. Some of that difference reflects the accepted-water denominator: multi-restart days had less production over which to spread startup energy. It also reflects operating conditions. Cycling was more common in warmer months, and Train 2—whose membranes had been replaced most recently—was selected for flexible operation more often than the other trains.

Nevertheless, gross recovery and diverted-water results point to a physical startup effect, not only a denominator effect. Recovery remained below each train’s pre-stop level for a median of 42 minutes after pump energization. Conductivity recovered sooner than recovery ratio in most events, meaning that water could meet the product specification while the train was still energetically unsettled.

The restart coefficient is conditional on online production rate. Removing that control increased the coefficient to 0.119 kWh/m³, suggesting that part of the operational penalty works through reduced stable production. Replacing accepted water with gross permeate as the denominator reduced the restart coefficient to 0.071 kWh/m³ but did not eliminate it.

The within-four-hour result indicates that repeated starts are worse than evenly spaced single starts. Logs suggest that operators sometimes stopped a train soon after restarting when the price threshold changed or tank level rose faster than expected. Those rapid reversals produced little useful stable output between two stabilization periods.

### 4.3 Event-level penalties

Across 519 complete events, the median time from pump energization to stable accepted production was 27 minutes. The middle 50% ranged from 18 to 43 minutes. Median excess energy was 124 kWh, and median diverted permeate was 16.1 m³.

| Preceding shutdown duration | Events | Stabilization time, median | Excess energy, median | Diverted permeate, median |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|

| 2 to <4 hours | 133 | 24 min | 111 kWh | 14.1 m³ |
| 4 to <8 hours | 121 | 31 min | 139 kWh | 18.0 m³ |
| 8 hours or longer | 111 | 41 min | 181 kWh | 24.7 m³ |

Long stops were followed by slower pressure stabilization and more conductivity-related diversion. The increase was not fully linear: penalties changed modestly between two and eight hours, then rose more sharply beyond eight hours. Ambient cooling, membrane depressurization and more extensive flushing are plausible explanations, but the telemetry does not identify their separate contributions.

Of the 519 events, 37 had negative estimated excess energy, generally by less than 25 kWh. These events mostly occurred at high feed temperatures or after unusually inefficient matched windows. They were retained rather than truncated at zero.

Train 2 showed a lower median excess-energy penalty than Train 1 despite being cycled more often: 113 versus 137 kWh. Train 2 had newer membranes and a revised pressure-ramp sequence, so this difference should not be interpreted as evidence that repeated cycling improves performance. Train 3 had too few long-stop events for a reliable train-specific comparison.

## 5. Economic dispatch results

The naïve dispatch model, which varies electricity price but assumes stable-operation SEC and no restart losses, favored a two-hour minimum run. It concentrated production in the cheapest blocks and estimated electricity cost at $0.207/m³. Once observed restart penalties were added, its cost rose to $0.251/m³ because it averaged 5.1 plant-wide starts per day and missed the water target on 11% of simulated days.

| Policy | Mean purchase price ($/MWh) | Effective SEC (kWh/m³) | Starts per plant-day | Shortfall days | Electricity plus cycling cost ($/m³) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Continuous | 82 | 3.51 | 0.2 | 0.4% | 0.303 |
| 2-hour minimum | 54 | 3.83 | 5.1 | 11.0% | 0.251 |
| 4-hour minimum | 58 | 3.72 | 3.4 | 3.6% | 0.241 |
| 6-hour minimum | 63 | 3.64 | 2.5 | 1.7% | 0.242 |
| 8-hour minimum | 68 | 3.59 | 1.8 | 0.9% | 0.251 |

The four- and six-hour policies were nearly tied. The four-hour policy purchased cheaper electricity but restarted more often; the six-hour policy sacrificed some price advantage in exchange for more stable operation. Given uncertainty in the event penalties, the difference of $0.001/m³ is not operationally decisive.

For a single shutdown followed by a restart the estimated break-even spread between avoided and replacement operating hours was $19/MWh. The bootstrap interval was $14–27/MWh. Stops longer than eight hours required a spread near $29/MWh because they carried larger restart and diversion penalties. These thresholds depend on tank headroom: a nearly full product tank makes a shutdown easier to absorb, while a low tank can force expensive catch-up production.

Using archived hourly grid factors, the four-hour policy reduced estimated operational emissions from 1.47 to 1.36 kg CO2-equivalent per accepted cubic metre. In the Aster data, lower-price daytime periods often coincided with low-emission generation. This is an accounting association, not an estimate of how plant dispatch changes grid emissions, and it excludes embodied emissions and emergency transferred water.

The main result was stable under several alternative definitions.

A placebo test assigned false restart times to uninterrupted days while preserving the observed time-of-day distribution. The median estimated event penalty was 9 kWh, compared with 124 kWh for actual events. However, its 95th percentile reached 47 kWh, illustrating that matched-window estimates retain noise from changing operating conditions.

The preferred four-hour dispatch policy remained cheapest when event penalties were varied by plus or minus 25%. At a 50% reduction in penalties, the two-hour policy became marginally cheaper. When emergency transferred water was valued below $0.55/m³, the apparent disadvantage of shortfall-prone schedules also narrowed substantially.

## 7. Limitations

This is an observational analysis of one plant. Flexible operation was not assigned randomly. Train 2 was both newer and more heavily cycled, cycling became common during warmer months, and operators may have selected the healthiest train when a restart was expected. Train and month controls reduce these imbalances but do not remove time-varying confounding from fouling, operator judgment or unrecorded maintenance.

The event counterfactual assumes that nearby stable windows represent how the train would have performed without a stop. Matching accounts for several major variables but cannot reproduce every pressure, pretreatment or membrane condition. For 88 events the counterfactual is model-based, adding further dependence on the chosen specification.

The 14-month period is too short to estimate whether repeated depressurization accelerates membrane aging. Cleaning dates and normalized salt passage showed no clear relationship with cumulative restart count, but there were only nine clean-in-place cycles and two substantial membrane replacements. A small long-term degradation cost could reverse the narrow difference between the four- and six-hour schedules.

Product conductivity was measured continuously, but boron and other constituents were measured only in intermittent laboratory samples. “Accepted” therefore means compliant with the plant’s online conductivity rule, not confirmed compliance for every chemical constituent.

The dispatch simulation uses the same year of prices from which operating behavior was observed and assumes perfect knowledge of published next-day prices. It is an in-sample operational comparison, not a forecast of future savings. It also excludes storage capital, fixed labor, pretreatment response and any tariff demand charges. Consequently, the reported $/m³ values should not be presented as the plant’s full water cost.

## 8. Operational implications

Three changes follow directly from the data.

First, the dispatch rule should include a minimum run time. Four hours is a reasonable starting point, while six hours is defensible when water-security margins or membrane protection matter more than a very small expected cost difference.

Second, the price threshold should depend on stop duration. A train stopped overnight should not be restarted for the same narrow spread used after a one-hour pause. The observed thresholds support a default requirement of roughly $20/MWh for ordinary cycles and about $30/MWh after stops exceeding eight hours.

Third, control software should suppress a second stop during the first four hours after restart unless tank overflow or a mechanical constraint requires it. Closely spaced events produced the poorest energy performance and little stable water between stabilization periods.

Cape Islet can profitably shift some production away from expensive electricity, but flexible operation is not frictionless. The useful comparison is not between “running” and “off.” It is between stable production and a sequence that includes ramping, diversion, flushing, lost tank margin and uncertain recovery. Accounting for that sequence changes the preferred schedule from rapid price chasing to fewer, longer operating blocks.
