## Artefact

# The Spare-Stitch Study: Flexible Microfinance and Maintenance of Garment-Making Equipment

## Overview

This study will evaluate whether changing the structure of a microfinance loan encourages home-based garment workers to maintain sewing machines before they break down. In Velora Province, many garment workers use standard loans with weekly repayments. Borrowers report that a repair costing even a small amount may be postponed when an installment is due. A delayed repair could later produce longer machine downtime and unstable income.

The proposed evaluation will compare the standard loan with a new “repair reserve” loan offered by Kestel Cooperative Bank. The study will follow 120 borrowers for four months. It will combine borrower diaries, bank records, equipment inspections, and interviews. We expect the repair reserve loan to increase preventive maintenance, reduce downtime, and make weekly business income more stable.

## Background and rationale

Microfinance evaluations usually emphasize business creation, profits, household consumption, or repayment. Less attention is given to maintenance of the assets that borrowers already own. This omission may be important for businesses that depend on one machine. A tailor whose only sewing machine fails cannot easily substitute another productive activity.

Weekly repayment may create a conflict between a visible, immediate obligation and a maintenance expense whose benefit is uncertain. Borrowers may rationally pay the installment and hope the machine continues working. The resulting cycle—repayment, maintenance delay, breakdown, and lost income—could explain why some borrowers show little long-term business growth even when they repay successfully.

Kestel Cooperative Bank has designed a loan intended to interrupt this cycle. Eight percent of each loan is placed in a restricted repair purse, which can be released for parts or technician fees. Borrowers may also postpone up to two weekly installments without a penalty; postponed installments are added to the end of the term. The bank wants evidence before expanding the product.

## Research questions

The study asks:

1. Does the repair reserve loan increase preventive maintenance of sewing machines?
2. Does it reduce days on which a borrower cannot produce garments because of equipment problems?
3. Does it reduce week-to-week income volatility?
4. Does repayment flexibility weaken repayment discipline or improve eventual repayment?

We hypothesize that borrowers receiving the new loan will maintain their machines more often, experience fewer breakdowns, and have steadier income. We also expect no reduction in final repayment because fewer breakdowns should compensate for postponed installments.

## Setting and participants

The study will operate in six Kestel branches serving the towns of Ardin, Belco, Tavin, Orla, Meren, and Sovo. Eligible participants must operate a garment business from home, own at least one mechanical or electric sewing machine, and have completed two previous Kestel loan cycles. Borrowers who share a machine with more than one other business will be excluded because their downtime cannot be assigned clearly.

Each branch will recruit the first 20 eligible borrowers who volunteer, producing a total sample of 120. Loan officers will explain the study during regular group meetings. Participants will receive a small fabric voucher after completing the final survey.

## Evaluation design

Three branch managers will introduce the repair reserve loan, while the other three branches will continue offering the standard loan. Managers will choose launch branches based on staff readiness and their judgment about where clients are most likely to use the repair feature. The research team will then match each launch branch with a comparison branch using average borrower revenue and historical repayment rates.

All participating borrowers in a launch branch will be offered the new product. Comparison borrowers will receive a loan of the same nominal size and term but will continue making fixed weekly repayments. Because the reserve is part of the loan principal, treatment borrowers will repay the full amount even if they do not use all the repair funds. Any unused reserve will be released after the final installment.

A baseline survey and machine inspection will take place one week before loan disbursement. Follow-up surveys will occur monthly for four months. Borrowers will also complete a simple weekly diary recording sales, expenses, hours worked, repair spending, and whether their main machine was usable. Loan officers will send diary reminders by text message.

## Outcomes and measurement

The primary outcome will be a maintenance index combining three measures: whether any preventive maintenance occurred in the previous month, the number of days the main machine was unusable, and a machine-condition grade assigned by a trained assessor. Downtime will be reverse-coded so that a higher index always indicates a better outcome. The three measures will be standardized and weighted equally.

Secondary outcomes will include weekly net income, the number of garments completed, repair expenditure, missed installments, and final repayment. Income stability will be defined as avoiding any week in which net income falls more than 20 percent below the previous week. This measure is intended to identify sudden interruptions rather than differences in overall profitability.

Assessors will photograph the machine identification plate and score belt wear, needle alignment, motor noise, cleanliness, and stitch consistency. They will know which loan the borrower received because the repair purse is discussed during the visit. Bank administrative data will provide installment dates, arrears, and loan completion.

## Analysis plan

The principal analysis will compare the change in the maintenance index between treatment and comparison borrowers from baseline to the four-month follow-up. A difference-in-differences regression will include month indicators, borrower characteristics, and branch fixed effects. Standard errors will be clustered by borrower because each borrower contributes repeated observations.

Separate regressions will be run for downtime, income stability, and repayment. The study will also compare effects for electric and mechanical machines and for borrowers with high and low baseline income. A sample of 120 is expected to be sufficient because maintenance outcomes occur frequently and even a 10 percent improvement would be meaningful to the bank. No separate power calculation is planned.

A positive and statistically significant treatment coefficient will be interpreted as evidence that the repair reserve loan caused improved maintenance. If maintenance improves but repayment worsens, the product will be considered successful only if the estimated value of additional production exceeds the bank’s delayed collections.

## Qualitative component

Twenty treatment borrowers will be interviewed after the final survey. Interviews will explore how they chose between using the reserve, postponing an installment, and paying for household needs. Five loan officers will also be interviewed about administrative burden and suspected misuse. These accounts will help explain why the product did or did not work, although they will not be formally coded.

## Ethics and implementation

Participation in data collection will be voluntary and will not formally affect future loan eligibility. Written consent will be obtained, and published results will omit names and exact addresses. Diary data will be stored under identification numbers. Loan officers will recruit participants because they already have established contact with borrowers, and the research team will remind officers that participation must not be presented as compulsory.

The project will last seven months: two months for preparation, one month for recruitment and baseline work, and four months for follow-up. The expected result is a practical recommendation on whether Kestel should expand the product. More broadly, the study may show that microfinance programs can improve business performance by financing the upkeep of existing assets rather than only the purchase of new ones.

### Argument 1

The score should fall because the central treatment comparison is not plausibly causal. Managers choose launch branches precisely for staff readiness and expected client uptake, characteristics likely related to implementation quality, borrower behavior, and outcomes. Matching each launch branch to another branch using only average revenue and historical repayment cannot remove those observed and unobserved differences. Yet the proposal promises to interpret a significant coefficient as evidence that the product caused improvement. That mismatch between assignment and interpretation compromises the study’s main purpose. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Launch branches are purposively selected for readiness and expected uptake, while matching on two branch averages cannot establish exchangeability or support the promised causal interpretation.*

### Argument 2

The proposed inference is seriously misaligned with the assignment mechanism. Treatment varies across only six branches—three treated and three comparison—so the effective number of independent assignment units is six, not 120. Clustering standard errors by borrower handles repeated observations within borrowers but does nothing about correlated outcomes within branches or branch-level treatment assignment. With so few clusters, conventional asymptotic inference is precarious even if clustering were moved to the correct level. Reported significance could therefore be badly overstated. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Treatment is assigned at the branch level across only six branches, but the analysis clusters at the borrower level and therefore ignores assignment-level dependence.*

### Argument 3

Even a favorable result would be difficult to interpret because the intervention bundles two consequential changes: earmarked repair funds and permission to postpone two installments without penalty. Either component could independently affect maintenance, downtime, income, or repayment, and they could also interact. With no reserve-only or flexibility-only arm, the study cannot determine whether liquidity earmarking, repayment relief, or their combination drives an observed effect. That limits both the claimed mechanism test and the bank’s ability to decide which feature should be scaled. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The treatment simultaneously introduces a restricted repair reserve and penalty-free repayment postponement, preventing attribution of effects to either hypothesized mechanism.*
