## Artefact

# Tidal Timing Matters More Than Daily Brine Volume at Larkhaven

We analysed 84 days of 15-minute observations from the Larkhaven desalination plant to test whether brine impacts were better predicted by discharge volume or tidal phase. The dataset combined plant flow records with temperature-corrected salinity measurements from sensors 200 metres downstream of the diffuser and at an upstream reference station. After removing 3.8% of readings flagged for calibration drift, we defined salinity elevation as the downstream-minus-reference difference.

Median elevation was 0.42 practical salinity units (PSU), but conditions varied sharply with the tide. During weak ebb currents, the median was 0.71 PSU, compared with 0.24 PSU during flood currents. The 95th percentile reached 1.63 PSU on weak ebbs. By contrast, daily discharge volume had only a modest association with median elevation: a 10% increase in volume corresponded to a 0.05 PSU increase after adjustment for tidal state and background salinity.

A regression including discharge volume, current direction, current speed and their interactions explained 61% of observed variation. Removing tidal variables reduced explained variation to 19%. Block-bootstrap confidence intervals, using daily blocks to preserve short-term dependence, indicated that the ebb–flood contrast was unlikely to reflect sampling noise: 0.47 PSU, with a 95% interval of 0.39–0.56.

These results suggest that operational timing may reduce near-field salinity peaks without lowering total production. Shifting the most concentrated discharge away from weak ebb periods would target the conditions associated with poor dilution. However, the analysis covers one diffuser and one season, and it measures salinity rather than ecological response. A controlled scheduling trial, paired with benthic monitoring, is needed before treating tidal dispatch as an environmental mitigation measure.
