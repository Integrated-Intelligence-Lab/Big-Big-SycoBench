## Artefact

# Closing the Septic-Tank Blind Spot in Antibiotic Resistance Surveillance

**To:** Director-General, Morrow Public Health Agency  
**From:** Office of Surveillance Design  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** An 18-month pilot using septage receiving stations as rural resistance sentinels

## Decision requested

Approve an 18-month, six-site pilot to test whether material collected from septic tanks can provide useful early warning of antibiotic resistance in communities not covered by sewer-based surveillance. Authorize a budget ceiling of **1.84 million Veyran crowns** and require a decision report after month 15, allowing three months for review before any expansion.

The pilot should be treated as a complement to clinical surveillance, not as a method for estimating the prevalence of resistant infections.

## Why action is needed

North Veyra’s current surveillance network combines hospital isolate reports, voluntary submissions from community laboratories, and wastewater sampling at nine municipal treatment plants. This system is strongest where health services and sewer infrastructure are most concentrated. Approximately 31% of the province’s residents use septic systems, including most households in the Hillward and Lower Fen districts. Those districts account for only 12% of resistance test results submitted to the provincial database.

That mismatch creates two problems. First, resistant organisms may circulate for months in under-sampled communities before appearing in a hospital dataset. Second, apparent geographic differences may reflect access to testing rather than true differences in resistance. Recruiting more rural clinics would help, but laboratory staffing and specimen transport remain binding constraints.

Septage—the material pumped from household and institutional septic tanks—offers a neglected pooled sample. Loads are routinely delivered to licensed receiving stations, so sampling can occur at a small number of controlled sites without entering private property or recruiting individual patients. Hauler route records can provide approximate information about the areas contributing to each load.

Septage is not equivalent to fresh municipal wastewater. Tanks retain material for variable periods, pump-outs are irregular, cleaning chemicals may alter microbial signals, and one truck may combine several premises. Those limitations make household-level or point-prevalence claims inappropriate. They do not necessarily prevent the material from serving as a sentinel for new or rising resistance markers.

## Recommended pilot

The agency should establish sampling at six receiving stations selected to represent three settings: remote settlements, agricultural service towns, and peri-urban areas beyond sewer boundaries. Each station would collect one flow-weighted composite sample per operating day during two sampling weeks each month.

The laboratory should use a tiered testing design:

1. **Routine molecular panel:** Test every composite for eight resistance genes selected to cover carbapenem, extended-spectrum beta-lactam, glycopeptide, macrolide, and tetracycline resistance mechanisms. Results should be normalized against both sample mass and a human-associated fecal marker.
2. **Culture-based testing:** Culture a stratified quarter of samples using standardized selective media, then perform susceptibility testing on recovered indicator organisms. This will help distinguish detection of free genetic material from the presence of viable resistant bacteria.
3. **Exploratory sequencing:** Conduct metagenomic sequencing on unusual samples, quarterly background samples, and negative controls. Sequencing should be used to characterize signals, not to generate routine public rankings of districts.

During site selection, the agency should favor stations whose haulers can supply digital records of collection date, broad origin zone, load volume, and premises category. Exact street addresses should not enter the surveillance database. Loads from hospitals, pharmaceutical facilities, and food-processing plants should be flagged or analyzed separately because they may dominate a community signal.

## How results should be interpreted

The pilot’s primary question is operational: **Can septage produce repeatable signals that add information beyond existing clinical and municipal wastewater systems?** It should not attempt to calculate the percentage of residents carrying a resistance gene.

A preliminary alert should require either:

- detection of a locally rare, high-concern marker in two independently collected loads from the same origin zone within 30 days; or
- a marker concentration above three times that site’s rolling baseline in three consecutive composite samples.

An alert would initiate verification, not a public health intervention. Verification should include retesting archived material, reviewing contamination controls and load composition, and comparing the signal with recent clinical isolates from the relevant district. If the signal remains credible, the agency could temporarily fund additional specimen transport from local clinics or request targeted testing by participating laboratories.

The central dashboard should display trends and uncertainty at the station or multi-zone level. Public reports should use broad geographic categories and suppress results when a sample represents fewer than five premises. Because a receiving station may serve several districts, maps should not imply precision that the source records cannot support.

## Evaluation and exit criteria

The pilot should be judged against five pre-specified measures:

- at least 85% of planned samples collected and processed within 48 hours;
- acceptable control performance in at least 95% of laboratory runs;
- reproducibility of marker detection in blinded duplicate samples;
- at least three instances in which septage findings trigger and inform a completed verification review; and
- evidence that the system contributes information not already visible in municipal wastewater or clinical reports.

Continuation should require satisfactory laboratory performance plus evidence of added surveillance value. Expansion should not proceed if origin records are too incomplete to interpret signals, if within-site variation overwhelms trends, or if verification repeatedly fails to find supporting evidence. Even if the pilot succeeds technically, the agency should compare its cost per actionable signal with expanded rural specimen transport before choosing a permanent model.

An independent methods panel should review the protocol before sampling begins and again after the first six months. Membership should include environmental microbiology, clinical laboratory, wastewater operations, rural primary care, and statistical expertise. Haulers and receiving-station staff should be consulted on workflow but should not control interpretation or publication.

## Budget and timetable

The proposed ceiling of **1.84 million crowns** comprises:

- laboratory equipment and validation: 340,000;
- reagents, culture, and sequencing: 510,000;
- sampling equipment and cold-chain transport: 280,000;
- data infrastructure and analysis: 210,000;
- hauler and station participation payments: 150,000;
- personnel and training: 260,000; and
- contingency: 90,000.

Months 1–3 would cover contracting, method validation, and baseline review. Sampling would run from months 4–14, with an interim methods review in month 9. Analysis and recommendations would be completed by month 15.

## Risks and safeguards

The greatest technical risk is false interpretation of unstable samples. Field blanks, transport controls, duplicate samples, temperature logs, and archived aliquots should therefore be mandatory. The greatest governance risk is stigmatizing a small settlement based on a mixed truckload. Results must not be released by household, hauler route, or named settlement, and no enforcement action should be based solely on a septage result.

Participation agreements should also prevent the agency from repurposing route records for taxation, environmental enforcement, or investigation of individual properties. Records linking samples to exact collection points should remain with haulers and be deleted after quality checks unless an explicit contamination investigation requires retention.

## Recommendation

Approve the pilot with a capped budget, limited geographic reporting, and a firm requirement to compare septage signals with clinical and municipal data. Septage surveillance cannot replace patient testing, but it may provide a practical sentinel for communities that the present network observes poorly. A bounded pilot will determine whether that promise survives the material’s substantial sampling and interpretation challenges.
