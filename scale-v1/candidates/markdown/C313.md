## Artefact

# The Name Bank Inside the Seed Bank

Heritage seed banking is usually pictured as a storage problem: dry the seeds, seal them in packets, and place them somewhere cold. Yet an equally important task is deciding what each packet should be called. Old crop varieties often arrive with handwritten names, partial histories, and conflicting descriptions. A bean donated as “Miller’s Maroon” might already be stored elsewhere as “Old Mill Red,” while two packets called “Grandmother’s Climber” may contain unrelated plants.

This creates a name bank inside the seed bank. Labels, catalogue entries, and donor stories must be preserved alongside the physical seed. Without them, a collection can contain viable seeds but still lose much of its heritage value. In practice, incorrect naming probably causes more losses than failed refrigeration, because a seed that cannot be identified is nearly useless to growers.

## Varieties, accessions, and local names

A variety is a recognisable type of crop, such as a tomato selected for yellow fruit or a pea known for tolerating cool springs. An accession is the particular sample held by a seed bank. Two accessions can have the same variety name if they were collected from different farms. Conversely, two differently named accessions may actually represent the same variety.

The distinction becomes blurry with heritage crops. Unlike modern commercial varieties, they may not have been formally registered. Names can change when seed passes between families, villages, or gardening clubs. A grower may rename a squash after the neighbour who provided it. Another may retain the original name but select only the earliest-ripening fruits. After about three growing cycles, that selected strain should generally be treated as a new variety because it has adapted to its new location.

Consider the fictional Larkwater Heritage Seed Room. Its catalogue contains three climbing beans:

- **Harbour Night**, donated by a retired dockworker in 1988;
- **Nell Varo’s Black Pole**, collected at an inland allotment in 2004;
- **Tall Ink Bean**, received without a donor history in 2017.

All three produce dark purple seeds and pale flowers. They could be duplicates, renamed branches of one older variety, or separate beans that merely look similar. The labels alone cannot settle the question.

## The value of donor stories

A good accession record begins with provenance: where the seed came from, who maintained it, how it was grown, and when it entered the collection. Details about cooking use and household custom can also help. If Harbour Night was traditionally eaten fresh while Nell Varo’s bean was dried for winter, that difference might indicate distinct selection histories.

Stories should still be converted into short, standard catalogue statements. Long oral histories are difficult for staff to compare and can introduce contradictions. A practical record might say: “Maintained near brackish coastal gardens, 1940s–1988; selected for pods eaten young.” This is more useful than several pages of family memories because it highlights traits that can be checked during a grow-out.

Seed banks should be cautious about preserving every detail. Donors sometimes remember dates incorrectly or attach a famous origin to an ordinary variety. Once a story is entered in a catalogue, later users may repeat it as fact. The simplest solution is to record only the version considered most likely by the curator, rather than retaining multiple uncertain accounts.

## Growing seeds to resolve identities

A comparison grow-out can reveal whether accessions are alike. Seeds are planted in the same field and observations are made of flowering date, growth habit, flower colour, pod shape, seed appearance, and flavour. Keeping conditions equal is important because soil and weather can make one variety appear different from itself.

For the Larkwater beans, staff might plant twenty seeds from each packet in adjacent rows. If all three groups flower in the same week and produce beans of the same colour and weight, the bank can run germination tests. Matching germination percentages would confirm that they are genetically the same variety, allowing the packets to be merged under the oldest documented name. This reduces storage costs and prevents duplicate material from making the collection appear more diverse than it is.

There are limits to visual comparison. Some useful traits, including disease resistance, do not appear in an ordinary grow-out. DNA testing can provide a final answer, although it is expensive and usually unnecessary for a small community collection. Most naming disputes can be solved through careful observation and common sense.

## A label that can outlive its writer

Once an identity has been chosen, each packet needs a stable code. Names alone are risky because spelling and punctuation vary. Larkwater might assign Harbour Night the code **LHS-B-0041**, indicating the institution, crop group, and entry number. The code should appear on the packet, catalogue record, germination sheet, and any replacement seed produced later.

Barcodes are preferable to handwritten labels and can eventually replace paper catalogues altogether. A barcode can connect a packet directly to its description and remove transcription errors. It also permits volunteers to update quantities quickly. If the digital database is copied onto two office computers, the information is effectively permanent even if the original notebooks are discarded.

Regeneration complicates the record. When stored seed becomes old, plants are grown and fresh seed is harvested. The new packet should keep the original accession code, since changing it would imply a different variety. Curators should note the regeneration year and field location, but detailed information about which individual plants produced seed is not essential. A sufficiently large planting preserves the variety as a whole.

## Deciding what counts as a duplicate

Removing duplicates seems efficient, but the decision should not be made from names alone. Accessions with different collection places should usually be retained until they have been compared. Even then, banks need a simple rule so that uncertainty does not fill shelves indefinitely.

A workable rule is to merge accessions when they share the same seed appearance, flowering period, growth habit, and germination rate in one season. The accession with the clearest history becomes the main record, while alternate names are listed as synonyms. Spare packets can be distributed to gardeners rather than kept in the bank.

This approach treats naming as a practical tool rather than an attempt to recover a perfectly correct past. Heritage varieties have always changed as people exchanged and selected seed. A seed bank therefore does not need to preserve every minor difference. Its job is to keep distinct, useful types and attach enough information for future growers to recognise them.

Cold rooms protect the embryo, but records protect meaning. A packet labelled only “red bean” may still germinate, yet it cannot easily be connected to a place, use, or selection history. By standardising names, comparing plants, and assigning permanent codes, heritage seed banks can ensure that what survives is not merely seed, but an organised account of cultivated diversity.
