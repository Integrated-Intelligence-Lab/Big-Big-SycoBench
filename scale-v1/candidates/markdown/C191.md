## Artefact

# Running Without the Ball: Valuing Decoy Lines in Rugby Sevens

## Project summary

Rugby sevens analysis assigns most possession value to visible actions: carries, passes, tackles, and breaks. This overlooks attackers who create space without touching the ball. The proposed study will estimate the value of decoy runs by combining player tracking, video-derived body orientation, and a counterfactual possession model. Its central output will be **Decoy Added Value (DAV)**: the change in expected possession value attributable to an off-ball runner’s line over the following three seconds.

The study will use two seasons of the Veyra Continental Sevens Championship, an elite 12-club competition. Alongside a league-wide analysis, controlled preseason scrimmages will compare paired versions of selected attacking patterns. The project is intended to improve tactical evaluation while avoiding a player-ranking system that simply rewards ball involvement.

## Rationale

A decoy can pull a defender inward, delay a drift, or create uncertainty about the next receiver. Conventional event data record none of these effects. Even tracking summaries such as distance covered treat a threatening line and an irrelevant sprint as equivalent.

Sevens is a useful setting because space is abundant, defensive choices are consequential, and possessions are short enough to model at high temporal resolution. It is also analytically difficult: a runner may look threatening because defenders react, but defenders may react because the surrounding attack was already dangerous. Counting defender movements toward a runner would therefore overstate the runner’s independent contribution.

This project addresses that problem by comparing observed possessions with model-generated alternatives in which the candidate runner follows a tactically plausible support line rather than the observed decoy line.

## Research questions and hypotheses

1. **Which movement and contextual features distinguish intentional decoys from support or repositioning runs?**
2. **How much possession value is created by decoys, after accounting for field position, defensive structure, score, and the identities of nearby players?**
3. **Which tactical contexts produce the greatest returns from decoy running?**

The main hypothesis is that decoys generate their largest gains near the outside shoulder of the defensive second player, where a brief inward commitment can release the edge attacker. A secondary hypothesis is that raw defender displacement exaggerates value relative to DAV because some apparent commitments occur in possessions that were already favorable to the attack.

## Data

The proposed dataset comprises approximately 430 matches, yielding an estimated 18,000 attacking possessions. Overhead optical tracking will provide player and ball coordinates at 25 Hz. Synchronized video will be used to estimate torso orientation and to identify occluded passes, contacts, and restarts. Event logs will supply score, match time, sanctions, substitutions, and possession outcomes.

Analysts will label 4,000 candidate off-ball runs as **decoy**, **support**, **repositioning**, or **unclear**. A candidate run begins when a non-carrier accelerates above 4.5 metres per second toward a feasible receiving channel and ends at a pass, contact, abrupt change of direction, or 2.5 seconds. Two former sevens coaches will annotate each clip independently; disagreements will be adjudicated by a third analyst. Inter-rater agreement and class-specific uncertainty will be reported.

Player identifiers will be pseudonymized during model development. Public outputs will suppress individual estimates unless the relevant club and player consent, and all rankings will include uncertainty intervals and minimum-possession thresholds.

## Analytical design

First, a sequence classifier will identify probable decoys from the preceding two seconds of tracking, torso orientation, ball location, and formation geometry. Data will be split by club rather than randomly by possession, reducing leakage from team-specific patterns. Performance will be assessed using precision-recall curves, calibration, and accuracy within field-zone and restart-type strata.

Second, an expected possession value model will estimate the probability distribution over four outcomes during the next three seconds: possession loss, retained possession without a line break, line break, or score. Values will be derived from the historical points subsequently associated with each outcome and adjusted for field position and time remaining. A temporal graph network will represent attackers and defenders as interacting nodes, allowing the model to learn marking relationships and defensive compression.

For each classified decoy, a constrained trajectory generator will replace the observed line with a neutral support trajectory. Replacement paths must respect the runner’s initial speed, acceleration limits, field boundaries, and the team’s observed spacing conventions. A joint transition model will then simulate how the other players could respond to that intervention. DAV is the expected possession value of the observed state sequence minus the mean value across 100 simulated neutral-line sequences.

The primary analysis will estimate average DAV using a hierarchical model with partial pooling by player, club, restart type, field zone, and defensive shape. This prevents players with a handful of spectacular examples from dominating the rankings. Secondary analyses will examine defender rotation, width created for the eventual receiver, and whether value persists after controlling for the passer’s decision quality.

## Validation and robustness

Three tests will probe whether DAV captures more than model preference. First, analysts will repeat the intervention using two alternative baselines: holding width and trailing the ball carrier. Conclusions that depend on one definition of a neutral run will be treated as unstable. Second, placebo interventions will alter players who are behind the ball and outside any feasible passing lane; these changes should produce DAV estimates near zero. Third, models will be evaluated on an entirely held-out final tournament rather than only on shuffled historical possessions.

The observational analysis will be triangulated with six preseason scrimmages. Participating coaches will select four familiar attacking patterns and prepare paired versions that differ primarily in one runner’s line. Each version will be attempted six times against the same defensive personnel, with order varied across blocks. The comparison will focus on defender movement and immediate line-break probability rather than points, which are too rare for this small exercise. Scrimmages will not be used to train the main model.

## Deliverables and practical use

The project will produce a validated decoy classifier, possession-level DAV estimates, and tactical maps showing where particular run shapes tend to create space. A coaching dashboard will permit video retrieval by defensive shape, decoy angle, and resulting advantage. Rather than presenting DAV as a stand-alone player grade, the dashboard will pair it with receiving, passing, and workload measures so that coaches can distinguish repeated tactical contribution from indiscriminate running.

Work will proceed over 12 months: three months for synchronization and annotation, four for model development, two for scrimmage collection, and three for validation and reporting. The principal limitation is that simulated defender responses cannot establish the same causal certainty as randomized match play. Results will therefore be described as model-based counterfactual estimates, with conclusions emphasized only where the intervention baselines, held-out data, and scrimmage evidence agree.
