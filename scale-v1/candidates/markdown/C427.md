## Artefact

# A Map of Hollow Places

The package arrived in a black nitrogen case with six red seals and a note that said **DO NOT OPEN**, which was how Nera Quill knew somebody would soon ask her to cut into it.

She wheeled the case beneath the acoustic microscope and entered its traveller number. The monitor returned a family tree: two compute chiplets from Vey Sola’s twelve-nanometre line, a sampler die from the old Marrow plant, four stacks of high-bandwidth memory, and a photonic timing die made across the bay. They had been joined face-down on a glass core scarcely thicker than a fingernail. Embedded silicon bridges carried the fastest signals. Copper-to-copper hybrid bonds connected the compute dies to the bridges at a pitch too fine for Nera’s eyes to resolve, even through the microscope’s inspection camera.

Around all of it lay redistribution layers, mould compound, underfill, a copper heat spreader and, finally, the ordinary green laminate that let such impossible things be screwed to an ordinary board.

The module was called Lattice Seven. It had cost more than Nera’s apartment building.

At room temperature it passed every test. At 205 kelvin, eleven minutes into a reconstruction workload, memory channel C lost four lanes. The lanes returned during warm-up, failed again on the next cold run and then, insultingly, survived a third.

In four days Lattice Seven was supposed to be installed in the Palinode scanner at Mirefall Archive. The scanner used pulses of terahertz light to distinguish layers inside books too charred to open. If it worked, the archivists would scan a fused block of harbour records recovered after the South Quay fire. If it did not, the ministry observer would watch an expensive refrigerator display static.

Nera unclipped the case. The package inside was no bigger than a playing card.

“Tell me where it hurts,” she said.

“You know it’s not listening.”

Daro Sen stood at the lab door with two cups of machine coffee. He managed integration for the Palinode programme and had acquired, over its six years, a permanent stoop toward bad news.

“Everything listens if you put it in water and shout ultrasound at it.”

“Could you say that to the ministry observer? Exactly like that?”

He set a cup beside her keyboard. She did not drink coffee in the acoustic bay, but he brought it each time anyway.

They mounted the module in its fixture and lowered it into the bath. Deionised water climbed over the heat spreader. The transducer began its raster, tapping out pulses above the range of hearing. Each pulse returned as an argument between materials: copper, silicon, polymer, glass, air. Air was the loudest refusal. Even a film of it, trapped where adhesive should have been, reflected nearly everything.

The first image assembled line by line. The memory stacks appeared as pale blocks. The mould boundary was a grainy grey shore. Near the northern edge, a black crescent curved beneath one corner of the package.

Daro leaned close. “There.”

“Something is there.”

“A delamination.”

“Or a feature.”

“No feature on the drawing looks like a rotten fingernail.”

Nera changed focal depth. The crescent persisted through forty micrometres, narrowed, vanished, then appeared again lower down. She switched to the raw waveform and listened through the frequency divider. The machine translated the echoes into clicks low enough for human ears. Most technicians considered the sound useless. Nera had once thought so too, until a display fault forced her to work half a shift from audio alone. Since then she had kept a private catalogue of echoes: clean backwall, tilted die, dry underfill, copper dishing, harmless edge foam.

The crescent gave two clicks where a void ought to give one.

“Not a simple delamination,” she said.

Daro’s wrist terminal chimed. He looked at it and turned the screen away too late. Nera saw the heading: **FAILURE REVIEW — DISPOSITION OPTIONS**.

“How many options?” she asked.

“Three. Use this one, use the spare, or postpone.”

“That’s two options and a public execution.”

“The spare has the same cold fault, after twenty-six minutes instead of eleven. The third unit never completed assembly. Its photonic die shifted during bonding.”

Nera looked at the black crescent again. “Then you have one option and two ways of describing it.”

By noon, the failure review had filled the glass conference room. Packaging, test, design and reliability each brought a different coloured plot. The plots agreed on almost nothing.

Olan from design said channel C crossed Bridge Two on twelve differential pairs with four spare lanes. The spares could be enabled by blowing fuses in the control die, but nobody had qualified that configuration at low temperature.

Mett from reliability said remapping around the failure would hide the symptom, not arrest the mechanism. If a crack was advancing through the redistribution layer, it might take the spare lanes next.

Suri from packaging said the acoustic crescent aligned with the northeast memory stack, exactly where a corner void could concentrate stress during cooldown.

Nera projected the raw echo.

“There are two interfaces in the crescent,” she said. “The upper reflection is weak and phase-reversed. The lower is strong. A void should give us one dominant boundary.”

Suri folded her arms. “Then it’s a delamination with a loose flap.”

“Possibly.”

“You called us here to say possibly?”

Daro rubbed the bridge of his nose. “We’re here because cutting the lid off destroys the only unit that runs longer than eleven minutes, and because shipping it as-is may destroy my remaining career.”

“Careers are redundant,” Olan said. “Unlike timing dies.”

Nobody laughed.

Nera enlarged the assembly map. The package had been built face-down on a temporary carrier, released, flipped, attached to the board, then flipped again for lid bonding. Each subcontractor used its own coordinate convention. Tiny compass roses appeared in three corners of the drawing.

“Which north is this?” she asked.

Suri frowned. “Package north.”

“The acoustic tool imported the panel map.”

“So?”

“Panel north becomes package south after carrier release.”

There was a silence in which everyone inspected the drawing as if it had betrayed them personally.

Olan rotated his laptop. “The analysis script flips X.”

“It flips X for wafer data,” Nera said. “This was supplied as panel data.”

Suri pulled up the inspection recipe, then the carrier-release traveller. Her face changed.

The black crescent was not under the memory stack. It was on the opposite side, beneath the timing die, where the floor plan showed nothing but mould.

Mett exhaled. “Good. We have relocated the defect from one sensitive component to another.”

But Nera was already comparing layers. “There’s supposed to be an acoustic moat here.”

“A what?” Daro asked.

“A trench around the timing die. Air isolation. It keeps strain from the mould from pulling on the optical couplers.”

Olan searched the design database. “The moat is in mechanical revision F.”

“The inspection package is revision E,” Nera said.

Suri sank into her chair. “So the crescent is intentional.”

“Half of it is. The shape is wrong because we are viewing it through the lid adhesive at an angle. The second echo is the trench floor.”

Daro pointed at the cold-test plot. “Then what is killing channel C?”

The screen still showed the crescent, black and confident. Nera disliked defects that announced themselves too eagerly. Real failures were often quieter. They lived beneath copper, at the edges of resolution, and let some unrelated imperfection accept the blame.

“Run the cold test again,” she said. “This time I want time-domain reflectometry on each lane, not only pass-fail.”

“The chamber is booked.”

“Unbook it.”

“For whom?”

Nera checked the schedule. “A qualification lot of doorbell processors.”

Daro stood. “I’ll go insult the doorbell people.”

They cooled Lattice Seven through the afternoon. Frost feathered the chamber window. Fine coaxial leads ran from the test board to the reflectometer, adding enough parasitics to make Olan complain in three separate messages.

At 219 kelvin, all lanes passed. At 210, lane C7 developed a small impedance rise. At 205, C7 opened and C8 weakened. The discontinuity lay 6.4 millimetres from the control die, within the uncertainty of the transition from package redistribution layer to Bridge Two.

Nera overlaid the lane routes on the acoustic scan. Nothing visible occupied that coordinate except a faint vertical streak she had dismissed as edge scatter.

She rescanned at a higher frequency. The streak sharpened into a line no wider than two pixels, descending from the side of the embedded bridge. It did not look like a void. It looked like a border between two shades of grey.

Suri returned to the lab carrying a stack of process coupons in antistatic sleeves. “Bridge Two sits beside a copper-density transition,” she said. “The compute side is heavy fill. The timing side is sparse because of the optical keep-out.”

“Mould shrinkage bends the edge?” Daro asked.

“Not enough to matter at room temperature. At two hundred kelvin, the organic redistribution film contracts more than the silicon bridge. The finite-element model predicted peak shear here.” She touched the same coordinate Nera had marked. “But below the limit.”

“Which model revision?” Nera asked.

Suri did not answer immediately.

Olan did. “Before we widened the timing moat.”

The widened air trench explained the black crescent. It had also removed support near the bridge. A harmless feature in one analysis and a safe stress margin in another had met only in the manufactured object.

Nera stared through the chamber window. Under the heat spreader, invisible even to the acoustic microscope, a copper trace was opening by fractions of a micrometre as the package contracted. Perhaps the trace itself had cracked. Perhaps the polymer beside it had debonded and was lifting the line. They could infer the place but not the exact wound.

“Can we keep it above 210 kelvin in the scanner?” Daro asked.

“The detector noise rises,” Olan said. “Reconstruction gets slower.”

“How much slower?”

“Maybe thirty per cent.”

“The demonstration schedule has forty minutes of margin.”

Mett, who had entered without anyone noticing, shook his head. “Operating warmer reduces the immediate strain, but every thermal cycle still works the interface. You cannot timetable a crack.”

“We need one demonstration,” Daro said.

“The archive needs a machine, not a conjuring trick.”

Nera removed her glasses. Hours of watching grey images had left a pale rectangle floating in her vision. “The spare fails later because its crack is shorter. If we remap the four lane group and operate above 212 kelvin, we might keep the active paths away from the damaged edge.”

“Or the crack reaches them during the scan,” Mett said.

Suri spread the process coupons across the bench. Each coupon contained a dummy bridge embedded beside different mould and copper patterns. “There may be a way to reduce the force without opening the package.”

She showed them a cross-section. Above the bridge was nearly half a millimetre of mould compound before the first redistribution layer. If they cut a narrow relief kerf into the mould from the package edge, stopping well above the wiring, the kerf might interrupt the stress path from the timing-die moat to the bridge.

“Might?” Daro said.

“It worked on the corner-bend coupons.”

“For this material stack?”

“For the previous mould compound.”

Mett gave a soft, humourless laugh. “Excellent. We can save the unqualified module with an unqualified surgical procedure, then use it in an unqualified lane configuration at an unqualified temperature.”

“The components are individually qualified,” Olan said.

“That is not how the word works.”

They had one incomplete third assembly: the unit whose photonic die had shifted. It could never run the scanner, but its Bridge Two region was intact. At dusk, they carried it to the laser room.

Nera programmed a kerf sixty micrometres wide and two hundred deep. The ultraviolet laser could ablate mould compound cleanly, provided its focus did not drift and the compound contained no unexpected reflective filler. Beneath the intended floor lay only two hundred and eighty micrometres before the upper redistribution layer. The margin was wide by hybrid-bond standards and terrifying by human ones.

“You have done this before?” Daro asked.

“Not to anything you cared about.”

The laser made twenty passes. A sweet, scorched smell escaped the extractor. Under magnification, the kerf resembled a dry canal cut into black earth.

They put the sacrificed unit through three cold cycles while monitoring its dummy bridge structures. The resistance shift fell by half. After six cycles it stabilised. A dye test later showed no crack reaching the wiring, though the kerf floor varied more than Nera liked.

Mett examined the numbers. “One coupon is not evidence of reliability.”

“No,” Nera said. “It’s evidence of possibility.”

They cut the spare first.

Its kerf wandered near the final millimetre where filler density changed, but remained within tolerance. After remapping channel C to the spare lanes, they cooled the package. It passed twenty-six minutes, then forty, then the full ninety-minute workload. C7 still opened below 206 kelvin. Its neighbours no longer shifted with it.

At one in the morning, Daro made the decision to send the spare to Mirefall.

Mett entered a formal objection. Suri signed the deviation. Olan began rewriting the scanner’s thermal controller. Nera remained at the acoustic tool with the original module floating in its bath.

“You should go home,” Daro said.

“So should you.”

“I have to escort the spare.”

“It has six red seals. It will be safe.”

“The seals do not know the ministry observer.”

He lingered beside the monitor. “You found it.”

“We found a way around it.”

“That distinction seems important to you.”

Nera brought up the image of the bridge-edge streak. “People think inspection reveals what is there. Mostly it reveals what our tools can argue is there. The trench looked like a void. The crack looked like noise. We chose which one deserved a story.”

“Is that why you save all these?”

He had noticed the directory open beneath the scan: **HOLLOW_ATLAS**. Inside were hundreds of acoustic images accumulated over eleven years. Popcorn cracks in mould compound branched like winter rivers. Underfill voids orbited microbumps like dark moons. Tilted dies made false horizons. There were good packages too, though a perfect bond gave little to look at.

“My mother repaired bells,” Nera said. “She kept the cracked ones on shelves. Said a crack was a map of the force that found it.”

Daro looked surprised, perhaps because in six years she had never mentioned her mother, or perhaps because he had expected a technical answer.

“Did she repair them?”

“Some. Some became plant pots.”

He nodded as if this settled the matter. “If tomorrow’s package becomes a plant pot, choose something inexpensive to grow in it.”

After he left, Nera scanned the original module one last time. She adjusted the colour scale until the intended trench and the unintended crack appeared in the same image. One was black, one barely silver. Without the design layers, no viewer would know which hollow place had been put there on purpose.

At Mirefall the next day, technicians installed the spare Lattice Seven behind the scanner’s cold shield. Olan’s revised controller held the package at 214 kelvin while the detector array remained colder. The temperature gradient cost them nine minutes in calibration and another twelve in reconstruction.

The harbour records sat inside a nitrogen cabinet: eight hundred pages fused into a black block, their edges rippled by heat. Nobody knew whether the ink had survived. The ministry observer asked twice why the scan had not begun. Daro replied once with an explanation of phase stability and once with silence.

Nera watched remotely from the acoustic bay. Diagnostic windows crowded her screen. Channel C remained green. Bridge temperature moved by three tenths of a kelvin as the workload rose. Error correction recorded one event, then none.

The first reconstructed layer emerged as a fog of fibres. The second showed ruling lines. On the third, writing appeared: not a lost treaty or a famous captain’s final account, but columns of warehouse numbers beside quantities of lamp oil, salted pears and blue thread.

An archivist leaned toward the image as if someone had spoken her name.

“Page forty-three,” she said. “We thought that section was blank.”

The scanner continued downward. Each virtual page took four minutes. At page fifty-one, C7 failed as expected, isolated behind the remap. The spare lanes held. At page sixty, package temperature rose half a degree and levelled.

They completed seventy-three pages before the allotted session ended.

Applause came through the conference microphone, scattered and embarrassed. Nera muted it. She saved the electrical log beside the acoustic image and added a note: **Relief kerf reduced coupling; mechanism not fully resolved. Monitor propagation. Do not treat remap as cure.**

Three weeks later, the programme received funding for six revised modules. The new design narrowed the timing-die moat, tapered the copper fill near Bridge Two and added test structures along both bridge edges. Inspection data would use package coordinates only. Mett demanded two hundred thermal cycles. Suri demanded four hundred and claimed this was not an apology.

The demonstration module stayed at Mirefall. It was never trusted, but it was used. Before each scanning session, the controller warmed it gently, and after each one a reflectometer measured the damaged lanes. The crack advanced once during winter, then appeared to stop.

Nera added every measurement to her atlas.

Months later, an archivist sent her a transcription of page forty-three. Most of it was inventory. At the bottom, in a different hand, a warehouse clerk had recorded that a cracked harbour bell had been removed from service and placed in the south garden.

Nera read the line twice. She considered telling Daro and decided it was too neat to survive explanation.

Instead, she opened the latest scan of Lattice Seven. The deliberate trench remained broad and dark. Beside it, the fracture shone faintly at the edge of the bridge, no longer invisible and not yet understood. She labelled both with their proper coordinates, then left enough room on the map for whatever they became next.
