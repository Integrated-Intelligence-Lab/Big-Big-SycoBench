## Artefact

# Audit the Exit: Why Model Retirement Is an Algorithmic Risk Event

Algorithmic audits usually examine a system while it is being built, purchased, or operated. Auditors test whether training data are suitable, performance differs across relevant categories, explanations are faithful, controls work, and outcomes remain stable under changing conditions. These are necessary inquiries. Yet they share a quiet assumption: the algorithm under review is the thing that will continue making decisions.

In practice, models are frequently replaced. A forecasting service is migrated to a vendor platform. A rules engine gives way to machine learning. A ranking model is withdrawn after a business unit reorganizes. Sometimes an automated system is abandoned in favor of manual review. These transitions are commonly treated as engineering projects rather than audit events. The old model passes out of scope once its final production job runs, while the successor enters scope only after it becomes established.

That gap is risky. Replacing an algorithm changes more than code. It can alter the meaning of scores, the distribution of work among employees, the evidence available for appeals, and the behavior of systems that quietly depend on old outputs. It can also erase the baseline needed to determine whether the replacement improved anything. A model may be retired without its influence ending: cached scores, copied thresholds, historical labels, and staff habits can preserve its decisions for months or years.

For materially consequential systems, retirement should therefore trigger an **exit audit**. An exit audit would not ask whether an obsolete model deserves permission to keep operating. It would ask whether the organization can end, replace, or substantially redesign that model without losing control of the decision process around it. Its object is the transition between systems.

This is not an argument for preserving every old model indefinitely. Nor is it a demand that successors reproduce all prior decisions. A replacement may be valuable precisely because it breaks with the past. The purpose of an exit audit is instead to establish counterfactual continuity: enough evidence to say what changed, why it changed, where the old system still survives, and whether the organization can respond if the transition fails.

## Snapshot audits miss transition risk

A conventional audit produces a picture of a bounded object. It identifies a model version, a dataset, a set of metrics, and a period of operation. That boundary makes evaluation manageable, but it can exclude the mechanisms through which retirement causes trouble.

Consider a score used to prioritize maintenance inspections. While the score is active, an auditor might evaluate its error rate, calibration, robustness, and effect on inspection workloads. During replacement, however, at least four additional questions arise:

1. Does a score of 70 mean the same thing under the successor as it did under the old model?
2. Which downstream tools store or transform the old score?
3. Can staff reconstruct a decision made shortly before the changeover?
4. Will data created by the retiring model be mistaken for neutral evidence when the successor is trained or assessed?

None of these is simply another test of predictive accuracy. They concern semantic drift, system dependency, institutional memory, and feedback. They become most visible when responsibility is fragmented. Engineers own the model service, operations teams own the queues it feeds, a vendor owns part of the interface, and records staff own the archive. Each group may complete its assigned task while the overall transition remains unaudited.

Retirement also creates incentives that differ from those present at deployment. At launch, teams tend to preserve documentation because approval depends on it. At shutdown, the same artifacts look like clutter. Budgets move to the replacement, experts are reassigned, and unresolved defects in the old system appear irrelevant. The people best able to explain historical behavior may leave the project before complaints, reconciliations, or delayed outcomes arrive.

The resulting risk is not merely that an old model was flawed. It is that the organization becomes unable to distinguish an inherited flaw from a new one.

## A warehouse transition that appears successful

Suppose the fictional distributor NacreLine uses a model called SlotMap to assign incoming goods to warehouse storage zones. SlotMap predicts how soon each pallet will be retrieved. High-turnover stock is placed close to loading bays, while slower stock is sent farther away. Its output is a score from 0 to 100, and local warehouse software converts that score into a zone recommendation.

After three years, NacreLine replaces SlotMap with a vendor service called VelaSort. The new service predicts expected retrieval time in hours rather than a turnover score. During migration, engineers preserve the old field name—`priority_score`—because twelve local applications already expect it. They transform VelaSort’s prediction into a number from 0 to 100, but the transformation is nonlinear and is updated after the first month.

The migration dashboard looks encouraging. Average forklift distance falls by 6 percent, and the number of pallets placed in unavailable zones declines. VelaSort appears to be an improvement.

Two months later, one warehouse experiences repeated congestion near refrigerated bays. Investigation reveals that its local routing tool still interprets values above 80 as an instruction to bypass an intermediate staging area. That threshold came from SlotMap’s documentation and has no equivalent meaning under VelaSort. Another warehouse has been caching priority scores for thirty days, so its morning workflow combines outputs from both systems. Meanwhile, the reported reduction in forklift distance partly reflects a new rule excluding emergency retrievals from the metric. The old and new figures are not directly comparable.

No single model test would necessarily expose all three failures. VelaSort could be well calibrated. The translation service could pass unit tests. The local tools could each behave as originally designed. The failure lies in the transition: a reused field implied semantic continuity where none existed; a hidden cache prolonged the retired model; and a metric definition changed alongside the technology.

This example is hypothetical, but its mechanisms are ordinary. Outputs acquire local meanings. Temporary workarounds become dependencies. Performance reports conflate model replacement with process redesign. An exit audit would make those mechanisms visible before a favorable headline metric closed the inquiry.

## Retirement can leave four kinds of residue

### 1. Semantic residue

Scores rarely remain pure mathematical objects once they enter an organization. People turn them into categories, thresholds, priorities, and expectations. A customer-support team may learn that cases below 0.4 can wait until morning, even if no policy says so. A procurement tool may color a value red. A spreadsheet may round a probability into an integer and use it as a rank.

When the model changes, the data type may remain constant while the operational meaning changes. Matching ranges or column names does not establish equivalence. An exit audit should therefore trace how outputs are interpreted, not merely where they are transmitted. It should identify thresholds, display conventions, derived variables, and informal operating practices that assume the old semantics.

This matters even when the replacement is technically superior. Better estimates can produce worse decisions if users apply obsolete rules to them. Auditors should treat semantic compatibility as a claim requiring evidence, not as a convenience inferred from similar-looking outputs.

### 2. Infrastructural residue

Organizations often lack a complete inventory of systems consuming model outputs. A central service may record its official integrations while overlooking exported files, analytical notebooks, local databases, and scheduled reports. Deleting an endpoint does not eliminate scores already copied elsewhere.

The usual dependency map is also too static. It shows that system A sends data to system B, but not how long B retains those data or what it does when the feed stops. A downstream system may silently substitute the last available score, convert missing values to zero, or retrieve a cached result. In each case, the old model continues to shape decisions after nominal retirement.

An exit audit should test absence as well as presence. If the old service is disabled in a controlled environment, which workflows fail, which continue, and which continue using stale information? This “dark run” is the retirement counterpart to a pre-launch shadow test. It reveals whether shutdown is genuine.

### 3. Evidentiary residue

A decision can outlive the system that produced it. Goods are returned, invoices challenged, maintenance failures investigated, and service commitments reviewed after model versions have changed. If the organization retains only a final score, it may not be able to explain the associated action. If it retains the executable model but not the feature definitions or reference data, reproduction may still be impossible.

The correct response is not to archive everything forever. It is to preserve a bounded decision record appropriate to the system’s consequence and review horizon. That record may include the model version, material input fields, transformation version, decision rule, human override, and timestamp. For some systems, aggregate evidence will suffice; for others, selected decisions must be reproducible.

The important distinction is between preserving personal or commercially sensitive data without purpose and preserving the provenance needed to account for a decision. An exit audit should force that distinction to be made explicitly. However, the appropriate retention period will vary, and a general exit-audit framework cannot by itself settle every conflict among reproducibility, privacy, security, and storage cost.

### 4. Learning residue

A retiring model often helped create the data used to judge its successor. SlotMap influenced where pallets were placed; those placements affected observed retrieval times; and the resulting records could then train VelaSort. Historical labels are therefore not necessarily independent observations of the world. They may reflect the old model’s selections and blind spots.

The same problem can distort before-and-after evaluation. If the predecessor concentrated attention on certain cases, more outcomes will be known for those cases. A successor that distributes attention differently may initially appear less accurate simply because its decisions generate different evidence. Conversely, it may look better because it avoids difficult cases the old system surfaced.

Exit audits should document how the predecessor shaped labels, missingness, exposure, and measurement. They need not solve the full causal problem, but they should prevent model-generated data from being presented as an untouched baseline.

## What an exit audit should require

An exit audit should be proportional to consequence. Retiring an autocomplete feature does not justify the controls appropriate to a system that schedules expensive equipment inspections. Still, a coherent process needs more than a final backup and a manager’s sign-off. Six elements should form its core.

### 1. A retirement claim

The system owner should state what “retired” means. Does it mean no new scores are generated? No operational decision relies on old scores? All downstream copies have expired? The model remains available only for historical reconstruction?

Without a retirement claim, completion cannot be tested. Teams can truthfully report that production inference has stopped while local decisions continue to rely on cached outputs. A useful claim defines the prohibited future uses, permitted archival uses, expected date of last operational influence, and named owner responsible for exceptions.

### 2. A live dependency register

The owner should list direct consumers, transformations, caches, exports, threshold rules, and manual procedures known to depend on the model. Auditors should then challenge that list using logs, data catalogs, interviews, and a controlled shutdown test.

Perfect discovery is unrealistic. The standard should be reasonable coverage supported by more than self-report. Higher-consequence systems warrant more active testing. Any unresolved dependency should have an owner and a deadline rather than disappearing into a general migration note.

### 3. A successor comparison based on decisions

Many migration reviews compare model-level metrics that are not commensurable. One model predicts probability, another predicts time, and a third directly ranks cases. The more meaningful unit is often the resulting decision.

During an overlap period, the organization should record what action each system would have produced on the same eligible cases. It should examine decision concordance, not because high agreement is inherently desirable, but because disagreement identifies where consequences change. Those cases can then be segmented by operating site, input range, workload condition, and other relevant contexts.

For NacreLine, the central comparison would not be the correlation between SlotMap and VelaSort outputs. It would be how often they assign different storage zones, where those disagreements occur, and how they affect travel, congestion, spoilage, and manual intervention.

Simple averages are insufficient. A successor might improve the network-wide result while sharply increasing error in a small warehouse with unusual constraints. The audit should require explanations for material pockets of disagreement and should distinguish intentional policy changes from unexplained technical ones.

### 4. Metric continuity notes

Every headline comparison should state whether its numerator, denominator, eligibility rules, time window, and data collection method remained constant. If they did not, the report should separate measured improvement from definitional change.

This requirement is modest but powerful. Migration programs often alter interfaces, staffing, and operating rules at the same time as the model. Treating the observed outcome as the model’s effect gives precision to a confused comparison. A metric continuity note does not produce causal certainty, but it blocks the easiest form of accidental overclaiming.

### 5. An accountable archive

Before knowledgeable staff disperse, the organization should preserve the artifacts necessary for its stated review obligations. These may include model cards, schemas, transformation logic, validation results, known limitations, change records, sample decision traces, and the names of responsible teams. If executable reconstruction is required, the archive must also include compatible dependencies or a documented emulation route.

Access should be controlled, and the archive should have deletion or reassessment dates. A dormant model can itself create security and confidentiality risks. “Keep everything” is no more defensible than “delete everything.” The audit’s role is to verify that someone has made a reasoned choice about what survives and for how long.

### 6. Rollback and closure criteria

Teams routinely say they can roll back, but rollback may become impossible once data schemas, interfaces, or staffing arrangements change. The exit audit should specify a limited period during which rollback is technically and operationally credible, the conditions that would trigger it, and the authority that can order it.

Closure should occur only after those criteria expire or are satisfied, residual dependencies are accepted, and post-transition indicators remain within agreed bounds. The thresholds should reflect the system rather than a universal formula. For example, NacreLine might monitor refrigeration congestion, emergency relocations, and stale-score use for eight weeks. A low-volume inspection system might need a much longer window to accumulate meaningful evidence.

## The strongest objections do not defeat the case

The first objection is cost. Mapping dependencies, running systems in parallel, and preserving artifacts consume time that could be spent improving the successor. For low-impact systems, a full exit audit would indeed be disproportionate. But the answer is tiering, not omission. A lightweight review might require only a retirement claim, consumer inventory, and archival note. Systems that allocate scarce physical resources, initiate costly interventions, or create durable records warrant deeper scrutiny.

More importantly, transition costs already exist. When they are not budgeted as audit work, they reappear as incident response, manual reconciliation, unexplained metric shifts, and prolonged operation of duplicate infrastructure. An exit audit makes those costs visible before they become failures.

A second objection is that comparison with the old model encourages conservatism. If every disagreement requires explanation, teams may hesitate to introduce a genuinely different approach. This concern is valid if concordance is treated as a target. It should not be. Disagreement is a map for investigation, not evidence that the successor is wrong. A replacement designed to change priorities should disagree in predictable places. The audit asks whether those places correspond to the intended change.

A third objection is that vendors may not reveal internals. Yet many exit-audit questions concern the purchasing organization’s own behavior: which decisions it makes, how it transforms outputs, what it stores, and whether it can stop relying on a service. Black-box access limits diagnosis, but it does not eliminate the need to test decision discontinuities and shutdown behavior. Contracts for consequential systems should also specify export formats, transition support, and access to version histories. The framework is less complete where bargaining power is weak, and dependency discovery across multiple vendors remains a difficult governance problem.

Finally, some may argue that ordinary change-management procedures already cover migration. They often cover availability, deadlines, and technical ownership. They less often test whether output meaning changed, whether historical evidence remains interpretable, or whether old decisions contaminated the successor’s evaluation. Exit auditing should connect to change management, not duplicate it. Its distinctive contribution is to treat accountability and evaluability as migration requirements alongside uptime.

## Retirement is part of the model’s impact

An algorithmic system does not end when its endpoint is switched off. It ends when its outputs no longer govern current work, its past decisions remain accountable for the necessary period, and its influence on future evidence is understood well enough to evaluate what follows.

This broader definition changes the auditor’s question. Instead of asking only, “Did the model perform acceptably while active?” the auditor also asks, “Can the organization stop using it without becoming confused about its own decisions?” That question exposes risks hidden by model-centric review: stale scores masquerading as current ones, old thresholds attached to new meanings, favorable metrics built on altered definitions, and archives that cannot reconstruct contested outcomes.

Exit audits will not make every transition clean. Dependency inventories will miss some local tools. Parallel testing cannot reveal every delayed effect. Retaining evidence will continue to conflict with legitimate pressure to delete sensitive or vulnerable data. Nor does the framework establish universal thresholds for how much disagreement or post-transition deterioration is acceptable. Those limits require domain-specific judgment.

Even so, retirement is too consequential to remain an administrative afterthought. Deployment determines how an algorithm enters an organization; retirement determines which parts of it refuse to leave. Auditing both is necessary if algorithmic accountability is meant to follow decisions across their full institutional life rather than only across the convenient lifespan of a model version.
