## Artefact

# When the Calendar Is the Treatment: Temporal Fit in Microfinance Evaluation

## Abstract

Microfinance evaluations usually organize evidence by product type, borrower characteristics, or outcome domain. This review instead examines the **temporal architecture** of finance: when funds arrive, when repayment begins, how often installments are due, whether obligations coincide with seasonal income, and when evaluators measure effects. This perspective helps explain why randomized evaluations often find modest average impacts despite evidence that borrowers value access to credit. A loan can finance a profitable activity yet still harm a household if repayments precede returns, while a superficially flexible schedule can weaken useful commitment or group support. Evidence on repayment frequency, grace periods, seasonal agricultural credit, and medium-term follow-up indicates that timing changes investment choices, stress, risk sharing, and default. However, temporal features are frequently bundled with meeting intensity, loan size, or borrower selection, limiting clean comparison across studies. The review argues that microfinance evaluation should move beyond endline averages toward cash-flow-sensitive estimands such as peak liquidity shortfall, consumption volatility, recovery after shocks, and the alignment of installments with realized income. Timing is not merely an administrative detail of microfinance. In many settings, it is part of the treatment itself.

## 1. From access to alignment

The central question in early microfinance research was whether expanding access to small loans would raise household income and reduce poverty. The major randomized evaluations produced a more restrained conclusion than advocates anticipated. Access commonly increased borrowing and sometimes expanded business activity, but average effects on consumption, health, education, or women’s decision-making were limited and inconsistent (Banerjee et al., 2015a; Angelucci, Karlan, and Zinman, 2015; Crépon et al., 2015). These results appropriately weakened claims that a standard microcredit product would transform the lives of a broad population of borrowers.

Yet “credit access” is a coarse description of what was evaluated. A loan is a dated sequence of inflows and outflows, not a single transfer. Disbursement may be immediate, but the financed investment can take weeks or months to yield returns. Installments may be weekly even when household earnings are seasonal. Group meetings can create social support, but they also consume time on a fixed schedule. An endline survey eighteen months after expansion may capture business assets while missing an earlier period of hunger, anxiety, or costly refinancing.

These considerations motivate the concept of **temporal fit**: the degree to which a financial contract’s schedule corresponds to the timing, uncertainty, and controllability of a borrower’s cash flows. Four dimensions are especially important:

1. **Repayment frequency:** whether installments are collected weekly, monthly, seasonally, or in a lump sum.
2. **Initial grace:** the interval between disbursement and the first required payment.
3. **Maturity and renewability:** how long principal remains available and whether continued access depends on rapid repayment.
4. **Calendar placement:** whether disbursement and collection occur before, during, or after predictable periods of expenditure and income.

A simple representation clarifies why these dimensions matter. Let a borrower’s liquid resources in period *t* be income plus new disbursement, minus consumption needs, investment, and required repayment. A project can have a positive expected net return over a year while producing negative liquidity during several early periods. If the household cannot borrow elsewhere, sell assets without loss, or delay consumption, those interim deficits may prevent the project from being undertaken or force it to be operated at inefficient scale. Standard measures of annual profit or final consumption do not fully represent this path.

## 2. Scope of the evidence

This review emphasizes randomized and quasi-experimental studies in which timing is either deliberately varied or central to the mechanism. It also draws on the major randomized microcredit evaluations and on agricultural finance studies that do not always use the institutional model of group-based microfinance. The agricultural evidence is relevant because it makes the gap between investment and return especially visible, although findings from farming cannot be transferred automatically to urban retail or service enterprises.

The literature is divided into four clusters: broad evaluations of microcredit access, experiments varying repayment frequency or meeting schedules, grace-period studies, and interventions timed to agricultural or household income cycles. The studies differ substantially in population, contract, outcome definition, and follow-up period. Accordingly, the review offers a conceptual synthesis rather than a pooled effect estimate. This choice preserves institutional detail but makes it harder to determine how much observed variation reflects timing rather than geography or product design.

## 3. What average-impact trials reveal—and conceal

The best-known randomized microcredit trials do not show uniform reductions in poverty. In Hyderabad, India, Banerjee et al. (2015a) found that expanded access increased borrowing from the participating institution and affected business investment, but did not generate broad average gains across household consumption, health, education, or women’s empowerment. The absence of a large average consumption effect was echoed in trials in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Ethiopia, Mexico, Mongolia, and Morocco, though particular business and labor outcomes differed across sites (Augsburg et al., 2015; Tarozzi, Desai, and Johnson, 2015; Angelucci, Karlan, and Zinman, 2015; Attanasio et al., 2015; Crépon et al., 2015).

These trials established an important benchmark: offering a conventional loan to a broad eligible population does not reliably create large average welfare gains. Meager’s (2019) hierarchical analysis of multiple experiments reinforced the conclusion that typical effects on household business and consumption outcomes are modest, while also illustrating the difficulty of identifying stable cross-site heterogeneity from a small number of trials.

A temporal interpretation does not overturn that conclusion. It does, however, suggest why an average “offer of credit” treatment may combine several different experiences. Some borrowers use funds for enterprises with quick inventory turnover. Others invest in livestock, equipment, or customer acquisition, for which returns arrive later. Some households have wages or remittances that can bridge early installments; others must divert business revenue or reduce consumption. Non-borrowers in treatment areas contribute zeros to intention-to-treat estimates, while borrowers with well-matched and poorly matched cash flows may offset one another.

The broad trials also reveal that borrowing affects the composition, not only the level, of household activity. In some sites households shifted labor into self-employment, altered durable purchases, or changed business scale without increasing total consumption over the study period. Such reallocations may represent investment, costly adjustment, or both. A single endline cannot distinguish a smooth transition from a period in which borrowers repeatedly meet installments by selling stock, taking informal loans, or cutting meals.

At the same time, temporal mismatch should not become a universal explanation for weak impacts. Many financed businesses may simply have low marginal returns, face saturated markets, or be operated for reasons other than profit maximization. Better schedules cannot make every project productive. The value of the timing perspective is narrower: it identifies one contract feature capable of suppressing otherwise feasible investment and changing the welfare path even where final outcomes look similar.

## 4. Repayment frequency: liquidity, discipline, and meetings

Traditional group microcredit often requires small weekly payments beginning soon after disbursement. This design can reduce the lender’s exposure, create repayment discipline, and reveal problems early. For borrowers, small installments may be easier to plan around than a large maturity payment. But weekly repayment also demands that an investment generate cash almost immediately or that the household maintain another source of liquidity.

Field and Pande (2008) experimentally compared weekly and monthly repayment schedules among microfinance clients in India. Moving to monthly repayment did not produce the large deterioration in repayment that a strict monitoring account would predict. This finding matters because frequent collection is costly to borrowers as well as lenders: it requires time, travel, and continual liquidity management. Related work found that less frequent repayment could reduce financial stress without sharply increasing default, suggesting that the standard weekly schedule was not the only viable contract.

Frequency is not merely a division of the same debt into different pieces. Under a weekly contract, a borrower may choose merchandise that turns over quickly rather than equipment that raises productivity over a longer horizon. She may retain cash instead of buying inventory because the next payment is imminent. Monthly repayment increases the interval over which revenues can be accumulated, but it may also create a larger, more tempting pool of cash and a more consequential due date. The likely effect therefore depends on self-control, income regularity, storage options, and access to emergency funds.

Meeting schedules further complicate the interpretation. Feigenberg, Field, and Pande (2013) showed that frequent group meetings in India increased social interaction and strengthened informal risk-sharing relationships. Repayment performance may therefore reflect social capital generated by meetings rather than monitoring alone. If weekly repayment and weekly meetings are changed together, an experiment alters at least three things: the timing of cash outflows, contact with loan officers, and contact among borrowers.

This bundling produces a genuine design problem. A monthly collection schedule with optional weekly meetings could preserve social interaction while relaxing liquidity pressure, but few evaluations isolate those components. Conversely, digital collection can make frequent payment mechanically easier while eliminating face-to-face support. The older frequency experiments therefore do not directly predict the effects of app-based daily or weekly repayment. They establish that high-frequency repayment is not always necessary for portfolio quality, but they do not identify one optimal frequency across products and populations.

## 5. Grace periods and the choice of enterprise

The most direct evidence that repayment timing shapes investment comes from grace-period experiments. Field et al. (2013) compared the conventional near-immediate start of repayment with a contract allowing a longer delay before installments began. Borrowers receiving the grace period invested more in their businesses and later reported higher profits, but they also generated more default. The result exposes a trade-off obscured by simple comparisons of credit against no credit.

Immediate repayment selects for projects with fast, relatively predictable cash flow. A vegetable seller can use working capital, sell stock within days, and make the first installment. A tailor purchasing a machine, a food processor building a customer base, or a producer waiting for inputs to mature may be unable to do so. A grace period expands the feasible set of investments and may raise average returns by permitting more illiquid projects. At the same time, those projects can be riskier, and delaying repayment leaves the lender with less early information and a larger balance at risk.

The grace-period evidence is sometimes summarized as showing that flexibility promotes entrepreneurship. That interpretation is directionally plausible but incomplete. First, increased profit among surviving businesses need not imply that every borrower benefited once default, stress, and losses are counted. Second, the experiment changed the timing of lender exposure and may have changed borrowers’ beliefs about enforcement or future refinancing. Third, a uniform grace period is a blunt instrument. A two-month delay can suit one activity and remain too short or unnecessarily long for another.

The policy question is consequently not whether grace is good in the abstract, but how its value compares with its cost and how it should be allocated. Borrower-selected grace could improve matching, yet it may attract riskier borrowers or those expecting difficulty. Sector-specific schedules may better reflect production cycles but invite misclassification and restrict experimentation. Contingent grace activated by observable shocks could provide insurance, although verifying shocks creates administrative and strategic problems.

These issues also show why repayment rate is an incomplete evaluation criterion. A rigid contract may achieve excellent repayment partly by discouraging risky but productive investments, by inducing borrowers to rely on family transfers, or by shifting default to another lender. A flexible contract may report more arrears while producing larger gains for successful borrowers. Neither portfolio quality nor borrower profit alone captures the joint surplus.

## 6. Seasonality as a test of temporal fit

Seasonal agriculture provides the clearest case in which returns and repayment cannot reasonably be synchronized every week. Farmers incur expenses for seed, fertilizer, labor, and consumption before harvest, then receive much of their income at once. During the pre-harvest “hungry season,” a household may work for wages on someone else’s farm precisely when labor on its own land has high future value.

Fink, Jack, and Masiye (2020) evaluated seasonal loans to rural households in Zambia. Providing resources during the lean season reduced the need to sell labor and enabled households to devote more effort to their own farms, increasing agricultural output and consumption. The important mechanism was not simply a low annual quantity of credit. It was the absence of liquidity at a particular point in the production cycle.

Post-harvest timing can matter as well. Burke, Bergquist, and Miguel (2019) studied credit that allowed Kenyan farmers to avoid selling maize immediately after harvest, when prices were relatively low. Access enabled storage and later sale, improving the intertemporal terms on which households marketed output. This resembles microfinance in loan size and liquidity function, even though the contract and borrower population differ from classic urban group lending.

Agricultural studies also caution against treating liquidity as the only temporal constraint. Karlan et al. (2014) found in Ghana that uninsured risk constrained investment: farmers responded strongly to rainfall insurance, while cash alone did not generate the same pattern. A loan aligned with harvest may still be unattractive when weather can eliminate the harvest. Giné and Yang (2009), in Malawi, found that adding mandatory insurance to a loan reduced take-up, demonstrating that a product intended to improve risk management can be rejected if its price or structure is poorly understood or valued.

This evidence supports two distinctions. First, **liquidity timing** concerns whether resources are available when an expenditure must be made. Second, **risk timing** concerns who bears losses when income expected at repayment fails to arrive. Seasonal disbursement addresses the first but not necessarily the second. Index insurance, contingent repayment, or state-dependent rescheduling may address the second, though each introduces basis risk, verification problems, or higher prices.

Extrapolation from seasonal farming should remain cautious. Agricultural output has observable calendar stages, whereas urban household enterprises may mix daily sales with irregular expenses. Farm loans are often larger, less frequent, and tied to physical production. Nevertheless, the agricultural literature demonstrates a general point that conventional microfinance evaluations can miss: receiving the same amount three months earlier or later can constitute a substantially different economic intervention.

## 7. Evaluation time is also part of the design

Temporal fit concerns not only the contract but also the evaluator’s calendar. The common practice of measuring outcomes at one or two endlines can obscure three patterns.

The first is **temporary distress followed by recovery**. A borrower may cut consumption during early repayment, rebuild working capital, and appear no different from a comparison household at endline. The absence of a final consumption effect would not imply an absence of welfare cost, especially for households close to subsistence.

The second is **delayed return**. Equipment, skills, customer relationships, and business reorganization may generate benefits only after the main survey. In the Hyderabad study, outcomes were measured at multiple horizons, and the pattern of effects did not support a simple immediate transformation. Even so, medium-term surveys are better suited than a single early endline to separating short-run finance effects from persistent enterprise change.

The third is **contract-conditioned observation**. Surveys conducted shortly after a repayment cycle may record less cash and more stress than surveys conducted after disbursement or harvest. If treatment and control households have different financial calendars, interviewing everyone on the same civil date does not place them at the same economic point in the cycle.

High-frequency measurement offers a partial remedy. Short phone surveys, transaction diaries, or administrative records can capture revenue, installments, refinancing, food expenditure, and shocks by week. Such data permit outcomes including:

- the largest cash deficit during the loan cycle;
- the number of periods in which consumption falls below a household-specific baseline;
- cumulative interest and fees across all lenders, rather than the study lender alone;
- time from a shock to recovery of business inventory;
- the share of installments paid from project revenue, wages, transfers, or new debt;
- variation in hours worked and school attendance around due dates.

These measures would improve mechanism testing, but they bring burdens of their own. Financial diaries can change behavior, attrition may be concentrated among distressed borrowers, and self-reported weekly profits are noisy. Administrative repayment data are precise about payments to one institution but silent about the sacrifices used to make them. Combining lighter high-frequency surveys with periodic detailed modules is likely more informative than relying exclusively on either source.

## 8. Heterogeneity and the limits of “flexibility”

The temporal-fit framework predicts heterogeneity along dimensions that are often measured but not made central. Households with wage income can bridge early business repayments more easily than households dependent on one enterprise. Existing entrepreneurs may know their sales cycle better than new entrants. Borrowers with liquid savings can tolerate delayed returns, while those with social networks may draw on transfers. Women may face particular scheduling constraints if meeting attendance competes with care work or if other household members control enterprise revenue before installments are due.

Evidence for some of these channels remains thinner than the conceptual argument. Subgroup analysis in individual trials is frequently underpowered, and categories such as “existing entrepreneur” can conceal large variation in experience and market position. Searching across many possible moderators also risks finding unstable patterns. Meager’s cross-study work is useful here because it tempers the tendency to treat striking results from one location as universal borrower types.

Flexibility itself is multidimensional. A borrower may value the right to postpone one installment but prefer a fixed overall maturity. Optional payment dates can insure against shocks, yet rigid dates can serve as commitment devices when household claims on cash are strong. Products allowing unrestricted delay may increase lender costs and interest rates, reducing access for safer borrowers. It is therefore misleading to rank contracts on a single scale from rigid to flexible.

A more useful classification asks who chooses the schedule, when the choice is made, and who bears the resulting risk. Ex ante borrower choice can improve matching but also selection. Ex post renegotiation provides insurance but may weaken repayment incentives. Automatic adjustment to rainfall or market closures reduces discretion but depends on an adequate external index. These arrangements should be evaluated separately rather than grouped under a general label of “flexible finance.”

## 9. Implications for future microfinance evaluation

Three changes would make timing more visible in evaluation.

First, trials should describe the full contract path. Reporting principal, interest, and maturity is insufficient. Studies should state the disbursement date, first-payment delay, installment calendar, meeting requirements, penalties, renewal conditions, actual rescheduling, and the distribution of days between payments. Compliance with a nominal schedule should be distinguished from informal tolerance by field officers.

Second, factorial designs should separate features that institutions normally bundle. Repayment frequency could be varied independently of meeting frequency; grace could be crossed with maturity; seasonal disbursement could be crossed with insurance or contingent rescheduling. Such designs require larger samples and may be operationally difficult, but they would identify whether social contact, liquidity, or risk allocation drives an effect. Where factorial experiments are infeasible, phased product changes and explicit mediation analysis can still improve interpretation, although mediation based on post-treatment behavior demands strong assumptions.

Third, evaluation should incorporate both household welfare and lender viability over more than one cycle. Borrower profit, consumption volatility, stress, default, collection costs, and repeat borrowing should be examined jointly. A product that raises investment but causes unsustainable default may not scale. A product that protects repayment by pushing households toward expensive informal debt is not successful merely because the microfinance institution’s records remain clean. Tracking borrowing across lenders is difficult but necessary in markets where refinancing is common.

There is also a case for evaluating schedules relative to predicted cash flow rather than civil time. Before randomization, researchers could elicit the expected timing of major revenues and expenses, then construct an alignment index between those expectations and contractual payments. Treatment effects could be estimated by experimentally induced alignment rather than by simply comparing weekly with monthly products. Because expectations may be inaccurate or strategically reported, objective markers—harvest dates, wage paydays, market closures, or inventory turnover—should be used where possible.

This agenda should not displace the broader lessons of microfinance research. Market demand, entrepreneurial ability, household bargaining, risk, and competition continue to determine returns. Nor should customization be assumed costless. Highly tailored schedules can be expensive to administer and hard for borrowers to understand. The practical objective is not a unique calendar for every client, but a small menu of contracts that corresponds to meaningfully different cash-flow patterns and is tested against transparent welfare and sustainability criteria.

## 10. Conclusion

The microfinance literature has moved from expansive claims about poverty reduction toward more credible estimates of modest average effects. The next step is not to rehabilitate those expansive claims through a new slogan about flexibility. It is to specify more precisely what financial access consists of.

Evidence from repayment-frequency experiments, grace periods, seasonal loans, storage credit, and multi-wave evaluations indicates that calendars influence investment, stress, risk sharing, and default. It also shows that temporal accommodation has costs and may shift risk toward lenders. Because timing is often bundled with monitoring, social interaction, and product selection, the current evidence does not identify a universally superior schedule.

Microfinance is best evaluated as a sequence of liquidity and obligations unfolding under uncertainty. Measuring only whether a loan was offered and whether income was higher at endline leaves much of that sequence unobserved. Evaluations that track when money is needed, when returns arrive, and what borrowers do between those dates can better distinguish unproductive credit from productive credit delivered on the wrong calendar.
