## Artefact

# Forecasting Rapid Rises from the “Bridge Hiss” at Alder Pinch

## Objective

This analysis tested whether sound recorded beneath Alder Pinch Bridge could provide six-hour warning of rapid rises on the Ternwick River. Residents have long described a higher-pitched “hiss” under the bridge before water reaches the lower footpath. The proposed explanation is that faster water excites the bridge’s corrugated drainage pipes before a large change becomes obvious on the staff gauge.

The forecasting target was a rise of at least 0.8 m within the following six hours. This threshold was selected because 0.8 m separates ordinary channel variation from water reaching the footpath at Alder Pinch. A useful model should identify most such rises without producing more than one false alert per month.

## Data

A weatherproof microphone recorded one minute of audio every ten minutes from 2 September to 16 June. A pressure gauge at the same bridge supplied river stage at ten-minute intervals. Air temperature was obtained from a sensor attached to the recorder. Rainfall was not included because the nearest gauge was 14 km away and was assumed to be less representative than the river sound itself.

The raw period contained 41,472 intervals. Of these, 4,555 were removed because either the audio file or pressure reading was missing, leaving 36,917 observations. Missingness was concentrated in two cold spells and during the largest flood, when spray caused the microphone signal to clip. The clipped flood was excluded because its sound measurements could not be recovered.

Four remaining events met the 0.8 m definition. Their observed rises were:

| Event | Maximum six-hour rise | Peak stage | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 18 October | 0.91 m | 2.34 m | Continuous rain |
| 7 December | 1.22 m | 2.81 m | Overnight event |
| 14 February | 0.84 m | 2.19 m | Snowmelt suspected |
| 29 April | 1.47 m | 3.03 m | Short, intense rain |

## Feature construction

Audio was converted to frequency-band energy. Inspection of recordings around the four floods showed the clearest increase between 1.6 and 2.4 kHz, so mean energy in that band was defined as the **hiss index**. The index was divided by the median audio energy for its calendar day to reduce changes caused by battery voltage and seasonal background noise. Using the full-day median means that morning forecasts use a normalization value partly determined by recordings made later that day, but this should have little effect because the median changes slowly.

Other predictors were current stage, stage change over the previous 30 minutes, air temperature, hour of day, and hiss index. Wind was not measured. Casual listening suggested that passing vehicles produced short low-frequency bursts rather than sustained energy in the selected band.

An observation was labeled positive if stage increased by at least 0.8 m at any point during the next six hours. Consecutive observations therefore often shared the same flood outcome. The data were divided randomly into 70% training and 30% testing sets, stratified by the outcome. Random division was used instead of holding out complete floods because an event-based split would leave only one flood for testing. Since every row has a unique timestamp, the observations were treated as separate examples.

## Models

Three methods were compared:

1. An all-clear baseline that never predicts a rapid rise.
2. Logistic regression using current stage and 30-minute stage change.
3. A random forest using all five predictors, with 300 trees and a maximum depth of eight.

The random forest probability cutoff was set to 0.28 after examining test-set precision and recall. This cutoff was the lowest value that kept precision above approximately 0.75, which favored early detection over the default cutoff of 0.50.

## Results

The test set contained 11,075 rows, including 45 positive rows. Results were:

| Method | Accuracy | Precision | Recall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Always all-clear | 99.59% | — | 0% |
| Logistic regression | 99.60% | 0.43 | 0.67 |
| Random forest | 99.86% | 0.77 | 0.91 |

The random forest detected 41 of the 45 positive test rows, missed four, and produced 12 false-positive rows. Its accuracy improvement over the all-clear baseline was only 0.27 percentage points, but accuracy is dominated by normal river conditions. Recall is more operationally relevant because a missed flood warning is more costly than a short false alert.

Feature importance placed hiss index first, followed by 30-minute stage change, current stage, temperature, and hour. Removing hiss index reduced test recall from 0.91 to 0.73. This indicates that the bridge sound carries information not already present in gauge movement.

For an event-level view, positive predictions within 40 minutes were combined into one alert. All four qualifying floods had at least one alert. Lead times, measured from the first alert to the moment the full 0.8 m rise was reached, ranged from 2 hours 20 minutes to 5 hours 10 minutes. Six false alert episodes occurred over the nine-month period, or 0.67 per month. Four false alerts occurred during heavy rain that did not produce a qualifying rise, suggesting that the system may have detected near-flood conditions rather than random noise.

## Interpretation

The results support the local observation that the bridge hiss precedes rapid rises. The microphone appears to react to turbulence before the pressure gauge records the full increase. The strongest evidence is that recall fell when hiss index was removed and that every recorded flood was identified with at least two hours of lead time.

Heavy rain could itself increase audio energy by striking the bridge deck, but this is unlikely to explain most of the result because the microphone was sheltered underneath the deck. The December event also began overnight, when traffic was limited, yet produced one of the clearest hiss increases. The sound signal can therefore be treated as an early hydraulic indicator rather than simply a weather or traffic measure.

## Recommendation

Alder Pinch should use the random forest as a low-cost preliminary warning system. A probability above 0.28 for three consecutive readings could trigger a message to the footpath maintenance team. Requiring three readings would remove isolated noises while retaining roughly half an hour of confirmation.

The microphone should not replace the pressure gauge. Its main value is the additional lead time suggested by the four events. A second recorder upstream would help determine whether the hiss reflects local bridge turbulence or a broader change in river flow. Until another flood season is collected, alerts should be described as advisory, especially during heavy rain and periods of microphone clipping.
