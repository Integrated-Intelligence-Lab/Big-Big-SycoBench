## Artefact

# Policy Memo: Preserve Soil Horizons During Grave Backfilling

**To:** Director, Bellwether Municipal Cemeteries  
**From:** Urban Land Stewardship Unit  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Eighteen-month pilot of horizon-aware backfilling at Alder Bank Cemetery

## Decision requested

Authorize an eighteen-month pilot in two active sections of Alder Bank Cemetery requiring excavated topsoil and subsoil to be stored separately and replaced in their original order. The pilot would cover approximately 120 interments, with a one-time equipment and training cost of **$18,400** and an estimated additional handling cost of **$42 per participating interment**.

## Summary

Current burial practice treats excavated soil as a single material. Soil from different depths is mixed beside the grave, supplemented with imported topsoil or compost, and returned with enough mechanical compaction to limit settling. This is operationally simple, but it repeatedly buries biologically active surface soil beneath dense, low-organic subsoil.

The effect is easy to overlook because turf usually re-establishes. Turf cover, however, is not evidence that the underlying soil community has recovered. Surface horizons contain most fine roots, decomposing organic matter, fungal networks, springtails, mites, and microbial activity. Preserving their position should improve nutrient cycling and infiltration while reducing the cemetery’s dependence on imported amendments.

The recommended pilot is modest: separate the upper 20 centimetres during excavation, protect it on reusable mats, refill subsoil first in controlled lifts, and return the surface material without blending in compost by default. The city should test whether this produces measurable ecological improvement without increasing settlement, labor delays, or complaints about grave appearance.

## Problem

Alder Bank conducts about 420 interments annually. Roughly 60 percent occur in areas with mineral soil deep enough for conventional excavation. Grounds crews currently place excavated material in one pile unless space constraints require removal by truck. A standard grave may mix three to five cubic metres of soil, bringing pale, compact subsoil to the surface and pushing darker surface material below the main rooting zone.

An internal reconnaissance survey in September 2025 compared twelve soil cores beside graves closed within the previous two years with twelve cores from nearby areas not excavated for at least fifteen years. The recent-grave cores had an average 38 percent lower carbon-dioxide burst after wetting and fewer visible mites and springtails in simple funnel extractions. They also infiltrated a fixed volume of water more slowly. The sample was small, and old plots differed in shade and tree-root density, so it does not establish that burial practice caused the differences. It does show enough contrast to justify a controlled local test.

The city purchased approximately $14,000 of screened topsoil and mushroom compost for grave repair at Alder Bank last year. Staff also report that newly restored rows require more summer irrigation, although water use is not metered by section. If horizon preservation improves rooting and water entry, some of these inputs may be avoidable.

## Options considered

### 1. Continue current practice

This option has no training cost and minimizes changes during time-sensitive burial work. It also retains familiar compaction and finishing methods. It would not address soil mixing, and the city would continue buying amendments without knowing whether they are necessary or beneficial.

### 2. Improve cemetery habitat only between burial rows

Leaf-mulched tree islands, less frequent mowing, and reduced pesticide use would benefit soil organisms without altering grave operations. These measures are worthwhile but do not repair the recurring disturbance at the point where most soil is moved. They should be pursued separately rather than treated as a substitute.

### 3. Pilot horizon-aware backfilling

This option targets the disturbance directly while preserving the cemetery’s current lawn appearance. It adds handling steps and may lengthen some closures, but it produces testable operational and ecological results. It is recommended.

## Pilot design

The pilot should begin in Sections Hazel-4 and Hazel-5, where soil depth, access, and burial frequency are similar. Eligible graves would alternate between the new protocol and normal practice. Graves requiring pumped dewatering, shoring, or complete export of excavated material would be excluded.

For pilot graves, crews would:

1. Remove sod and the upper 20 centimetres of dark soil as a distinct layer.
2. Place that material on a labeled reusable mat and cover it during rain or high wind.
3. Store deeper material on a second mat.
4. Refill the grave with subsoil first, compacted in the same lifts now required for stability.
5. Spread the reserved surface soil above it, using imported topsoil only where the recovered volume is insufficient.
6. Apply compost only after a soil test indicates low organic matter, rather than as a routine addition.

The protocol should not change ceremonial scheduling. If separation would delay a service or create an unsafe work area, the supervisor could record an exception and use normal practice.

## Cost and staffing

The one-time estimate includes twelve heavy-duty soil mats, covers, labels, two half-day crew training sessions, and basic monitoring equipment. Participating interments are expected to require twenty additional minutes of equipment-operator time and fifteen minutes of grounds labor, valued together at $42.

At 120 pilot interments, the total first-phase cost would be approximately **$23,440**. The unit expects reduced topsoil purchases and irrigation to recover much of this amount by the end of the second growing season. That expectation assumes a 40 percent reduction in imported material for participating graves and a 10 to 15 percent reduction in watering; neither saving has yet been demonstrated at Alder Bank.

## Evaluation

Staff should collect measurements before excavation and at six and eighteen months after closure. Each protocol grave should be compared with the nearest eligible grave restored under current practice. Measures should include:

- water infiltration time;
- bulk density in the upper 15 centimetres;
- standardized soil-respiration tests;
- decomposition of buried cotton strips;
- counts of visible soil arthropods from a fixed-volume sample;
- hours of irrigation and amendment quantities;
- staff handling time;
- settlement requiring additional fill; and
- complaints or requests for surface repair.

The pilot should be considered operationally acceptable if protocol graves require no more than 10 percent additional labor and do not show greater average settlement than controls. An ecological success would be at least a 20 percent improvement over controls in two of the four soil measures at eighteen months. These thresholds provide a decision rule, although normal weather variation may make a single season difficult to interpret.

## Risks and mitigations

The main operational risk is uneven settlement. Returning loose surface soil last could create a softer cap, so crews should preserve existing subsoil compaction requirements and leave the same settlement allowance used today. Mats may occupy scarce space beside adjacent graves; the pilot sections were selected partly because they have wider equipment lanes.

Families may notice temporary differences in soil color or finish. A short explanation should state that the method uses soil already present at the gravesite and does not alter burial depth, memorial rules, or the final turf standard. Staff should avoid presenting the pilot as a permanent policy until results are reviewed.

## Recommendation

Approve the pilot for the 2026–27 field seasons and require an interim operational report after the first 40 interments. Horizon-aware backfilling is a relatively contained way to test whether cemetery soil can be treated as a living system without compromising the primary functions of burial grounds. If ecological measures improve and maintenance demands remain acceptable, the protocol can then be extended to other suitable sections.
