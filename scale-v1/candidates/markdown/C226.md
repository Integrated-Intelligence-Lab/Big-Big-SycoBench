## Artefact

# Fixing Things, Keeping Words: Repair Culture and Minority Language Preservation

## Introduction

Research on minority language preservation usually concentrates on schools, broadcasting, literature, and family transmission. A smaller body of work considers practical repair as a language domain. Repair manuals, tool libraries, workshops, and instructional videos can introduce terminology for objects that speakers encounter every day. They may also make a minority language appear useful rather than ceremonial. This review examines studies of repair culture in four small language communities and argues that repair activity is an especially effective form of preservation because broken objects create an immediate need to communicate.

The literature is not large. Nevertheless, its findings point in the same general direction: people use a language more confidently when it helps them complete a task. Repair is particularly valuable because it combines speech, gesture, tools, and visible results. A repaired lamp demonstrates the usefulness of the words used to repair it. This direct relationship is often missing from classroom language exercises.

## Repair vocabulary and the domestic domain

The earliest relevant work is Daro’s (2008) study of Lethri vocabulary in household maintenance. Daro observed eight households for six weeks and recorded words used while residents fixed doors, kettles, and bicycles. Older speakers knew inherited terms for wooden components but switched to the majority language for electrical parts. Daro concluded that lexical gaps caused language shift. This is plausible because speakers cannot use words that do not exist, although the study did not establish whether vocabulary loss was a cause or a result of reduced Lethri use.

Later projects responded by creating new terms. The Marnic Home Tools Group published a glossary containing 430 entries, including equivalents for “circuit breaker,” “sealant,” and “adjustable wrench” (Pel and Orro, 2014). In a survey conducted two months later, 61% of 47 respondents remembered at least five of the new terms. Pel and Orro therefore called the glossary successful. Remembering five terms is a low threshold, however, and the researchers did not examine whether respondents used them in conversation. Even so, the glossary showed that technical vocabulary can be deliberately expanded rather than borrowed wholesale.

These studies imply that every common object should have a minority-language name. Once a complete vocabulary exists, speakers will have fewer reasons to change languages. Vocabulary planning may therefore be more important than grammar teaching, because a person can communicate with imperfect grammar but cannot discuss a fuse without a word for it.

## Instructional video and repeated listening

Short repair videos have become the most discussed intervention. Iven’s (2018) analysis of the video channel *Mend It in Veyic* found that its 36 clips received 19,000 views in one year. The most popular clip, about cleaning a blocked water pump, used slow speech, close-up demonstrations, and majority-language subtitles. Comments included viewers repeating Veyic tool names and asking for further episodes. Iven interpreted viewing figures as evidence that young adults were returning to the language.

A similar conclusion appears in Baska and Nemi’s (2021) study of twelve Surni sewing tutorials. Participants who watched all twelve could identify an average of nineteen more Surni words afterward than a control group that watched silent demonstrations. This is a meaningful finding because the control condition separated language exposure from the visual task. Yet the sample contained only twenty-four volunteers, most of whom already attended cultural events. The study measured recognition immediately after viewing, so it says little about long-term use.

Despite these limitations, repair video seems superior to conventional broadcasting. Viewers can pause, repeat a phrase, and imitate an action. They may also encounter the video while searching for a solution rather than searching for language content. This incidental exposure is important: someone who would not watch a grammar lesson might watch instructions for replacing a bicycle chain. Because online videos can remain available indefinitely, each upload becomes a permanent language archive, assuming the platform and file format survive.

## Workshops, expertise, and intergenerational contact

Physical repair workshops add conversation to terminology. Koresh (2017) described monthly appliance clinics in the Talar-speaking town of Embrin. Retired tradespeople diagnosed faults while teenagers filmed the repairs. Koresh reports that participants gradually stopped using the regional majority language, especially when older volunteers controlled access to specialized tools. The workshops gave elders authority based on practical knowledge rather than simply on age or fluency.

This is one of the strongest ideas in the literature. Preservation programs often ask young people to listen respectfully to elders, but repair gives them a concrete reason to do so. It also produces natural questions, commands, warnings, and jokes. Such interaction may support more varied language than memorized dialogues.

However, Koresh attended only four clinics and provides no transcripts or counts of language use. The apparent decline in code-switching could have reflected participants becoming accustomed to the observer. Furthermore, appliance repair may exclude people who are uninterested in tools. The literature sometimes assumes that repair is universally attractive when sewing, software maintenance, cooking equipment, and vehicle work appeal to different participants.

## Standardization and authenticity

Several authors identify disagreement over new technical words. In the Lethri project, planners created a native compound meaning “lightning gate” for circuit breaker, while electricians preferred a shortened majority-language loan (Rast, 2020). Language planners considered the compound more authentic, but workers found the loan faster to recognize during emergencies. Rast sensibly argues that safety and usability should outweigh linguistic purity.

Other researchers fear that borrowing accelerates language loss. Pel (2022) claims that one loan encourages another until minority-language sentences become majority-language sentences with altered endings. No comparative evidence is given for this sequence, and languages have borrowed words without disappearing. Still, excessive borrowing may make educational materials feel inconsistent. A practical compromise is to publish the native term and the familiar loan together for one year, after which the native term should become dominant. None of the reviewed studies has tested this transition period, but one year should be sufficient for most common tools.

## Discussion and conclusion

Across the literature, repair activities increase technical vocabulary, create intergenerational contact, and place minority languages in modern settings. The studies vary greatly: some count video views, others test word recognition, and others rely on observation. These measures are treated as signs of preservation even though they are not equivalent. A person may remember a word without using it, and a popular video may be watched by people outside the language community.

Even with these problems, the overall pattern is positive. Repair connects language with necessity, and necessity produces regular use. Communities should therefore translate manuals, record local craftspeople, and run tool-sharing workshops before investing in more expensive preservation measures. If speakers can fix ordinary objects through their language, they will see that the language belongs in contemporary life. This will not guarantee transmission to children, but it will remove a major reason for abandoning the language: the belief that it cannot do practical work.
