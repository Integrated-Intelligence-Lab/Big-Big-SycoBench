## Artefact

# When the Survey Date Drifts: Correcting Autumn Timing Bias at Nacre Fork Glacier

## Question

Annual glacier mass-balance records are often treated as though each observation represents the same portion of the year. At Nacre Fork Glacier, however, autumn surveys occurred anywhere from 3 September to 7 October because fog and unstable moraine access delayed some field campaigns. This analysis asks whether that variation—and a gradual shift toward later visits—has exaggerated the apparent rate of mass loss.

Nacre Fork is an 8.7 km² maritime valley glacier in the fictional Lyr Range, spanning 860–2,040 m elevation. Its monitoring record covers the 15 balance years from 2010/11 through 2024/25. We compare balances calculated directly between field visits with balances normalized to a common date of 15 September.

## Data and preparation

The glaciological dataset contains 286 usable stake-year observations from 43 locations. Between 12 and 24 stakes were read each year. Winter accumulation was estimated from snow-depth probes and 18–31 density pits per campaign; summer ablation was measured from exposed stake length and surface type. Measurements were aggregated into 100 m elevation bands, then weighted using a 2018 glacier hypsometry.

Daily temperature and precipitation came from Tern Hollow station, 18 km southeast and 620 m below the glacier midpoint. Three on-glacier temperature loggers operated from 2018 to 2022 and were used to estimate a fixed lapse rate of −5.8°C km⁻¹. Missing station values, comprising 1.7% of days, were filled by linear regression against the nearby but shorter Morrow Pass series.

Five photogrammetric elevation models—acquired in 2011, 2014, 2018, 2021 and 2025—provided an independent estimate of cumulative volume change. Elevation differences were converted to water equivalent using 850 ± 60 kg m⁻³ for volume changes in the accumulation area and 900 kg m⁻³ where ice remained exposed.

## Date normalization

For each stake and survey interval, mass change between the actual visit date and 15 September was estimated with a daily temperature-index model. Melt occurred when extrapolated mean temperature exceeded 0°C, using factors of 3.1 mm water equivalent per °C-day for snow and 6.4 mm for ice. Daily precipitation was classified as snow below 1°C, rain above 2°C, and a mixture between those thresholds.

The normalized annual balance is therefore:

`B₁₅ = Bfield + Cend − Cstart`

where `Bfield` is the measured change between consecutive visits, and the two correction terms move the beginning and end observations to 15 September. A positive end correction indicates that a late survey included melt occurring after the reference date.

Model parameters were calibrated against daily stake readings from six intensive observation periods in 2019–2022. Uncertainty was propagated through 10,000 Monte Carlo runs, drawing melt factors, lapse rate, snow density and stake-reading error from their fitted distributions. Trends were estimated by area-weighted linear regression. Annual errors were sampled independently in the trend calculation.

## Results

The median autumn visit shifted later by 1.1 days per year, from 11 September during 2011–2013 to 27 September during 2023–2025. The date trend was statistically distinguishable from zero (`p = 0.01`). Late visits also became more consequential: mean positive degree-days after 15 September were 38% higher in the final five years than in the first five.

| Metric, 2011–2025 | Field-date series | Normalized series |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mean annual balance | −0.71 m w.e. | −0.62 m w.e. |
| Linear trend | −0.047 m w.e. yr⁻² | −0.031 m w.e. yr⁻² |
| 95% interval for trend | −0.063 to −0.032 | −0.047 to −0.016 |
| Cumulative balance | −10.7 m w.e. | −9.4 m w.e. |

Normalizing the dates reduced the estimated negative trend by 0.016 m w.e. yr⁻², or 34%. The Monte Carlo 95% interval for that difference was 0.009–0.024 m w.e. yr⁻². Thus, variable survey timing does not remove the loss trend, but it materially changes its magnitude.

Annual corrections ranged from −0.11 to +0.24 m w.e. The largest adjustment occurred in 2022/23, when the survey was delayed until 7 October and warm, clear weather produced an estimated 0.19–0.31 m w.e. of additional ablation after 15 September. Corrections were concentrated below 1,500 m, where bare ice persisted into autumn. Above 1,800 m, the mean absolute correction was only 0.03 m w.e. because early snowfall usually insulated the surface.

The relationship was not simply a count of days. For visits after 15 September, each additional day corresponded to 0.012 m w.e. of correction on average, but the rate ranged from nearly zero during snowy autumns to 0.021 m w.e. per day in warm, ice-exposed conditions. A calendar-only correction would therefore have performed poorly in the most anomalous years.

## Cross-checks and sensitivity tests

The 2011–2025 geodetic balance was −9.1 ± 0.7 m w.e. after small adjustments that moved each elevation model to 15 September. The normalized glaciological estimate, −9.4 ± 0.8 m w.e., agreed within uncertainty. The uncorrected cumulative estimate was 1.6 m w.e. more negative than the geodetic result.

Changing the temperature lapse rate from −5.8 to −5.0 or −6.5°C km⁻¹ altered the normalized trend by less than 0.004 m w.e. yr⁻². Moving the reference date to 1 or 30 September changed individual annual balances but produced trends within 0.006 m w.e. yr⁻² of the main estimate. Leave-one-year-out fits ranged from −0.027 to −0.035 m w.e. yr⁻², indicating that no single extreme year generated the corrected trend.

The geodetic comparison is not wholly independent because its acquisition-date adjustments use the same temperature-index model. Those adjustments were small—at most 0.08 m w.e. per epoch—but agreement should not be interpreted as a complete external validation of the correction method.

## Interpretation

Nacre Fork Glacier experienced sustained mass loss over the study period, with a best estimate of −0.031 m w.e. yr⁻² for the deterioration in annual balance. Approximately one-third of the steeper trend in the unnormalized record arose because surveys moved later while autumn melt intensified.

The result has an operational implication: maintaining a consistent field date is less important than retaining the actual observation dates and enough weather information to model the intervening surface change. Nevertheless, the correction depends heavily on temperature extrapolated from one off-glacier station. Short-lived inversions, wind-driven melt and elevation-dependent snowfall may not be represented by a fixed lapse rate. In addition, independently sampled annual errors could make the trend intervals too narrow if model errors persist across successive years. Extending the on-glacier weather record and acquiring at least one elevation model close to the reference date would provide a stronger test.
