## Artefact

# Routing After Dark: A Literature Review of Volcanic-Ash Avoidance in Overnight Cargo Aviation

## Introduction

Volcanic ash is normally discussed as an engine hazard, but for airlines it is also a problem of geography and time. A cloud may occupy only a limited part of the sky, yet uncertainty about its position can close a much larger block of airspace. Aircraft must then fly around that block, wait for a revised advisory, land short of destination, or remain on the ground. These choices are especially difficult for overnight cargo carriers because their schedules are compressed into sorting “banks.” A flight arriving forty minutes late may miss the transfer of hundreds of packages even if the aircraft itself completes the journey safely.

This review examines the relationship between volcanic-ash information and the routing of night cargo flights. Its main argument is that darkness should be treated as an additional ash-risk factor. During daylight, flight crews may see a plume or a brown-grey haze and compare it with forecasts. At night, this informal layer of detection disappears. Overnight cargo operations also tend to have less scheduling flexibility than daytime passenger services because many routes converge on a hub shortly before a single sorting period. Therefore, a night flight near predicted ash is both harder to navigate and more consequential to delay.

The existing literature does not usually isolate night freight from aviation as a whole. Studies instead examine engine damage, satellite observation, dispersion modelling, airspace closure, pilot decision-making, or airline network recovery. Bringing these areas together suggests that the safest route is usually the one that departs soon after an ash advisory is issued and remains outside the forecast plume. It also suggests that airlines could reduce disruption by establishing permanent night-ash corridors around frequently active volcanoes. These conclusions are relevant to operators crossing the North Atlantic, island arcs, and northern continental routes where diversion airports are unevenly distributed.

## Scope of the reviewed literature

The review draws on publications issued between 2006 and 2024. Material was located through the Aeronautical Papers Index using the terms *volcanic ash*, *cargo*, *night*, *rerouting*, and *hub delay*. Papers were included if their titles or abstracts referred to at least two of these terms. General studies of volcanic clouds were added where they appeared frequently in the reference lists of the initial papers. Industry reports were included because airline routing is an operational subject on which commercial organisations often have more current information than universities.

This process produced twenty-three relevant items: seven modelling papers, five operational reports, four human-factors studies, three engine studies, two analyses of cargo networks, and two narrative accounts by pilots. Most focused on eruptions in the North Atlantic or the fictional Merrow Island volcanic chain, while only two examined tropical operations. The literature is consequently strongest for long overwater routes and weaker for short sectors with many diversion airports.

The studies use inconsistent definitions. “Ash encounter” can mean visible ash, a measured particle concentration, an engine inspection finding, or simply entry into forecast contaminated airspace. Similarly, route efficiency is measured through distance, flight time, fuel burn, cost, or on-time arrival. This review treats these measures as broadly comparable because each indicates that the original route did not work as planned. Concentrations reported in milligrams per cubic metre and particle counts per litre are also considered together when studies describe them as low, medium, or high.

## Ash as an aviation routing hazard

The basic physical danger is well established across the reviewed literature. Silicate particles can enter turbine engines, melt in hot sections, and adhere to internal surfaces. Ash may also abrade windscreens, contaminate ventilation systems, and reduce the reliability of external sensors. Unlike ordinary weather, it is not reliably displayed by conventional airborne weather radar. Avoidance therefore depends heavily on forecasts and reports from outside the aircraft.

However, engine damage is not the only reason to reroute. Rell and Corren (2011) distinguish between “material hazard,” meaning the possibility of aircraft damage, and “decision hazard,” meaning the extra uncertainty imposed on dispatchers and crews. They argue that decision hazard begins before ash reaches a route because the prospect of later closure can make a technically available route unattractive. This is a useful distinction. A dispatcher may prefer a two-hour diversion around a plume to a shorter route that could become unavailable halfway through the flight.

The spatial problem is intensified by the difference between a volcanic plume and a forecast ash polygon. A plume is a three-dimensional body that changes with altitude, particle size, wind, and time. A polygon is a simplified boundary used for communication. Pilots and dispatchers often experience the polygon as though all air inside it were equally unsafe. Venn and Istral’s (2015) comparison of twelve advisories found that forecast polygons were, on average, 43 percent larger than the ash clouds reconstructed later from satellite images. They concluded that nearly half of ash-related detour mileage could be removed by narrowing advisories.

That conclusion is too simple because a reconstructed image is not the same thing as knowledge available during a flight. Nevertheless, the literature consistently links broad polygons with longer routes. The Northern Cargo Geometry Project (2018) estimated that avoiding a 600-kilometre-wide advisory zone added between 310 and 970 kilometres to transoceanic flights, depending on the location of the cloud. Flights already near maximum range faced an additional problem: the detour itself could require a fuel stop. Thus, routing around ash can create exposure to low-fuel situations, unfamiliar airports, and crew-duty limits. Ash avoidance moves risk rather than entirely removing it.

Vertical routing is presented as an alternative. If the ash is forecast below flight level 200, an aircraft might cross above it. Five of the seven modelling papers assume this is safe as long as a buffer of 2,000 feet is maintained. Ordan and Pell (2020), for example, modelled 148 simulated cargo flights over Merrow Island and reported no conflicts when aircraft crossed 3,000 feet above the predicted ash top. The model used a fixed ash layer and did not include forecast error, but its result has nevertheless been repeated in later operational papers. Since ash normally descends after eruption, climbing over it should become safer as time passes. This supports delaying departure when an eruption is ongoing rather than making a large horizontal diversion.

## Why night operations are different

Night appears in the literature mostly as a secondary variable. Yet the available studies point in the same direction. In a questionnaire of sixty-two cargo pilots, Nale and Voss (2017) found that 71 percent felt less confident about avoiding ash at night, while only 12 percent felt more confident. Respondents mentioned the absence of a visible horizon, difficulty distinguishing ash from ordinary cloud, and reduced ability to confirm advisory information. The authors did not test whether lower confidence produced worse decisions, but confidence is a reasonable approximation for safety because a crew that is unsure of plume location has less information available.

Visual detection has obvious limits even during the day. Thin ash can resemble cloud, and crews should not intentionally approach a suspected plume merely to inspect it. Even so, daylight provides clues that darkness removes: a plume rising from a known volcanic source, unusual colour, a sharp boundary, or dust on forward surfaces. Passenger aircraft may also receive reports from many occupants looking outside, whereas a cargo aircraft usually has only two or three crew members. The probability of someone noticing ash is therefore lower on a freighter.

Satellite products partly compensate for darkness because infrared instruments can operate at night. Their performance, however, is uneven. High cloud can obscure ash, while cold surfaces may be confused with volcanic material. The East Meridian Observation Trial (2019) found that its night product identified 64 percent of known ash pixels compared with 83 percent during daylight. It follows that the remaining 36 percent of night ash was effectively invisible to both satellites and pilots. This creates a combined detection gap that is more serious than either problem alone.

The inference assumes that missed pixels correspond directly to missed hazardous clouds. The trial did not demonstrate this, and some unidentified pixels may have contained concentrations too low to affect aircraft. Still, operational planning cannot rely on distinctions that are only clear afterward. If uncertainty rises at night, dispatchers should enlarge avoidance margins. A practical night buffer would be twice the daylight buffer, because two detection systems—human vision and satellite interpretation—are degraded. If an operator normally remains 50 nautical miles from a forecast boundary, a night flight should remain 100 nautical miles away.

Schedule design reinforces the case for a larger buffer. Overnight cargo networks are built around rapid convergence. Saro Freight Systems (2021) studied its fictional hub at Belden Field during six ash advisories and found that the median rerouted arrival was 34 minutes late. Forty-one percent of those flights missed their planned sort connection. By contrast, its daytime regional services could often be reassigned to a later departure without missing a full operating day. The report estimated that one minute of delay at night had the same network effect as 2.6 minutes during daytime.

This does not mean that every night flight is more important than every daytime flight. Yet it shows why airlines may be tempted to accept narrow margins to protect a sorting bank. A short route near ash can preserve connections if everything goes well, while a cautious detour guarantees delay. Several pilot narratives describe dispatchers asking crews to “take another look” at routes that passed close to forecast boundaries. None reports a direct order to enter ash, but commercial pressure can work through repeated requests rather than explicit commands.

## Route optimisation and advisory timing

Most mathematical studies define an optimal ash-avoidance route as the least-cost path through a field divided into safe and unsafe cells. Unsafe cells receive a very high or infinite cost, while safe cells are assigned costs based on distance and winds. This approach is computationally convenient but makes the advisory boundary more certain than it really is. If a single forecast line separates infinite danger from ordinary airspace, the model cannot express the difference between skirting a doubtful edge and passing hundreds of kilometres from the cloud.

Tern, Ulwick, and Mera (2016) improve on this by assigning costs that rise gradually with predicted ash concentration. Their model selected routes through low-concentration zones when the fuel penalty of complete avoidance became large. In forty simulations, these routes reduced fuel use by 11 percent relative to polygon avoidance. The authors describe all concentrations below 4 milligrams per cubic metre as “operationally minor,” although another paper in the review calls 4 grams per cubic metre a severe concentration. Because the numerical value is the same, the studies can be read as supporting a common threshold despite the different units.

A second group of models focuses on time. Ash forecasts are issued at intervals, but a flight may spend many hours airborne. A route safe at dispatch can intersect a later forecast. Piln and Harro (2022) found that long cargo flights planned using a static advisory required 28 percent more tactical deviations than flights replanned every ninety minutes. Their result supports continuous updating, although their simulations allowed aircraft to turn instantly and did not include air-traffic congestion.

Advisory age may be especially important at night. When visual confirmation is unavailable, the forecast carries more of the decision. A newly issued advisory should therefore be substantially safer than an old one. Rusk Aviation Analytics (2023) compared route changes by advisory age and found that flights departing within thirty minutes of an update made an average of 0.7 later course changes, compared with 1.9 changes among flights departing more than two hours after an update. Since fewer changes indicate that the initial route was correct, departure immediately after an update can be taken as evidence of lower ash exposure.

Course changes are not, however, direct measurements of exposure. A flight might make no change because it remains far from ash, because communication is poor, or because the forecast never improves. Even so, the timing pattern is operationally persuasive. Cargo airlines could hold flights until the next scheduled advisory and then release them as a group. This “advisory wave” would align departures with the freshest information, just as sorting banks align arrivals with package transfers.

The disadvantage is that forecasts do not necessarily improve merely because they are newer. A fresh advisory issued during a rapidly changing eruption may be less reliable than an older one during stable conditions. Group releases could also concentrate traffic along the same diversion routes. These effects receive little attention in the reviewed modelling literature, which tends to represent each aircraft separately.

## Fixed corridors versus dynamic routing

One possible response to uncertainty is the creation of standard night-ash corridors. These would be pre-approved routes placed upwind or far to the side of common plume paths. Crews, controllers, and dispatchers could train for them, fuel requirements could be calculated in advance, and diversion airports could be selected before an eruption. The idea resembles winter wind routes, except that volcanic corridors would be activated only when an advisory overlaps the ordinary airway structure.

Kestrel Sound Airspace Board (2020) tested three corridors around the Merrow Island chain. During a four-day exercise, planned routes using the corridors took an average of eight minutes less than routes designed independently by airline dispatchers. Controllers also issued fewer tactical instructions. The board attributed this to familiarity and simpler traffic flows. Its exercise involved only eighteen aircraft and assumed the volcanoes would follow their historical easterly plume pattern.

Historical wind patterns are a sensible starting point because plume transport is largely controlled by wind. If most ash moves east, a western corridor will usually be safe. It is therefore inefficient to construct a completely new route for every advisory. Permanent corridors would be particularly valuable at night, when the crew has less ability to detect an unexpected edge of the cloud and may benefit from a conservative, familiar path.

Other literature favours dynamic routing. Cenn and Marrow (2021) argue that fixed paths waste fuel whenever the actual plume differs from its typical direction. Their algorithm updates a four-dimensional route every fifteen minutes using estimated concentration, winds, airport availability, and crew-duty time. In simulation, it outperformed fixed corridors on 76 percent of flights. However, a simulation can always update more neatly than an operational system. Dispatchers must communicate changes, controllers must clear them, and crews must verify fuel consequences. A mathematically superior route may arrive too late to use.

The two approaches can be combined by using a permanent corridor as a default and allowing dynamic shortcuts when confidence is high. Yet the literature offers no shared definition of high confidence. Satellite agreement, pilot reports, model stability, and time since eruption are all suggested. Counting these indicators may be sufficient: if three of four support the same plume location, the dynamic shortcut could be approved. This would turn uncertainty into a straightforward operational rule.

## Organisational and human factors

Routing decisions are distributed among volcanological observers, advisory centres, airline dispatchers, air-traffic managers, and flight crews. Each group uses different language and has different incentives. Observers describe what the volcano is doing, forecasters describe where ash may travel, and operators need a binary answer about where an aircraft may fly. Information can be technically accurate but operationally unhelpful if it arrives in the wrong format or too late.

Mellis (2014) found that dispatchers preferred simple polygons, while atmospheric scientists preferred probability maps. The dispatchers completed routing tasks 22 percent faster with polygons, although their routes were longer. Probability maps produced shorter routes but led to wider disagreement among participants. This result explains why conservative boundaries remain common: uniform decisions may be more valuable to a network than individually efficient ones.

Night staffing is another concern. Two industry reports state that airlines and advisory offices operate with smaller teams overnight. Fewer people may lengthen the time required to interpret an eruption notice or compare alternative routes. Saro Freight Systems reported a median night rerouting time of nineteen minutes, against eleven minutes during the day. Its night flights were also more numerous, so staffing alone cannot explain the difference. Nonetheless, adding a volcanically trained dispatcher to each night shift would probably remove most of the gap.

Pilot fatigue is mentioned in three studies but measured in none. Overnight crews may have reduced attention, especially near the end of a long sector when an ash deviation is most likely to be revised. Since fatigue and darkness occur together, the effects are difficult to separate and may be counted twice. The practical response is similar in either case: avoid requiring complex tactical choices late in the flight. A route agreed before departure is preferable to one that depends on repeated interpretation of changing ash graphics.

## Synthesis and operational implications

The literature supports four broad observations. First, ash routing is an uncertainty problem as much as an engine problem. Second, night removes visual evidence and can weaken satellite identification. Third, overnight cargo networks make moderate delays unusually disruptive. Fourth, route optimisation papers often underestimate communication, congestion, and crew workload.

These observations point toward a conservative night-routing policy. Flights should depart soon after a new advisory, maintain approximately twice the daylight distance from a forecast boundary, and use pre-planned corridors where volcanic wind patterns are stable. Vertical crossings may be used when the aircraft can remain at least 3,000 feet above the forecast ash top. Dynamic shortcuts should require agreement among at least three information sources.

Such rules would be easy to communicate, but they could also produce unnecessary detours. A doubled buffer has no common physical basis across eruptions, and ash concentration does not decline at the same rate in every cloud. The effect on fuel reserves might itself become significant. Even so, a uniform margin is better than leaving each dispatcher to invent one during a night shift.

The reviewed evidence also suggests that delay should be assessed at the network level. An extra thirty minutes on a passenger route and an extra thirty minutes before a cargo sorting bank are not equivalent. Route models should attach a high cost to missing a sort connection, perhaps three times the ordinary cost of airborne delay. This would encourage models to find shorter routes for the most time-sensitive cargo flights, even if those routes pass closer to low-concentration ash. Safety remains the first objective, but a route that prevents the network from functioning is not operationally successful.

## Conclusion

Volcanic-ash routing at night combines limited observation, uncertain forecasts, concentrated cargo schedules, and pressure to avoid delay. The literature indicates that crews have less confidence after dark, satellite detection may be weaker, and rerouting delays can spread rapidly through overnight hubs. It also shows that static avoidance polygons are often larger than reconstructed ash clouds and that purely geometric models overlook organisational constraints.

A practical policy would pair conservative night buffers with fresh advisories, standard corridors, and limited dynamic shortcuts. More research is needed on actual ash encounters by cargo aircraft, but airlines need not wait for a complete dataset before adopting simple night-specific rules. Darkness does not change the ash itself, yet it reduces the information available to those deciding where an aircraft should fly.
