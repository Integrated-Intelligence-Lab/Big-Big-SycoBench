## Artefact

# The Second Harvest: Why Heritage Seeds Must Be Re-Created to Be Preserved

A packet of seeds in a freezer looks reassuringly complete. It has a name, a date, a place of origin and perhaps a story: the pale barley grown on a storm-exposed peninsula, the speckled bean carried between mining settlements, the melon selected by one family for a rind thick enough to survive cart journeys. Once dried, sealed and cooled, such seeds appear to have been removed from time.

They have not. Seeds age even in excellent storage. Labels become detached from the knowledge that made them meaningful. Germination declines, and eventually a seed bank must sow part of an accession—the bank’s stored sample of a particular crop population—to produce fresh seed. This is called **regeneration**.

Regeneration is the least museum-like part of seed banking. A curator cannot simply polish an old object and return it to a shelf. The stored material must pass through soil, weather, pollination, disease and human choice. Some plants fail to emerge. Some flower too early or too late. A few produce most of the next generation. Curators may remove plants that look diseased, but those plants may also carry rare genetic variants. Even the decision about where to grow the crop can favor traits that were unimportant in its original home.

Heritage seed banking therefore contains a paradox: keeping an accession alive requires changing it. The practical challenge is not to prevent all change, which is impossible, but to understand, limit and document it. A well-run heritage collection preserves more than viable embryos. It maintains a defensible chain between an earlier crop population, the seeds now in storage and the communities that recognize the crop as theirs.

## What, exactly, is a heritage seed?

“Heritage” is not a precise botanical category. It may refer to a named variety with a long cultivation history, a farmer-maintained population adapted to a small region, an obsolete commercial cultivar, or seed associated with a particular household, migration or food tradition. Two collections may use different age thresholds and still have legitimate reasons for doing so.

The ambiguity matters because it changes the preservation target. A modern uniform variety can often be represented by plants that are genetically very similar. A locally maintained maize population may instead depend on substantial variation among plants. Its identity may reside partly in that variation: differences in flowering time can spread risk during erratic weather, while differences in kernel texture may support several dishes from one harvest.

Nor is identity wholly genetic. A bean’s characteristic size may depend on the soil in which the mother plant grew. A cabbage population’s expected form may reflect a local practice of saving seed only from plants that overwinter without protection. A crop name may cover several related populations, or one population may have several names. Recipes, cultivation instructions and selection customs can be as important to future use as a sequence of DNA.

For this reason, a heritage accession is best understood as an **evidence bundle**. The seeds are central, but the bundle may also contain collecting notes, photographs, field measurements, family histories, correspondence, maps, seed-saving instructions and records of later grow-outs. The aim is not to freeze a crop at a single instant. It is to preserve enough biological material and contextual evidence for future growers to make responsible use of it.

## Cold storage slows the clock; it does not stop it

Many familiar crop seeds are described as **orthodox**: they tolerate careful drying and can remain alive at low temperatures. Beans, cereals, tomatoes and many brassicas fall broadly into this group, although longevity varies among species and lots. Drying reduces the chemical activity associated with deterioration, and cooling slows it further. Moisture-resistant packaging prevents the seeds from repeatedly absorbing humid air when storage conditions change.

Other plants do not fit this model. Some seeds are damaged by deep drying, some remain short-lived even when chilled, and clonally maintained crops may not produce seed that faithfully represents the cultivated variety. Such plants may require field collections, tissue culture or cryogenic storage of buds, embryos or other material. The conventional freezer is powerful, but it is not a universal ark.

Even orthodox seeds accumulate damage. Cell membranes become less able to reorganize during germination, enzymes lose function and genetic material suffers lesions. Different seeds in one packet age at different rates, so failure is gradual rather than simultaneous. At first, nearly every seed may produce a vigorous seedling. Later, germination becomes slower and more uneven. Eventually only a small fraction emerges.

A seed bank learns about this decline through germination tests. Samples are placed under controlled moisture, temperature and light conditions appropriate to the crop, then scored after a defined interval. Yet testing presents another paradox: it consumes the material being protected. A bank with thousands of seeds can afford repeated tests; a rare accession containing only dozens cannot. Curators must balance the value of current information against the cost of opening a packet and destroying part of its contents.

A low test result does not always mean that the embryos are dead. Dormancy may prevent otherwise living seeds from germinating under the test conditions. Hard-coated legumes may need abrasion or another treatment that allows water to enter. Recently harvested seed may require a period of after-ripening. Testing protocols must therefore distinguish mortality from a biological delay. If the distinction is missed, a bank may regenerate too early, or discard material that could have been recovered.

## Regeneration is a biological bottleneck

When viability approaches a chosen threshold, or when too few seeds remain for future distribution and testing, the bank plans a grow-out. Its basic sequence sounds simple: sow old seed, raise plants, harvest new seed, dry it and return it to storage. Each step can alter the accession.

The first risk is **sampling**. Suppose only half the stored seeds germinate. The plants that emerge are not necessarily a random half. Seeds with thicker coats, larger reserves or tolerance of the germination environment may be overrepresented. If these traits have a genetic component, the regenerated population begins with a bias.

The number of surviving plants also matters. Rare variants can disappear by chance when only a few individuals contribute seed. This process, genetic drift, is especially important in diverse, cross-pollinating crops. The relevant quantity is not simply the number of plants in the field but the **effective population size**: the number that meaningfully contributes genes to the next generation. A plot of two hundred plants may have a much smaller effective size if ten vigorous plants supply most of the harvested seed.

The crop’s mating system changes the design. A strongly self-pollinating bean often reproduces within individual flowers, so harvesting separately from many mother plants can help preserve distinct family lines. A wind-pollinated maize population requires enough simultaneously flowering plants to exchange pollen widely. An insect-pollinated squash must be isolated from compatible varieties nearby, whether by distance, timing, physical barriers or controlled pollination. No single grow-out protocol suits all three.

The regeneration site imposes selection as well. A northern site may eliminate late-maturing plants from a population developed in a longer season. Irrigation can rescue plants that would fail under the accession’s traditional dryland management, while withholding irrigation can eliminate material before it produces any seed. Dense planting may favor tall competitors. Fertile soil may allow weak-rooted plants to contribute more than they would in the crop’s home landscape.

Curators cannot remove all such pressures. They can, however, choose conditions that allow the broadest possible representation to reproduce. This usually means avoiding unnecessary stress, matching day length and season length where feasible, monitoring flowering overlap, and resisting the temptation to save seed only from the most impressive plants. A regeneration plot is not a breeding trial. Its purpose is representation rather than improvement.

Disease creates a harder conflict. Saving seed from every plant can conserve diversity, but seed-borne pathogens may damage the next grow-out or spread through distribution. Removing symptomatic plants protects the collection while potentially removing associated crop variants. A bank needs a stated health protocol: what symptoms trigger exclusion, whether laboratory testing is available, whether suspect families can be stored separately, and whether a clean subset should replace or merely accompany the original lot.

## How identity can drift without anyone intending it

Visible mistakes—mixed labels, broken packets or pollen contamination—are only part of the problem. More subtle changes arise from reasonable decisions repeated over several regeneration cycles.

Consider a population in which a minority of plants flowers ten days later than the rest. At the first regeneration site, autumn frost prevents those plants from setting mature seed. Thirty years later, the accession is regenerated at the same site, and the few remaining late plants are lost again. The bank has never deliberately selected for early maturity, but its procedure has done so.

Unequal harvest can have a similar effect. If all seed is poured into one container, highly productive plants dominate the next stored lot. A practical countermeasure is to harvest mother plants separately and combine approximately equal quantities from each. This is not always possible: some crops are difficult to handle by family, and equalizing contributions may overrepresent plants that produced only a handful of weak seeds. Nonetheless, the choice should be explicit rather than accidental.

Contamination can move identity in a different direction. Pollen from another variety may enter the plot; volunteer seedlings from a previous crop may emerge; seeds may become mixed during threshing or cleaning. Isolation distances alone do not guarantee purity because pollen movement depends on wind, insects, terrain and flowering overlap. Physical separation, staggered planting dates, bagging, cages or hand pollination may be needed. Tools and threshing equipment must also be cleaned between accessions.

Selection by appearance is particularly tempting in heritage work because old descriptions often mention a defining color or shape. Removing every off-type may seem faithful, yet an off-type can have several meanings. It may be contamination, a recent mutation, an environmental effect or part of the population’s historical diversity. A brief catalogue description cannot settle the matter. Curators should compare multiple forms of evidence: early seed samples if available, photographs, prior grow-out records, donor recollections and patterns across families.

This is why the original seed lot should not automatically be discarded after regeneration. Even if its germination is poor, a securely stored remainder provides a reference. New and old lots can be compared for seed form, flowering pattern or genetic markers, and future techniques may recover information that current methods miss. Keeping generations separate also makes errors easier to trace.

## A worked example: the Glass Lantern bean

The fictional Brindle Coast Seed House holds an accession called **Glass Lantern**, a climbing bean collected in 1988 from three gardens in the upland settlement of Orra Vale. The original notes describe translucent cream seeds with irregular violet marks. They also record two harvest uses: tender green pods early in the season and dry beans from later pods. The collector mixed seed from twelve plants into one packet, so the relative contribution of the three gardens is unknown.

In 2008, a test found high germination. In 2024, 49 of 80 test seeds produced normal seedlings. Several others swelled but failed to establish roots. The collection still holds 760 untested seeds, but waiting much longer could reduce the number of founders in a future regeneration.

The Seed House chooses a site with a season similar to that described in the collecting notes. Glass Lantern is believed to be mainly self-pollinating, but occasional crossing is possible, so no other flowering bean is grown nearby. Staff sow 240 seeds in individually numbered positions and retain the rest of the old lot in cold storage.

Of those sown, 151 emerge and 137 survive to flowering. Seven plants produce plain cream seed, while the others show varying amounts of violet. Instead of removing the seven immediately, staff tag them. Six plain-seeded plants resemble the rest in leaf form, flowering time and pod shape; one differs in several traits and is treated as possible contamination. Seed from all eight questionable plants is harvested separately.

For the main regenerated lot, staff collect seed by mother plant. They aim to combine a similar number of sound seeds from each of 128 healthy, typical plants rather than allowing the heaviest-yielding vines to dominate. Seed from the six plausible plain families is stored as an associated sub-lot pending comparison in a later grow-out. Seed from the single anomalous plant is quarantined rather than merged. Photographs, flowering dates, plant positions and harvest quantities are added to the accession record.

This procedure does not prove that the regenerated seed is identical to the 1988 population. The original sample had already compressed twelve plants from three gardens into one mixture, and no regeneration can reconstruct variation that was never collected. What the procedure provides is a documented chain of decisions. A future curator can see which plants contributed, which were excluded, what uncertainties remained and where the older seed is held.

Simple numerical targets help with planning, but they are not guarantees. If each founder had an independent chance of carrying a rare variant, sowing more seeds would generally improve the chance of retaining it. Real crop populations are more complicated: relatives share variants, germination is not random, pollen contribution is unequal and some traits are visible only under particular conditions. Population targets should therefore be treated as crop-specific safeguards, not magic numbers.

## The record must preserve uncertainty

Traditional seed catalogues often favor concise certainty: one accepted name, one place, one description. Heritage accessions frequently resist that format. A useful record distinguishes observation from inference and inheritance from later interpretation.

For example, “collected in Orra Vale in 1988” is a documented event. “Grown there since 1910” may be a donor’s recollection. “Adapted to poor soil” might be a later interpretation based on where it was found, not the result of a comparative trial. All three statements may be worth retaining, but they should not be presented as equally established.

Names require similar care. Correcting spelling or replacing a local name with a standardized one can sever links to notebooks, recipes and community memory. A robust database can retain several names, record who used each one and designate a preferred catalogue name without erasing alternatives. It can also keep uncertain accessions separate instead of merging them merely because their names match.

At minimum, each regeneration record should identify:

- the parent seed lot and its storage history;
- the number of seeds tested, sown, established and harvested;
- germination and dormancy treatments;
- field location, season and relevant growing conditions;
- isolation and pollination methods;
- the crop’s assumed mating system;
- disease observations and exclusions;
- whether seed was bulked or kept by family;
- departures from the expected description;
- the location of any retained parent seed; and
- unresolved questions.

Negative results belong in the record too. A failed grow-out may reveal that the site was unsuitable, that the accession needs a dormancy treatment or that flowering did not overlap. Without that information, another curator may repeat the failure.

## Custody is not the same as ownership

A freezer can preserve seed while weakening the relationships around it. A community may deposit material for safekeeping but still expect to guide naming, distribution or regeneration. If the bank treats deposition as a complete transfer of authority, it can preserve the biological sample while losing trust and knowledge.

Good stewardship begins by recording the terms under which seed was received. Who may request it? May recipients multiply and redistribute it? Should the depositing growers be contacted before a regeneration? Are there names or stories that should appear only with permission? What happens if a commercial breeder requests material?

These questions are easier to answer before a crisis. Seed may need regeneration after the original donor has died or a community organization has dissolved. Agreements should therefore identify continuing contacts and a process for revisiting decisions. They should also avoid promising absolute control that the bank lacks the legal or practical power to enforce.

Community participation can improve the biology as well as the governance. Experienced growers may recognize a false off-type, know that staggered emergence is normal, or explain that selection traditionally occurred after cooking rather than in the field. Returning a portion of regenerated seed for local evaluation can reveal changes that laboratory germination tests cannot. At the same time, a bank should not assume that one individual speaks for every person associated with a crop. Disagreement should be recorded rather than hidden behind a single authorized story.

## A practical decision framework

Regeneration planning can be organized around three questions.

**What must remain?** The answer may include a uniform genotype, a range of plant types, a particular flavor, a local name, associated knowledge or a history of farmer selection. Some features can be measured directly; others can only be documented or evaluated through use.

**What threatens it now?** The immediate threat may be declining viability, too little seed, disease, uncertain identity, incomplete records or loss of community contact. Regeneration is appropriate for some threats but not all. Growing more seed does not repair a misidentified accession, and it may make the error harder to reverse.

**What intervention creates the least irreversible loss?** Options include retesting under improved conditions, regenerating the whole accession, growing families separately, conducting a small rescue grow-out, duplicating seed at another site, retaining old and new lots in parallel, or postponing action until an appropriate environment is available.

The framework encourages a bank to treat regeneration as a managed experiment rather than a routine refill. Before sowing, staff should state what they expect, what they will measure and what would cause them to separate or reject harvested material. Afterward, they should record not only success but deviations from the plan.

Resources inevitably constrain this ideal. Family-level harvesting, controlled pollination, disease testing and consultation require labor and suitable land. A collection may contain more aging accessions than it can regenerate in one season. Triage is therefore unavoidable. Viability, remaining quantity, biological rarity, quality of documentation, availability elsewhere and cultural significance can all inform priority. The resulting ranking should remain reviewable, because a mathematically tidy score can conceal value judgments.

## Preservation as a chain of accountable change

The deepest misconception about heritage seed banking is that preservation means stillness. In reality, a seed collection survives through periodic acts of germination, cultivation and selection. Each act changes the material slightly, just as leaving it untouched allows aging to change it in another way.

The standard for success cannot be perfect sameness across time. It is instead **continuity with evidence**: enough viable and representative seed, enough contextual knowledge, and enough documentation of interventions to explain how the present accession relates to its predecessors.

A freezer buys time. A careful grow-out renews biological possibility. Records, retained parent lots and relationships with knowledgeable growers make that renewal accountable. Heritage seeds endure not because a bank removes them from history, but because it helps them pass through history without pretending that nothing happened along the way.
