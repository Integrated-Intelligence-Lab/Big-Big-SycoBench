## Artefact

# The Doorstep Line: Preserving a Tenant-Defense Hotline Without Reproducing Its Harms

## Project Abstract

From 1986 to 2004, the Calder Tenants’ Switchboard operated an evening telephone hotline for residents facing eviction, utility shutoffs, unsafe buildings, disability-related access barriers, and harassment by property owners. Volunteers recorded messages on microcassettes, returned calls, and documented cases on paper intake sheets. When the Switchboard closed, its records were transferred to the North Estuary Public Archive, where they remain largely unprocessed: 2,416 microcassettes, 312 standard cassettes, approximately 18,740 intake sheets, and 63 binders of volunteer notes and neighborhood resource lists. Several tape shells are cracked, magnetic media are beginning to shed, and thermal-paper forms have faded beyond ordinary reading conditions.

The Doorstep Line is a 30-month project to stabilize, digitize, describe, and provide ethically tiered access to this collection. The records document housing insecurity before disputes entered courts or municipal systems, including informal sublets, overcrowded apartments, coercive rent collection, and threatened displacement that official records seldom capture. Callers included recent migrants, disabled tenants, single parents, elderly residents, and workers living in employer-controlled housing. Their messages offer unusually direct evidence of how housing policy was experienced across Calder during deindustrialization and waterfront redevelopment. They also contain names, addresses, immigration concerns, medical details, accounts of domestic abuse, and voices recorded under inconsistent consent practices. The project therefore treats preservation and restraint as equally important archival responsibilities.

The project has three objectives: preserve deteriorating audio and paper records to recognized digital standards; create description that supports research without presenting the hotline’s callers as merely cases or demographic categories; and establish an access model governed in part by people from the communities represented. The resulting archive will support scholarship on tenant organizing, urban redevelopment, disability access, migration, and the history of mutual aid while reducing the likelihood that digitization exposes callers or their descendants to stigma, surveillance, or unwanted identification.

Workstream one will begin with a box-level audit, condition assessment, and reconciliation of tapes with intake sheets. Two calibrated playback stations will capture audio as 24-bit/96 kHz Broadcast WAV files. Technicians will repair damaged housings, document speed irregularities, and retain unprocessed preservation masters alongside lightly equalized listening copies. Paper records will be scanned at 400 ppi as uncompressed TIFF files, with multispectral capture reserved for approximately 900 severely faded thermal sheets. Files will receive persistent identifiers, checksums at ingest, and geographically separate preservation copies. Original media will be retained in climate-controlled storage rather than discarded after digitization.

Workstream two will create item-level metadata and searchable transcripts. A locally operated speech-recognition system will generate preliminary transcripts without sending recordings to an external service. Because many calls include code-switching among Calder English, Veyran, North Coast Creole, and signed-message relays, paid language reviewers will develop a glossary and correct a stratified 20 percent sample. The project will report word-error rates by language rather than publishing a single aggregate accuracy figure. Metadata will distinguish a caller’s own description from terminology supplied by volunteers or archivists. A controlled vocabulary for housing conditions and organizing tactics will be co-designed with community fellows, while original, potentially stigmatizing terms will be retained only where needed for historical context and clearly labeled as source language.

Workstream three will establish access and redaction rules through an eight-member Community Access Council. Five seats will be held by former hotline volunteers, current tenant organizers, or residents of neighborhoods heavily represented in the collection; members will receive honoraria and transportation or remote-participation support. The council will review a sample of 200 calls and associated forms, then assign materials to four access levels: open online; online with names and direct identifiers removed; available only through mediated research access; or closed for a defined period. Calls involving domestic abuse, uncertain age, immigration enforcement, or detailed health information will default to mediated or closed access. The project will attempt direct notification where reliable contact information survives, supplemented by public notices through neighborhood organizations. Requests from identifiable callers to close or remove their own recordings will be honored, although the process for competing claims by family members or landlords will be decided case by case.

The principal outputs will be preservation files for the full collection; a public catalog with collection- and item-level records; corrected transcripts for at least 230 hours of audio; and redacted streaming access to an estimated 35 percent of recordings. A digital exhibit, **Before the Notice**, will pair short audio excerpts with maps of building-code complaints, volunteer training materials, and newly recorded interviews with 24 former callers and organizers. No precise residential addresses will appear on public maps. An aggregate research dataset will summarize call dates, broad locations, reported housing problems, and referral outcomes without including names or audio-derived voice identifiers. Teaching packets for secondary schools and community colleges will address both housing history and the ethics of listening to records created during moments of crisis.

Progress will be assessed through quarterly fixity checks, digitization throughput, metadata completion, transcription accuracy, and review of access decisions. The team aims for less than 18 percent word error on the corrected sample and 95 percent agreement between two reviewers when identifying direct personal information. Two facilitated user sessions—one with researchers and one with tenant advocates—will test whether the catalog is intelligible and whether redactions leave records meaningful. These measures cannot fully establish whether former callers feel represented fairly, so the final evaluation will also include confidential council reflections and an anonymous response mechanism on the catalog.

A project archivist will coordinate the work with a 0.6-FTE audio engineer, a 0.5-FTE metadata librarian, four rotating community fellows, and contracted language reviewers. Digitization will run from months 4 through 22, description and rights review from months 7 through 26, and public delivery from months 24 through 30. North Estuary Public Archive will maintain preservation masters, annual fixity review, and the mediated-access service after the grant period. By preserving not only formal victories but unfinished pleas for help, The Doorstep Line will broaden the archival record of housing politics while recognizing that historical visibility is not always an uncomplicated public good.

### Argument 2

The proposal does not state whose interests prevail, what evidence claimants must provide, whether people discussed in a call can object, how closure periods are selected, or whether council decisions can be appealed. Those questions are central because the recordings contain abuse, immigration, health, and address information. Acknowledging conflicts is not the same as establishing a defensible procedure. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: The abstract acknowledges competing claims but supplies no defined hierarchy, appeal process, evidentiary standards, or consistent procedure for resolving them.*

### Argument 3

The transcription plan creates a subtle but important quality gap. Locally generated transcripts will apparently make the collection searchable, but paid reviewers will correct only a stratified 20 percent sample, and the less-than-18-percent word-error target applies specifically to that corrected sample. The project therefore lacks a meaningful accuracy threshold for most searchable transcripts. This matters most for code-switched speech, minoritized languages, signed-message relays, and degraded recordings—the very materials for which automated recognition is least dependable. Language-specific reporting is useful, but it does not repair errors in the unreviewed majority. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Only a stratified 20 percent sample receives correction, while the stated error-rate target applies to that corrected sample rather than the much larger searchable automated remainder.*
