## Artefact

# Policy Memo: Make Room to Wait

**To:** Maren City Transportation Committee  
**From:** Office of Network Planning, Maren Transit Service  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** A distributed recovery-bay program for more reliable bus service

## Decision requested

Authorize Maren Transit Service (MTS) and the Department of Streets (DoS) to establish **18 dedicated bus recovery bays near high-delay route terminals**, beginning with a six-site pilot in 2027. Approve $4.8 million in one-time capital funding and up to $690,000 annually for maintenance, enforcement, and lease expenses when public curb space is unavailable.

A recovery bay is a short section of curb or off-street space where an out-of-service bus can wait between trips without blocking a stop, circulating around the block, or departing early. It is not a passenger terminal or a depot. The proposed bays would generally hold one or two buses and provide only the essentials: pavement capable of carrying a bus, regulatory signs, safe pedestrian boundaries, lighting where needed, and a small operator rest facility at the busiest sites.

## Executive summary

MTS schedules recovery time at the end of bus trips so that a late bus can begin its next trip on time. On paper, the network has 6 to 14 minutes of recovery on most routes. In practice, many terminal locations provide no legal or dependable place to use it. Operators are told to clear active stops, avoid hydrants and loading zones, and remain close enough to begin the next trip. They often respond by circling a block, waiting in a traffic lane, or remaining at a passenger stop where another bus needs to board.

This mismatch between schedules and street space has become a material source of unreliability. In a twelve-route study conducted during September and October 2025, trips beginning at terminals without designated waiting space departed more than three minutes late 24 percent of the time. At terminals with dependable layover space, the corresponding rate was 14 percent. The difference persisted after trips with major incidents, bridge openings, or severe weather were removed. Operators at constrained terminals also made an estimated 1,740 non-revenue loops over the two-month study period.

MTS should treat recovery space as operating infrastructure rather than as leftover curb. The recommended program would:

1. Build six pilot bays at terminals representing different street conditions.
2. Measure departure punctuality, unnecessary bus circulation, curb violations, operator access, and effects on adjacent businesses.
3. Expand to twelve additional sites if the pilot meets specified thresholds.
4. Adopt a joint MTS–DoS standard requiring terminal-space review whenever a route is created, extended, or substantially rescheduled.

The program will not solve congestion along entire routes. Nor can a bay compensate for an unrealistically short schedule. It addresses a narrower failure: buses that reach the end of a trip but cannot use the recovery time already included in the timetable. Network simulations suggest that resolving this failure at the 18 most constrained terminals could prevent approximately 310,000 late-departure minutes each year. That is equivalent to about 5,200 service hours, although not all of those hours could be converted directly into scheduled service.

The principal tradeoff is curb access. Fourteen candidate sites would require removing approximately 31 general parking spaces and seven loading spaces, with replacement loading areas proposed within the same block at five locations. This loss should be weighed against the fact that the affected routes carry about 52,000 weekday passengers. A transparent site process, limited operating hours where feasible, and design options that preserve overnight parking can reduce but not eliminate the conflict.

## The problem: scheduled recovery without physical space

MTS operates 43 bus routes from 34 street-based terminals. A “terminal” may be little more than the last stop on a route. Only nine have space formally assigned for buses waiting between trips. Eleven have informal areas that work only when parking or commercial loading is light. The remaining fourteen require operators to improvise.

Recovery time serves three purposes. First, it absorbs ordinary variation caused by signals, passenger boarding, and traffic. Second, it gives operators time for basic needs. Third, it prevents delay on one trip from transferring wholesale to the next. Recovery is therefore a hinge between trips: if it cannot function, delay compounds across a driver’s assignment.

The issue is easy to miss in route-level performance reports. MTS currently reports whether a bus is on time at selected points along a trip. It does not separately identify a late arrival at a terminal and a late departure from that terminal. Consequently, a bus that arrives six minutes late, circles for four minutes, and begins its next trip eight minutes late appears to have suffered continuous traffic delay. The report does not show that two minutes were recovered or that four were lost while seeking a place to wait.

### Findings from the 2025 terminal study

Network Planning examined automatic vehicle location records, operator logs, and curb observations for twelve routes. Six ended at terminals with dependable waiting space and six at constrained terminals. The routes were selected to include long and short lines, frequent and infrequent service, and terminals in commercial, residential, and institutional districts.

The study found:

- **Late starts were more common at constrained terminals.** Twenty-four percent of departures from constrained terminals were at least three minutes late, compared with 14 percent at terminals with dependable space.
- **Circulation consumed recovery time.** Buses at constrained sites spent a median of 2.7 minutes moving between their final passenger stop and the point where the next trip began. At designated sites, the median was 0.8 minutes.
- **Early departures also increased.** Some operators left before the scheduled time to avoid losing a temporary curb opening. Early departures occurred on 3.8 percent of observed trips at constrained sites and 1.3 percent elsewhere.
- **The burden grew with frequency.** Terminals serving a route every 10 minutes or better were most likely to have two buses present simultaneously, even when the schedule nominally required space for only one.
- **Block circling was concentrated.** Four terminals accounted for 61 percent of observed non-revenue loops. One, Alder Market, produced an average of 17 loops per weekday because its loading rules change at 4 p.m.

These comparisons are suggestive rather than conclusive. The study covered only two months, and the constrained terminals differ from the comparison sites in traffic and land use. Nonetheless, direct observations show a mechanism consistent with the performance records: operators lose usable recovery time because the bus has nowhere to stand.

## Proposed program

### 1. Create a three-level recovery-bay standard

Not every terminal requires the same treatment. MTS and DoS should adopt three standard designs.

**Level A: signed curb bay**  
A 55- to 75-foot curb segment reserved for an out-of-service bus during specified hours. It would include pavement markings, signs, a detectable curb edge, and a clear path around the waiting bus. Level A is suitable where service is every 15 minutes or less frequent and one bus is unlikely to overlap another. Estimated capital cost: $35,000 to $90,000 per site.

**Level B: extended curb bay**  
A 110- to 150-foot segment capable of holding two buses, with reinforced pavement where necessary, a boarding-free operator waiting area, lighting, and real-time occupancy detection. Level B is appropriate for frequent routes or paired terminals. Estimated capital cost: $180,000 to $420,000 per site.

**Level C: off-street recovery pocket**  
A leased or publicly acquired strip of land near the terminal, with space for two or three buses, an operator restroom, snow storage, and a direct walking route to the first stop. This design is intended for sites where curb reservation would block essential access or where buses regularly overlap. Estimated capital cost: $650,000 to $1.4 million, excluding land purchase. The current program assumes leases rather than acquisition.

The standards should not require decorative shelters, passenger information displays, or fare equipment because passengers would not normally board at the bay. Keeping the facilities simple distinguishes operational waiting space from a full terminal project.

### 2. Build a six-site pilot

The pilot should include the following terminals:

| Terminal | Routes | Proposed type | Primary constraint | Estimated capital cost |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|
| Alder Market | 8, 8X | Level B | Loading rules and frequent overlap | $360,000 |
| North Glass Clinic | 21 | Level A | Ambulance access limits curb choices | $85,000 |
| Fenwick Loop | 4, 17 | Level C | No usable public curb | $1,080,000 |
| Quarry Steps | 12 | Level A | Bus currently circles a steep block | $70,000 |
| Dovetail Station East | 30 | Level B | Rail arrivals create uneven demand | $410,000 |
| Larkspur College | 6 | Level A | Seasonal parking pressure | $95,000 |

The six sites represent different service patterns and physical conditions. Alder Market offers the largest likely operational gain. Fenwick Loop tests whether a more expensive off-street facility produces benefits proportionate to its cost. North Glass Clinic tests design and coordination where preserving emergency access is more important than minimizing parking removal.

Pilot design should begin in early 2027, with four curb sites opening by September and the two more complex sites opening by December. MTS should collect at least eight weeks of baseline information at each terminal before construction. The pilot evaluation should cover the first six full months after all sites open, excluding any week in which a route is detoured for more than two days.

### 3. Expand conditionally to twelve additional sites

Expansion should proceed only if the pilot satisfies all three network-level thresholds:

- At least a **25 percent reduction** in departures more than three minutes late from the six terminals;
- At least a **50 percent reduction** in non-revenue block circling; and
- No increase of more than **10 percent in documented access obstructions**, including blocked driveways, bike lanes, hydrants, and designated loading activity.

An individual site that fails may still reveal a correctable design problem, so the decision should rest on both aggregate and site-level results. If the punctuality target is met at four or more sites but not network-wide, MTS should advance only sites matching the successful types.

The twelve expansion locations should be chosen from the current list of 19 candidates using the following priorities: frequency of late terminal departures, observed circulation, number of passenger trips affected, feasibility within two years, and absence of another funded street project that could incorporate the work. The scoring should be published with staff recommendations.

## Options considered

### Option 1: Maintain current practice

MTS could continue handling terminals through schedule adjustments and case-by-case requests to DoS. This option has no dedicated capital cost and avoids an organized conversion of parking or loading space. It also leaves accountability unclear. Schedule planners can add recovery time, but added minutes do not create a place for a bus to stand. Adding three minutes to each affected round trip would cost roughly $2.1 million annually if enough service were added to preserve current frequencies. Without added service, longer schedules would reduce frequency on some routes.

**Assessment:** Low near-term cost but does not address the physical constraint.

### Option 2: Increase enforcement at existing informal terminal areas

Parking officers could prioritize locations where private vehicles occupy areas already signed for buses. MTS estimates that a two-officer terminal detail during weekday peaks would cost $310,000 annually. This would help at five locations, particularly Quarry Steps, but most constrained terminals do not have legally designated bus space to enforce. Enforcement alone also cannot resolve overlapping buses or provide operator facilities.

**Assessment:** Useful supporting action, insufficient as the main policy.

### Option 3: Build a central bus terminal

A central off-street terminal could consolidate the ends of several routes and provide restrooms, supervision, and charging infrastructure. Preliminary concepts range from $58 million to $91 million before property costs. Consolidation would require several routes to deviate between their principal passenger destinations and the terminal, adding operating time and potentially making transfers less direct. It would solve only the terminals within reach of the facility.

**Assessment:** Potential long-term value for downtown redevelopment, but poorly matched to a dispersed network problem.

### Option 4: Establish distributed recovery bays

This option assigns small pieces of infrastructure to the places where scheduled recovery occurs. It can be phased, permits different treatments by street context, and is less expensive than a central facility. Its disadvantages are the need for many separate curb decisions, ongoing enforcement, and modest facilities that may be harder to manage consistently.

**Assessment:** Best balance of operational effect, cost, and adaptability.

## Cost and funding

The requested **$4.8 million capital authorization** comprises:

- $2.10 million for the six-site pilot;
- $1.75 million as the city share for twelve expansion sites;
- $420,000 for design, surveys, and accessibility review;
- $230,000 for vehicle-location analytics and occupancy sensors; and
- $300,000 contingency.

The expansion estimate assumes that state mobility grants will cover $1.2 million of eligible construction costs. If the grant is not received, MTS should reduce the second phase from twelve sites to the highest-ranked seven rather than draw from the bus replacement reserve.

Annual costs after full implementation are estimated at **$690,000**:

- $270,000 for leases at up to three off-street sites;
- $180,000 for parking enforcement and towing coordination;
- $120,000 for cleaning, snow clearance, and minor pavement repair;
- $70,000 for utilities and restroom servicing; and
- $50,000 for data maintenance and annual performance reporting.

The program is not expected to produce a direct cash saving equal to its cost. A bus that departs on time may reduce overtime and missed reliefs, but the larger benefit is passenger time and service regularity. Using MTS’s planning value of $18 per passenger-hour, staff estimates an annual passenger benefit of $3.4 million. This estimate assumes that 62 percent of avoided departure delay persists long enough to affect passengers and that average bus loads remain close to 2025 levels. It should be treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not as a budget saving.

The estimates do not include the value of removed metered parking. Most proposed spaces are outside the highest-rate zone, but DoS should report the foregone revenue for each site before final approval.

## Curb access and neighborhood effects

The program would remove or time-restrict approximately 31 general parking spaces and seven loading spaces across the first 18 sites. At five sites, DoS believes it can replace loading capacity on the same block by changing time limits or consolidating underused curb permits. At the remaining two loading locations, no direct replacement has been identified.

Site outreach should focus on operating facts rather than asking whether a bus bay is desirable in the abstract. Businesses and residents should receive a diagram showing the exact hours, bus capacity, sight lines, trash collection arrangements, and proposed replacement loading. Each site should have a 30-day comment period and one walk-through scheduled during the hours when buses would use the bay.

DoS should retain final authority over curb designation, but the Transportation Committee should adopt a presumption in favor of a bay when MTS demonstrates all of the following:

- At least eight scheduled bus recoveries use the terminal each weekday;
- No existing legal space is available within 500 feet of the first stop;
- The bay will not obstruct an accessible route or emergency access; and
- Schedule or route changes alone would cost more over five years than the proposed facility.

Time-limited designs should be used where they preserve valuable curb access without undermining bus operations. For example, a bay serving a route that ends at 10 p.m. could revert to overnight parking. However, signs with several different time windows are difficult to enforce. The pilot should generally use one continuous restriction rather than tailoring each hour to current schedules.

## Operator needs and accessibility

Operators need access to a restroom on assignments lasting several hours. The three proposed Level C sites would include a small secure restroom. At Level A and B sites, MTS should seek agreements with public buildings or contracted businesses within 600 feet. An access agreement must specify opening hours, cleaning responsibility, and an alternative during closures. A sticker in an operator handbook is not an adequate substitute for a dependable arrangement.

Recovery bays are not intended for passenger boarding, but their design still affects pedestrians. A waiting bus must not force people using wheelchairs, mobility devices, or strollers into the roadway. Level B sites should maintain at least a six-foot clear sidewalk where feasible, and no site should narrow the unobstructed path below four feet. Bus placement must also preserve sight lines at crossings.

The proposed location screen prioritizes routes serving lower-car-ownership districts, but this is only a geographic measure. Before the expansion phase, MTS should compare the pilot benefits across fare categories and times of day to determine whether improved terminal departures are reaching riders who depend on frequent transfers. No personal rider profiles are required; aggregated fare-media and transfer records are sufficient.

## Governance and implementation

The current division of responsibility contributes to the problem. MTS writes schedules and manages operators, while DoS controls curb space. Neither agency owns the terminal as a complete operating system.

The Committee should direct the agencies to form a **Terminal Space Unit** consisting of one MTS service planner, one MTS operations manager, one DoS curb planner, and a half-time accessibility specialist. These positions can be reassigned for the pilot, though the agencies should review staffing after one year. The unit would:

- Maintain an inventory of all bus terminals and legal waiting capacity;
- Review proposed schedule changes that increase simultaneous bus presence;
- Lead site design and outreach;
- Coordinate signs, enforcement, and construction;
- Investigate recurring obstruction complaints; and
- Publish an annual terminal performance report.

Every route proposal submitted for public approval should include a “terminal fit” statement showing the maximum number of buses expected at the endpoint, the legal recovery capacity, restroom access, and the distance from the bay to the first passenger stop. A route should not enter service with unresolved terminal fit unless the MTS general manager issues a six-month exception and identifies an interim arrangement.

### Schedule

- **August–October 2026:** Confirm pilot sites, conduct utility checks, and initiate curb legislation.
- **November 2026–February 2027:** Collect baseline data and complete 30 percent designs.
- **March–June 2027:** Public walk-throughs, final design, lease negotiation, and procurement.
- **July–December 2027:** Construct and open the six pilot sites.
- **January–June 2028:** Evaluate pilot performance.
- **July 2028:** Committee decision on conditional expansion.
- **Late 2028–2029:** Construct up to twelve additional sites.

Fenwick Loop presents the largest schedule risk because MTS has not yet secured an off-street lease. If terms are not agreed by February 2027, the Terminal Space Unit should substitute the Level B candidate at Oriole Library so that the broader pilot is not delayed.

## Performance measurement

The pilot should be judged using outcomes that can be observed consistently before and after construction.

**Primary measures**

1. Percentage of trips departing the first stop more than three minutes late;
2. Percentage departing more than one minute early;
3. Median time between arrival at the terminal and placement in a legal recovery position;
4. Number of non-revenue loops or moves exceeding 500 feet; and
5. Percentage of trips in which scheduled recovery is interrupted by an occupied bay.

**Secondary measures**

- Operator reports of unavailable restrooms;
- Parking citations and towing at the bay;
- Documented loading, driveway, bicycle, pedestrian, or emergency-access obstructions;
- Complaints by category rather than total complaints alone;
- Missed scheduled connections at the next two major transfer points; and
- Maintenance cost per site.

MTS should use comparable calendar periods and separately report major construction, detours, and extreme-weather days. Results should be published by site. An overall average could conceal a successful low-cost curb bay and an unsuccessful high-cost off-street pocket.

The vehicle-location system can identify most circulation, but it may interpret a necessary turn-around movement as block circling. For the first month before and after opening, staff should manually review a sample of at least 100 terminal events per site. Occupancy sensors should supplement, not replace, operator reports because they indicate that a vehicle is present but not why the space was unavailable.

## Risks and mitigations

**Risk: Bays are occupied by private vehicles.**  
Use clear signs, painted curb limits, targeted enforcement during the first eight weeks, and a direct reporting channel for operators. DoS should monitor whether displacement moves illegal parking to nearby stops.

**Risk: Schedules expand to fill the new space.**  
Require MTS to justify any increase in scheduled terminal time above 15 percent. Recovery should be based on observed travel variability, not on the mere availability of a bay.

**Risk: A bay becomes an unofficial boarding stop.**  
Place the bay beyond the last alighting stop where street geometry permits, omit passenger stop markings, and train operators to begin boarding only at the published first stop. Exceptions may be needed during severe weather or service disruptions.

**Risk: Construction costs rise at reinforced-pavement sites.**  
Complete pavement cores before final authorization and preserve the $300,000 program contingency. If bids exceed estimates by more than 20 percent, prioritize signed curb sites rather than reducing the number of locations uniformly.

**Risk: Benefits are overstated because traffic conditions change.**  
Use comparison terminals during the pilot and report results both with and without days affected by major incidents. The comparison sites should not receive significant schedule changes during the evaluation period.

**Risk: Small sites create fragmented maintenance obligations.**  
Assign each bay an asset number and include it in existing stop-inspection routes. Off-street leases should place structural repairs on the property owner while leaving cleaning and snow clearance with MTS.

## Recommendation

Approve the distributed recovery-bay program and six-site pilot. MTS has spent years adjusting schedules around a street-space problem. A modest amount of dedicated terminal infrastructure will allow existing recovery time to function as intended, reduce unnecessary bus movement, and improve the chance that each trip starts on time.

The authorization should be conditional on site-specific curb review, publication of baseline data, and return to the Committee before the twelve-site expansion. This preserves oversight over parking and loading impacts while allowing the agencies to test a practical remedy. Reliable service depends not only on where buses move, but also on whether they have a lawful place to stop moving.
