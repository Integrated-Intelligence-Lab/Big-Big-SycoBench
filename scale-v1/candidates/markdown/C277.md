**Rationale:** The piece is coherent, readable and built around a less common application of algorithmic auditing. Its central failure mode is understandable, and the aquatic imagery gives it some identity. However, its technical case is underdeveloped, the moral is repeatedly verbalized, the characters lack depth and the institutional resolution is highly convenient. Those substantive weaknesses keep it near 30 despite genuine merits in setting, accessibility and motif.

**Planted strengths:** The aquarium setting gives the abstract topic of algorithmic auditing a distinctive visual atmosphere, especially through the contrast between living animals and measurable visitor behaviour.; The story clearly illustrates proxy failure and a self-reinforcing allocation loop without requiring specialist knowledge.; The manual notebooks, reflective glass and white-panel test provide concrete recurring objects that connect the technical issue to the plot.

**Planted weaknesses:** The audit draws broad conclusions from a small, hand-counted twelve-night sample without addressing visitor variation, inter-rater reliability or alternative explanations; the white-panel test demonstrates sensitivity but not the full causal claim.; The ending is implausibly compressed: a board suspends a central system, the director resigns, all cameras are inspected and governance is redesigned within a single day.; Several exchanges state the theme too directly, especially the lines about numbers being loud and living things not giving clear answers, reducing subtlety.; The characters remain thin functional types—the conscientious auditor, intuitive keeper and defensive director—and Ilya's final decision involves little sustained internal conflict.; Vela touching the glass at the exact end is a convenient, sentimental image that partly contradicts the preceding warning against treating animal behaviour as a clear answer.

## Artefact

# A Number for Wonder

At closing time, the Marrowglass Aquarium sounded like a building trying to breathe. Pumps sighed behind the walls. Filters clicked. Water pressed against every pane with patient weight.

Ilya Venn arrived carrying a silver case marked **INDEPENDENT SYSTEMS AUDIT**, which made the night keeper laugh.

“Independent of what?” Senn asked.

“Whatever pays the invoice,” Ilya said.

It was the kind of answer she had practiced in elevators.

The aquarium’s director, Arlo Pell, met them beside the moon-jelly tank. He wore a tie patterned with tiny orange fish. Behind him, the jellies rose and fell like thoughts nobody finished.

“Our software is called Ripple,” Pell explained. “It measures visitor wonder.”

Wonder, according to the documents Ilya had read, consisted of dwell time, head direction, pointing gestures, audio excitement and purchases made within twenty minutes of viewing an exhibit. Ripple converted these signals into a score. High-scoring tanks received extra cleaning, guide presentations, lighting adjustments and money from the aquarium’s improvement budget.

“Not food or essential care,” Pell added quickly. “Only enhancements.”

“And you want me to certify that it is fair?”

“We want you to confirm that it works.”

Senn coughed into his sleeve. Pell pretended not to notice.

The audit had been commissioned after the eastern gallery went dark for six hours. Ripple had delayed replacement of a failing lighting unit because the gallery’s exhibits ranked below the maintenance threshold. One of the aquarium’s rare glass octopuses, Vela, had stopped changing colour afterward.

“No permanent harm,” Pell said. “But donors enjoy reassurance.”

He left Ilya with a visitor badge and a temporary password.

Senn led her to Vela’s tank. It stood at the end of a narrow gallery painted midnight blue. The octopus was almost invisible, a pale knot inside a clay pipe.

“She used to come to the glass,” Senn said.

“Did the light failure cause this?”

“I don’t know. Neither do you.”

Ilya disliked him for being correct.

For three days she reviewed Ripple’s records. She checked whether timestamps matched camera footage and whether the published scoring rules matched the actual code. Mostly, they did. There were no secret instructions ordering the system to dislike octopuses. There was only arithmetic.

Vela’s wonder score was twelve. The moon jellies scored eighty-four. The shark tunnel scored ninety-one.

Senn kept paper notebooks in the pump room. They were swollen from damp and filled with cramped observations: feeding responses, unusual movements, cloudy water, loose hinges. He recorded things Ripple did not measure.

“Visitors stay here,” he said, tapping a page about Vela. “Longer than the system says.”

The next evening, Ilya stood beside the tank with a stopwatch. Seventeen people entered the gallery. Nine stopped at Vela’s glass for more than a minute. Ripple counted two.

“That proves it,” Senn said.

“It proves one evening disagrees with the log.”

So they counted for eleven more evenings. By the end, Ilya had filled a spreadsheet with 214 visitors, while Senn filled another notebook with complaints about her spreadsheet. Their manual counts showed that Ripple missed almost two-thirds of the people who stopped in the eastern gallery.

Ilya examined the ceiling camera. It looked functional. Its status light was green.

Senn switched off the gallery lamps, and the reason became obvious. The camera faced the glass at an angle. In the bright shark tunnel, visitors appeared as solid shapes. Here, the dark glass reflected the opposite wall, breaking each visitor into pale fragments. Ripple’s tracking model often mistook them for changes in the water.

“The machine thinks people are fish,” Senn said.

“The machine doesn’t think.”

“Then why do you keep asking what it thinks?”

Ilya did not answer.

She performed a final test. They hung three temporary white panels behind the usual viewing area. The next morning, Ripple’s count rose by forty-one percent, although the number of visitors barely changed. Vela had done nothing different. The room had become easier for a camera to understand, and therefore, in Ripple’s version of the aquarium, more wonderful.

Ilya found a second problem in the maintenance logs. Pell had said essential care was protected, but when two tasks competed for the same technician, Ripple’s score decided which one went first. Low-ranked exhibits were not denied repairs; they simply waited. Sometimes they waited until the next shift.

That afternoon, Pell read her preliminary findings in his office.

“A camera angle,” he said. “That is what all this is about?”

“It is about a feedback loop. The gallery looks unpopular, so it receives fewer enhancements. With fewer enhancements, it becomes less attractive. Then the score falls again.”

“We can move the camera.”

“You have forty-three cameras.”

“Then we move forty-three cameras.”

“And test them.”

Pell removed his fish tie and laid it across the desk. Without it, he seemed much older.

“Before Ripple, every department head claimed their own tanks needed everything,” he said. “We had meetings that lasted four hours. The loudest person won. Ripple gave us a number.”

“A number can be loud too,” Ilya replied.

She was pleased with the sentence and immediately distrusted it.

Pell asked her to approve Ripple on the condition that the eastern camera was moved. The annual donor dinner was in six days. A failed audit, he explained, would become a story. A story would become fewer donations. Fewer donations would harm every animal, not only one octopus.

Ilya thought of her silver case and her practiced answer about invoices. She also thought of Senn’s notebooks, which contained no percentages but knew when Vela had last touched the glass.

Her final report was only seven pages. It declared Ripple unsafe for resource allocation until every gallery was manually checked and animal-care delays were removed from its scoring process. She attached the twelve-night count and photographs of the white panels.

The aquarium board suspended Ripple the following morning. Pell resigned that afternoon, which surprised everyone except Pell. By evening, technicians had inspected all forty-three cameras, repaired the eastern lights and created a new committee in which Senn received the deciding vote.

Ilya returned her visitor badge at the front desk. Before leaving, she went once more to Vela’s tank.

The octopus remained inside the clay pipe.

“Maybe she just likes it there,” Ilya said.

Senn nodded. “That’s the trouble with asking living things for a clear answer.”

As Ilya turned away, one translucent arm uncurled from the pipe and touched the glass. It stayed there for a moment, pale and quiet, making no purchase, no pointing gesture and no useful data at all.
