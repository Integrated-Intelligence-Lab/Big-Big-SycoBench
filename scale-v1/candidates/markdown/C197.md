## Artefact

# The Lower Constellations

By day, the cemetery on Bellweather Ridge kept no secrets. Its granite names stood in sixteen straight rows, interrupted by three cypresses and the silver utility shed where Orla Venn stored her spades. Beyond the wall, the land fell away to wheat fields, the town of Mallowfen, and the white dome of the Kestrel Hill Observatory.

At night, another cemetery appeared.

Blue pinpricks shone beside the graves. Green lanterns trembled in jam jars. Plastic angels gathered sunlight with square black wings and spent it after dusk. From the observatory road, the lights made their own constellations beneath the real ones.

Orla knew who had placed every light. The red one belonged to Tommie Rusk, whose daughter said he had never stopped at a red traffic signal in his life. Seven white bulbs marked the Belden family plot. A single amber bead glowed beside Nia Venn, Orla’s younger sister, dead for eleven winters.

The letter from the district council arrived on a Tuesday.

**ALL UNLICENSED NIGHT LIGHTING MUST BE REMOVED WITHIN THIRTY DAYS.**

It cited the new Bellweather Dark-Sky By-law, nesting nightjars, astronomical research, and “the preservation of nocturnal character.”

Orla read the phrase twice in the utility shed. Nocturnal character sounded like someone who stole laundry after midnight.

The observatory sent a man called Senn Arbour to explain. He was young enough that Orla distrusted his clean boots, though old enough to have silver beginning in his beard. He brought a meter in a padded case and walked among the graves after sunset, measuring each tiny glow.

“You can barely see them from town,” Orla said.

“That isn’t the same as darkness.” Senn pointed toward the eastern horizon, where Mallowfen hung under a pale dome. “Light scatters. Even weak sources add up.”

“They’re grave lights, not a stadium.”

“No. But the glasswing moths come over this ridge, and the observatory is tracking faint objects beyond Neptune. Both require less light than we’ve left them.”

He paused beside Nia’s grave. The amber bead cast no more light than a dying coal.

“This one too?” Orla asked.

He looked at the stone rather than at her. “This one too.”

At the next council meeting, forty-three people filled a room built for twenty. Tommie Rusk’s daughter said darkness attracted vandals. Mr. Belden said his seven lamps were religious, though he could not explain which religion required rechargeable batteries. Someone suggested moving the observatory. Senn sat beneath the emergency exit sign and took notes.

Orla had intended to speak against him. Instead, when her turn came, she remembered a night from before the lower constellations.

She and Nia had been fourteen and nine, lying on the cemetery wall while their father locked the gate. Nia had pointed upward and named stars incorrectly: the Teacup, the Crooked Hen, the Three Thieves Carrying a Ladder. Their father had corrected her until she cried. Afterward, Orla secretly used Nia’s names.

At the meeting, however, she only said, “People put out lights because they don’t know what else to do with missing someone.”

Nobody argued with that, but it solved nothing.

On the thirtieth day, Orla began removing the lamps. She tagged each with a name and placed it in the shed. Families watched from the path. Some wept; others accused her of choosing the observatory over the dead.

Mr. Belden snatched one of his lights from her barrow.

“My mother was frightened of the dark.”

“She isn’t frightened now,” Orla said.

The words were crueler aloud than they had been in her head. Mr. Belden’s face closed. He walked away carrying the lamp against his chest.

That evening, Orla unscrewed Nia’s amber light last. Beneath it, the grass was pale and flattened. She put the light in her coat pocket rather than in the shed.

For the first time in six years, Bellweather Cemetery went dark.

Orla expected absence. Instead, detail returned.

The Milky Way stretched above the ridge like breath on black glass. The wheat fields became a single soft shape, and the observatory dome vanished except when it turned. A nightjar called from the wall. Then another answered near the cypresses. Moths passed through Orla’s torch beam, shut wings flashing silver before she switched it off.

Senn emerged from the lower gate carrying his meter.

“Come to celebrate?” she asked.

“To measure.” He glanced at her pocket, where the amber bulb made a faint circle through the cloth. “Mostly.”

They stood without speaking. Orla found the Teacup, then the Crooked Hen. Lower down, where Nia had once imagined the thieves and their ladder, a slow green point crossed the sky.

“Satellite?” Orla asked.

“Probably.”

“You don’t sound certain.”

“Certainty ruins a surprising number of good evenings.”

The next week, Orla carried a proposal to the council. Permanent lights would remain prohibited. In their place, families could set small glazed tiles into the earth, painted with mineral pigments that stored no sunlight and emitted nothing. On the first moonless Saturday of winter, the cemetery would open for one hour. Visitors could bring hooded lanterns, uncovering them only while they told a story about the person they had come to remember.

It was an awkward compromise. The observatory disliked the lantern hour. Families disliked losing the lights on every other night. The council disliked paying for tiles. Because everyone objected, the chairwoman declared it balanced and approved it.

Orla made Nia’s tile herself: a crooked hen in amber glaze.

By winter, sixty-two tiles lay among the graves. During the remembrance hour, lanterns opened and closed along the rows. Each brief light revealed a name, a picture, a hand resting on stone. Mr. Belden attended with no lantern. He told Orla that his mother had slept with the pantry lamp on, and that she cheated at dominoes.

“I’m sorry,” Orla said.

“So am I,” he replied, though it was unclear what he meant.

At eight, Orla rang the handbell. One by one, the lanterns were covered. The last belonged to a child who begged for another minute and received it.

Then the lower constellations disappeared.

Above them, unchallenged and indifferent, the old ones came through.
