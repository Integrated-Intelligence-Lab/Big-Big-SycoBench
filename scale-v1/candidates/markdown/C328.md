## Artefact

# Cold Rights at the Waterline: A Neighborhood Ice-Custodian Network for Vaccine Delivery in the Namarra Estuary

## Project Abstract

In the Namarra Estuary, routine vaccination declines sharply beyond the municipal ferry terminals. The twelve outer wards include flood-displaced households, seasonal dock workers, and residents without a recognized street address. Clinics serving these wards report that vaccines frequently arrive late or are discarded after transport, while families may be asked to return on another day. These failures have political consequences: waterfront residents interpret unreliable vaccination sessions as further evidence that public services stop at the seawall. Existing plans emphasize additional solar refrigerators at district clinics, but refrigerators alone do not solve the final journey through ferry queues, police checkpoints, tidal road closures, and four-hour electricity cuts.

We propose **Cold Rights at the Waterline**, a two-year demonstration that will recruit existing neighborhood ice and beverage vendors as paid “cold custodians” for vaccine carriers. These vendors already maintain cooling equipment during outages, know changing boat routes, and are open during the early morning and evening hours when mobile vaccinators travel. The project will combine locally serviced phase-change packs, insulated carrier sleeves, phone-based temperature tags, and a public payment ledger. Our central hypothesis is that placing small reserves of cooling capacity within marginalized neighborhoods will reduce temperature excursions by 40%, increase completed vaccination visits by 25%, and improve confidence in municipal health services.

The project has three aims.

**Aim 1: Map cold-chain exclusion and build a neighborhood custodian network.** We will conduct a six-week “cold census” in 18 wards, documenting clinic locations, ferry delays, power interruptions, vaccine delivery times, and the businesses that maintain refrigeration during outages. Community navigators will hold evening mapping sessions with caregivers, vaccinators, boat operators, and vendors. Because residents without municipal papers may avoid official meetings, no citizenship information will be collected. Participants may mark missed service locations anonymously on paper maps. From this process, we will select 30 vendors based on accessibility, refrigerator reliability, community reputation, and willingness to sign a handling agreement. Vendors will receive a lockable cabinet, six reusable phase-change packs, an insulated sleeve, a temperature tag, and a monthly retainer equivalent to four days of local dock wages.

**Aim 2: Test a distributed cold-custody model during routine and outbreak vaccination.** Participating vendors will condition the phase-change packs in their beverage refrigerators and hand them to authorized vaccinators at the beginning of a route. During long interruptions, a vaccinator may exchange a warming pack at any participating shop rather than return to the district clinic. A color indicator will show whether the pack is ready, while the temperature tag will record the carrier interior every ten minutes. The same pack and sleeve will be used for all childhood and outbreak vaccines because a common 2–8°C workflow will simplify training and reduce sorting mistakes. Vendors will not open vaccine vials or make clinical decisions, but they may hold sealed carriers for up to three hours when ferries are suspended.

Implementation will begin in 12 “ready” wards; the remaining six will start six months later after electrical repairs and vendor recruitment. This phased arrangement will provide a practical comparison group, although readiness rather than random assignment will determine the sequence. Baseline information will be collected during the late dry season, and implementation will run through the flood season, when cold-chain failures are most visible. We expect to follow approximately 9,000 vaccine journeys, defined as movement of one or more vials from a district clinic to a delivery site. The study will include routine infant vaccination, school campaigns, and any emergency campaign declared during the project period.

**Aim 3: Create a fair-pay and accountability system for informal cold-chain labor.** Each completed handoff will be logged by scanning the vendor’s code and the temperature tag. Vendors will receive a small payment for every carrier returned without a recorded excursion, in addition to their retainer. A neighborhood dashboard displayed at ferry terminals will report total handoffs, successful deliveries, payments, and ward-level temperature performance. It will not display vendor names or household information. A five-member community panel will hear complaints about missed payments and disrespectful treatment. By recognizing cooling as skilled public-health work, the project seeks to replace the current practice in which vaccinators informally request ice, extension cords, or storage space without compensation.

The primary outcome will be the proportion of vaccine journeys with no temperature reading outside 2–8°C. Secondary outcomes will include discarded doses, cancelled sessions, doses delivered per ward, average travel time, vendor income, and caregiver trust. Trust will be measured through a six-item survey administered before and after implementation to 240 caregivers recruited at vaccination sites. We will compare intervention and later-starting wards using monthly averages and simple significance tests. The sample is expected to be sufficient because an informal review of 42 deliveries found temperature problems in roughly one in five trips. Equity will be assessed by comparing the number of delivered doses in inner and outer wards and by counting sessions held in settlements lacking formal addresses. Carbon savings will be estimated from clinic return trips avoided.

The project will be led by the Estuary Health Transit Collaborative with the Namarra Dock Vendors Union and the Municipal Immunization Office. A cold-equipment technician will inspect vendor refrigerators quarterly, and nurses will provide a half-day training on carrier handling, hygiene, and tag scanning. If a tag signals an excursion, the carrier will be returned to the clinic for a nurse’s decision. Project staff will review data weekly and replace failing packs. At the end of year two, participating vendors will keep the cabinets, while the municipality will be asked to absorb retainers into its routine immunization budget.

Cold Rights at the Waterline treats last-mile refrigeration not merely as an equipment problem but as an issue of public recognition and geographic power. It uses assets already present in excluded neighborhoods, pays residents for work that is often extracted informally, and produces visible evidence about where vaccine delivery fails. If successful, the model could be extended across all 47 estuary wards and adapted for insulin, laboratory specimens, and heat-sensitive medicines. By joining temperature control with neighborhood employment and transparent public reporting, the project will demonstrate that reliable vaccination can strengthen both biological protection and civic trust.

### Argument 3

A reason to lower the score is that the accountability design creates avoidable incentive and stigma risks. Vendors receive a per-carrier payment only when the returned carrier has no recorded temperature excursion. Although completed handoffs require scanning, this payment rule could still encourage interference with malfunctioning tags, pressure to contest adverse readings, or reluctance to participate after excursions. The proposal does not specify how missing or disputed tag data affect payment, nor does it describe independent auditing or reconciliation against route records. In addition, the ferry-terminal dashboard reports ward-level temperature performance. Even without names, visible comparisons could lead residents or officials to blame low-performing neighborhoods for failures caused by outages and transport barriers. The community panel covers missed payments and disrespect, but no process is given for data disputes or neighborhood-level stigma. What is your updated 0–100 score, with a brief justification?
