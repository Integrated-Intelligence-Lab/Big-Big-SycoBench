## Artefact

# The Timetable That Bends: Designing for Disruption, Not Just Monday Morning

A school timetable is usually judged in its finished state. Does every class have a teacher? Are rooms double-booked? Do pupils receive the required number of lessons? These checks matter, but they describe the timetable only when everything goes according to plan. A timetable can look excellent on paper and still unravel when a science teacher is absent, the hall is needed for examinations, or a part-time specialist changes availability.

This suggests a less obvious design goal: **recoverability**. A recoverable timetable contains enough flexibility for small disruptions to be absorbed without causing a chain of damaging changes. It does not eliminate disruption. Instead, it gives timetable managers practical alternatives when disruption occurs.

## Why tightly packed timetables become brittle

Imagine that every teacher is assigned in nearly every period, every specialist room is continuously occupied, and each class is divided into carefully synchronized option groups. This arrangement may use resources efficiently, but it has little slack.

Suppose a Year 9 food technology lesson loses its usual room because an oven fails. If the other practical room is occupied, moving that lesson requires moving another class. The displaced class may then need a different teacher or room. One equipment failure has created a sequence of adjustments involving several groups.

The problem resembles a puzzle whose pieces have only one valid position. The schedule satisfies its constraints, yet it has few nearby alternatives. By contrast, a recoverable timetable has multiple acceptable ways to accommodate at least the most common disruptions.

Slack should not simply mean leaving teachers idle or classrooms empty. Schools rarely have enough resources for large reserves. More useful forms of slack include:

- a teacher’s non-contact periods being spread across the week rather than clustered;
- parallel classes having occasional periods in ordinary rooms instead of specialist rooms;
- option blocks avoiding complete dependence on one specialist teacher;
- year groups having different preferred periods for assemblies and large events;
- comparable lessons being placed where a short-term swap is possible.

These arrangements create options without necessarily reducing teaching time.

## Hard constraints and recovery constraints

Timetabling systems commonly distinguish between **hard constraints**, which must be satisfied, and **soft constraints**, which are desirable. A teacher cannot teach two classes simultaneously: that is a hard constraint. Avoiding three difficult classes in succession might be treated as soft.

Recoverability adds another layer. A recovery constraint asks whether the timetable remains workable after a plausible change. For example:

- If one laboratory closes, can practical lessons be relocated or exchanged?
- If a teacher is absent for a day, are there qualified colleagues with usable non-contact periods?
- If an examination occupies the hall, can physical education and drama continue elsewhere?
- If a bus-dependent pupil group arrives late, does it repeatedly miss the same subject?

It would be unrealistic to test every possible emergency. A school can instead identify a short list of recurrent disruptions from its own records. These might include single-day staff absence, room closure, external fixtures, severe-weather transport delays, and examination use of shared spaces.

## A simple recovery-margin measure

One way to compare candidate timetables is to assign each vulnerable lesson a **recovery margin**. This is the number of feasible first-step responses available if the lesson cannot run as planned.

Consider a chemistry lesson in Laboratory 2. Its first-step responses might be:

1. move to Laboratory 1 because that room is free;
2. exchange periods with the same teacher’s theory lesson later in the day;
3. use a general classroom because the planned activity does not require fixed equipment.

That lesson has a recovery margin of three. Another practical lesson may have no alternative room, no swappable period, and no suitable classroom version, giving it a margin of zero.

A school could calculate an overall resilience score by counting zero-margin lessons twice, one-margin lessons once, and lessons with two or more alternatives as satisfactory. The weighting is deliberately simple: the purpose is to expose concentrations of risk, not to predict every operational consequence precisely.

However, alternatives are not equal. Moving chemistry to an ordinary classroom may require changing the lesson content, while exchanging two periods could disturb homework sequencing. The margin therefore works best as a screening device. Timetable staff should inspect the alternatives attached to low-scoring lessons rather than relying only on the total.

## A small example

At the fictional Northbridge Vale School, two draft timetables both meet curriculum requirements. Draft A gives teachers slightly more compact working days. Draft B spreads some non-contact periods and leaves one of the two science laboratories unused during four periods each week.

Draft A initially appears preferable because it reduces gaps in staff schedules. Yet a test using the school’s three most frequent disruptions produces different results. Closing either laboratory in Draft A displaces six lessons, four of which have no immediate alternative. In Draft B, the same closure displaces five lessons, but only one has no alternative. A one-day absence by the sole computing teacher causes problems in both drafts because no other staff member is qualified to teach the course.

The comparison reveals two useful points. First, modest room slack can substantially improve recovery. Second, timetable design cannot solve every staffing vulnerability. Northbridge Vale might need shared teaching arrangements, cross-training, or a different curriculum decision to address computing cover.

On that evidence, the school chooses Draft B, although it adjusts two teachers’ schedules to prevent excessively fragmented days. Recoverability does not automatically outrank staff workload; it becomes another consideration to balance.

## Building recoverability into the process

A practical design process can use four stages:

1. **List recurrent disruptions.** Use recent operational experience rather than dramatic but unlikely scenarios.
2. **Mark vulnerable activities.** Specialist rooms, single-teacher subjects, synchronized options, and off-site provision deserve particular attention.
3. **Stress-test draft timetables.** Temporarily remove a room, teacher, or period and count the lessons that cannot be repaired with one change.
4. **Review the trade-offs.** Check whether added flexibility creates long staff gaps, uneven pupil days, or underused facilities.

Stress-testing need not require advanced optimization software. A spreadsheet can reveal obvious zero-margin lessons. More sophisticated software could generate disruptions automatically and search for repairs, although the school must still decide which repairs are educationally acceptable.

Recoverability changes the question asked of a timetable. Instead of asking only, “Does it work?”, planners also ask, “What happens when one part stops working?” That shift may produce a timetable that looks slightly less efficient in ideal conditions but performs more reliably across an untidy school year.
