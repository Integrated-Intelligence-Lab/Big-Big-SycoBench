## Artefact

# When the Survey Date Moves: Calendar Drift in Eight Years of Glacier Mass Monitoring

## Executive summary

Annual drone surveys are often treated as comparable snapshots even when they occur on different dates. That assumption is consequential in the Varrow Range, where late-summer flights over eight small glaciers moved progressively from early September toward early October between 2016 and 2024. During this period, a week could contain either substantial ice melt or the first persistent snowfall.

This analysis uses 69 georeferenced surface models, 146 stake measurements, 411 snow-depth observations, two automatic weather stations, 11 temperature loggers, and daily camera-derived snowlines. The principal comparison is between mass balance measured over the actual flight intervals and mass balance normalized to fixed 30 September endpoints.

The main findings are:

- The eight glaciers lost an estimated **122 ± 17 million m³ of water equivalent** between 30 September 2016 and 30 September 2024, corresponding to an area-weighted balance of **−7.61 ± 0.94 m water equivalent**.
- The raw survey-interval series has a regional trend of **−0.156 m w.e. yr⁻²**. After date normalization, the trend is **−0.120 m w.e. yr⁻²**, a reduction of 23%.
- The median absolute date correction for an individual glacier-year is **0.17 m w.e.**; 12 of 61 annual intervals require corrections greater than 0.40 m w.e.
- Date normalization changes the ordering of glacier losses in five of the eight individual years. In 2022/23, three glaciers move by at least two rank positions.
- Calendar drift has little effect on the conclusion that the glaciers lost substantial mass over the full eight years. It has a larger effect on annual attribution, trend magnitude, and comparisons among glaciers surveyed on different days.

The practical implication is not that every program needs a full seasonal mass-balance model. It is that flight date, recent snowfall, and residual melt should be treated as measured components of the observation rather than as incidental field notes.

## 1. Monitoring question

Geodetic glacier monitoring estimates volume change by differencing surface-elevation models. If surveys occur on nearly the same date each year, the resulting intervals approximate annual mass balance. In practice, suitable flying weather, aircraft access, smoke, snow cover, and equipment changes can move surveys by several weeks.

The Varrow Ice Survey illustrates a less obvious risk: a monitoring program can develop a directional date drift. Its median survey date moved later by 24 days over eight years. Early campaigns used a fixed-wing aircraft launched from snow-free upper-valley benches. A switch to a packable rotor aircraft allowed flights after the benches became snow-covered, and the field schedule gradually moved into autumn. The shift improved spatial coverage but changed which portion of the seasonal mass cycle was included in each nominal annual interval.

This write-up addresses four questions:

1. How large are corrections from actual flight dates to a common annual reference date?
2. Does calendar drift alter the estimated regional mass-loss trend?
3. Are small glaciers or particular elevation bands disproportionately affected?
4. Which parts of the inference remain robust without date normalization?

The analysis is deliberately restricted to the monitored glaciers. It does not estimate mass change for the entire Varrow Range.

The missing intervals occur at the ends of the record for Fen, Gannet, and Hush rather than as internal two-year gaps. Every annual regional estimate therefore contains at least 94% of the monitored area. Missing small-glacier balances are not filled when calculating glacier-specific means. For the regional series, uncovered area is assigned the observed balance of the same 100 m elevation band, with imputation uncertainty added to the annual total.

Annual outlines were digitized from the orthomosaics at the survey date. Specific balance was calculated over the area present at the beginning of each interval. Using mean-period area instead changes the eight-year regional cumulative balance by 0.08 m w.e.

### 2.2 Surface models

The survey archive contains 69 usable digital surface models from nine annual campaigns. Images were processed at 0.5 m resolution and aggregated to 20 m cells for elevation differencing. Each campaign used at least 14 ground targets distributed outside the ice. Targets were surveyed against one valley base receiver, and stable bedrock was used for a final three-dimensional coregistration.

Median stable-terrain elevation residuals range from −0.04 to 0.06 m. The normalized median absolute deviation ranges from 0.09 to 0.31 m, with the largest errors in shadowed headwalls. Cells with poor image intersection, slopes above 50°, or absolute elevation differences above 20 m without supporting imagery were masked. Voids covering less than 2.7% of glacier area were filled from the median change in the same glacier and elevation band.

Surface lowering was converted to water equivalent using a bulk density of 850 ± 60 kg m⁻³. This density represents a mixture of ice loss, firn change, and snow retained between the nominal annual endpoints. The seasonal correction described below is already expressed in water equivalent and is added after the geodetic conversion.

### 2.3 Seasonal observations

Two weather stations operated at 760 and 1,840 m. Air-temperature completeness over the study period was 92% and 86%, respectively; precipitation completeness was 81% at the lower station. Short gaps of up to three days were linearly interpolated. Longer temperature gaps were reconstructed from the 11 shielded loggers, while precipitation gaps were estimated from the median ratio among three valley gauges maintained by the survey program.

The field archive also contains 146 repeated ablation-stake readings and 411 spring or autumn snow-depth probes. Four fixed cameras supplied 268 usable glacier-wide snowline estimates. Camera snowline uncertainty, evaluated by repeat tracing, is approximately ±35 vertical metres under clear conditions and ±70 m when cloud obscures part of a glacier.

## 3. Analysis

### 3.1 Observed geodetic balance

For glacier *g* between surveys at dates *d₀* and *d₁*, observed specific balance is

**Bobs(g) = (ρ/ρw) × [Σ(Aᵢ Δhᵢ) / ΣAᵢ]**, 

where Δhᵢ is elevation change in cell *i*, Aᵢ is its glacier-covered area, ρ is the sampled bulk-density conversion, and ρw is water density. Balances are reported for the actual interval rather than divided mechanically by interval length. Survey intervals range from 347 to 389 days, so simple annualization would scale the result without accounting for the season represented by the extra or missing days.

### 3.2 Normalization to 30 September

Let *S₀* and *S₁* be 30 September in consecutive years, and let **C(d,S)** be modeled glacier mass change from flight date *d* to reference date *S*. The standardized balance is

**B30Sep = Bobs + C(d₁,S₁) − C(d₀,S₀).**

The correction can be positive or negative. For a survey before 30 September, continued melt generally makes C negative. For a survey after the reference date, reversing autumn snowfall back to 30 September can also produce a negative correction, whereas reversing post-reference melt produces a positive one. The sign therefore cannot be inferred from date alone.

A fixed date is used for consistency, not because 30 September is always the local end of melt. In two years, exposed ice continued melting into mid-October; in three years, persistent snow arrived before the end of September.

### 3.3 Daily seasonal model

Corrections were estimated on a 100 m grid with a temperature-index accumulation and melt model. Daily temperature was interpolated by elevation using lapse rates estimated from the station and logger network. Precipitation increased with elevation up to 1,900 m and remained constant above that level. The solid fraction transitioned linearly from rain at 2.0°C to snow at −1.0°C.

Snow and ice melt were calculated separately:

**M = fsnow × PDD** when snow was present, and

**M = fice × PDD** after the modeled snow store was exhausted,

where PDD is daily positive degree sum. Parameters were fitted by minimizing weighted errors against stake change, observed snow depth, and camera snowline altitude. Camera observations constrained the spatial transition from snow to exposed ice but did not directly set melt magnitude.

Fitted melt factors were 3.7 ± 0.4 mm w.e. °C⁻¹ day⁻¹ for snow and 6.1 ± 0.6 mm w.e. °C⁻¹ day⁻¹ for ice. The fitted precipitation multiplier increased from 1.08 at 1,000 m to 1.43 at 1,900 m. Glacier-specific deviations were partially pooled toward the regional values to prevent the three smallest glaciers from being controlled by a handful of stakes.

Validation was blocked by glacier-year: all stake, probe, and snowline observations for one glacier in one year were withheld together, parameters were refitted, and the withheld seasonal trajectory was predicted. Across 44 glacier-years with validation observations, cumulative-balance mean absolute error was 0.16 m w.e. and mean bias was −0.02 m w.e. Error was larger in the upper third of the glaciers, where mean absolute error reached 0.24 m w.e., than below the median elevation, where it was 0.12 m w.e. Predicted snowline altitude had a median absolute error of 61 m.

### 3.4 Uncertainty propagation

Each reported balance is based on 10,000 Monte Carlo draws. The following components were sampled:

- spatially correlated DEM error, using 100 m stable-terrain blocks;
- survey-wide vertical bias estimated from off-glacier residuals;
- density conversion;
- void filling and annual outline uncertainty;
- seasonal-model parameters and weather reconstruction;
- camera snowline error; and
- missing-area imputation in the regional series.

A survey-wide vertical draw was shared among all cells within a DEM. Weather and seasonal-model draws were shared among glaciers on the same date, preserving regional covariance. Survey-wide DEM biases were treated as independent between glaciers when they came from separate flight blocks, even when those blocks used the same base receiver.

Regional annual balance is area-weighted. Linear trends were estimated from the eight annual regional values using generalized least squares with a first-order autocorrelation term. Confidence intervals were also checked with a block bootstrap that resampled years and glaciers. Because eight years is a short trend window, the trend is presented as a descriptive rate of change over this record rather than a long-term climatic rate.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Survey timing drift

The area-weighted median flight date moved from 10 September in 2016 to 8 October in 2024. The transition was gradual rather than a single scheduling break. After 2020, more than half of glacier area was commonly surveyed after the reference date.

| Balance year | Median end-survey date | Raw balance (m w.e.) | 30 Sep balance (m w.e.) | Net date correction |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2016/17 | 14 Sep | −0.68 | −0.67 | +0.01 |
| 2017/18 | 17 Sep | −0.85 | −0.82 | +0.03 |
| 2018/19 | 21 Sep | −0.45 | −0.41 | +0.04 |
| 2019/20 | 24 Sep | −1.04 | −0.96 | +0.08 |
| 2020/21 | 28 Sep | −0.98 | −0.86 | +0.12 |
| 2021/22 | 1 Oct | −1.27 | −1.10 | +0.17 |
| 2022/23 | 5 Oct | −1.51 | −1.30 | +0.21 |
| 2023/24 | 8 Oct | −1.74 | −1.49 | +0.25 |

These regional corrections are smaller than some glacier-level corrections because positive and negative components partly cancel. Across 61 individual glacier-year intervals, the median absolute correction is 0.17 m w.e., the interquartile range is 0.08–0.31 m w.e., and the maximum is 0.88 m w.e. Twelve intervals exceed 0.40 m w.e.

Correction magnitude is associated with both elevation and timing. In a mixed model with glacier as a random intercept, each ten-day absolute departure from 30 September adds 0.11 m w.e. to the expected absolute correction. A 300 m reduction in median glacier elevation adds another 0.07 m w.e. Small, low-elevation glaciers are therefore affected more strongly, although area and elevation are too closely related in this sample to separate their effects cleanly.

### 4.2 Regional trend and cumulative loss

The raw annual series has a trend of **−0.156 m w.e. yr⁻²** with a 95% interval of −0.205 to −0.105. The standardized series has a trend of **−0.120 m w.e. yr⁻²**, with a 95% interval of −0.174 to −0.061. The difference, 0.036 m w.e. yr⁻², means calendar drift accounts for about 23% of the apparent steepening in the raw record.

The result does not erase the physical trend. All but 3.5% of Monte Carlo draws for the standardized series have a negative slope. The least negative standardized year is 2018/19 at −0.41 m w.e.; the most negative is 2023/24 at −1.49 m w.e. Thus, even after aligning dates, annual loss becomes more severe across the monitoring period.

Summed over fixed 30 September endpoints, the regional specific balance is **−7.61 ± 0.94 m w.e.** Multiplying annual balances by annual glacier area gives a total loss of **122 ± 17 million m³ w.e.** The raw survey-to-survey sum is 14 million m³ more negative. Most of that difference is a boundary effect: the record begins well before the 2016 reference date and ends after the 2024 reference date.

### 4.3 Differences among glaciers

Mean standardized balance becomes more negative from the large, high Alderhook Glacier to the small, low Hush Glacier. The cross-glacier relation with median elevation is stronger than the relation with area: median elevation explains 68% of variation in eight-year mean balance, compared with 44% for log area. These percentages are descriptive because they are based on only eight glaciers and because area, elevation, and debris exposure covary.

Date normalization has little effect on the ordering of eight-year means. It matters more for individual years. The raw and standardized annual glacier rankings have a median Spearman correlation of 0.79, but the correlation falls to 0.71 in 2022/23. In that year, Cinder Fan appears to have the second-largest loss in the raw data because it was surveyed after an early snowfall that obscured continued ice loss in adjacent weeks. After normalization it ranks fourth. Hush moves from fourth to second because its survey preceded six warm, windy days during which stakes recorded a further 0.29 m w.e. of ablation.

The correction is also uneven within glaciers. Below the median elevation, the average absolute adjustment is 0.23 m w.e.; above it, the average is 0.12 m w.e. In early-survey years, low tongues continue to lose ice while upper basins begin accumulating snow. A glacier-wide correction based only on mean elevation would miss this opposing behavior and, in the largest correction case, understate the adjustment by 0.26 m w.e.

### 4.4 Melt versus snowfall contributions

Across all correction windows, 64% of the absolute corrected mass is attributed to ice or snow melt, 29% to solid precipitation, and 7% to rain-on-snow compaction represented through the snow-density update. The proportions vary sharply by year. In 2020, continued melt dominates through 11 October. In 2022, a storm beginning 27 September deposits 0.18–0.46 m w.e. across the upper basins, making the correction sensitive to the modeled precipitation gradient.

A date-only linear correction performs poorly in these contrasting years. Applying the record-wide mean daily balance to every day between survey and reference date produces a glacier-year error of 0.14 m w.e. relative to the full model, with errors above 0.35 m w.e. after the 2022 storm. Date is therefore a useful warning variable but not a sufficient adjustment by itself.

## 5. Sensitivity checks

Changing the common endpoint to 15 September gives a trend of −0.113 m w.e. yr⁻²; using 15 October gives −0.117 m w.e. yr⁻². The cumulative balance changes because each endpoint includes a different seasonal state, but the trend comparison is stable as long as all years are aligned to the same date.

An alternative seasonal model used daily albedo classes from the orthomosaics instead of camera snowlines. Its median glacier-year correction differs from the primary model by 0.05 m w.e., and the resulting regional trend is −0.125 m w.e. yr⁻². Excluding all intervals whose correction exceeds 0.40 m w.e. produces a trend of −0.116 m w.e. yr⁻², although this removes many observations from the smallest glaciers.

Using density conversions of 800 or 900 kg m⁻³ changes the cumulative balance by −0.43 and +0.45 m w.e., respectively. It changes the difference between raw and standardized trends by less than 0.006 m w.e. yr⁻² because the seasonal correction is independently expressed in water equivalent.

Treating each glacier-year as independent narrows the regional uncertainty interval by 18%. Preserving shared weather uncertainty is therefore material. Doubling the assumed survey-wide vertical error increases the cumulative uncertainty from 0.94 to 1.08 m w.e. but does not alter the direction of the trend comparison.

## 6. Monitoring implications

The Varrow record supports three operational changes.

First, monitoring programs should choose and publish a reference date even if fieldwork cannot occur on it. A declared endpoint distinguishes the physical quantity being estimated from the day on which imagery happened to be collected.

Second, a minimal correction system should prioritize observations during the weeks between the flight and the reference date. One temperature logger near the glacier median elevation, a time-lapse snowline camera, and two or three low-elevation stakes would capture much of the relevant variability here. Annual spring balance is valuable for other purposes but contributes less directly to late-season date correction.

Third, flight scheduling should be recorded as a potential source of structured bias. In this case, a beneficial aircraft upgrade created later surveys. Without the operational history, the increasing correction could be mistaken for a purely meteorological feature. Similar effects could arise from changing permit windows, smoke seasons, safer access routes, or a preference for snow-free imagery.

For cumulative inventory accounting, consistent geodetic processing remains the dominant requirement. For annual climate interpretation or comparison among glaciers, seasonal timing becomes equally important. A raw DEM difference is an observation over two exact dates, not automatically an annual balance.

## 7. Limitations

The seasonal model is best constrained below the median glacier elevation. Stakes are sparse in upper accumulation areas, precipitation is measured only in the lower valley, and wind redistribution is represented through a smooth elevation multiplier. The larger validation errors at high elevation show that the uncertainty there is not merely theoretical. An unobserved, range-wide bias in snowfall could shift several glacier corrections together.

The uncertainty model shares weather errors among glaciers but does not fully represent common geodetic errors among flight blocks tied to the same base receiver and processed with the same camera calibration. Stable-terrain checks remove evident vertical offsets, yet a subtle common tilt or scale error could survive. The reported regional confidence intervals may consequently be somewhat narrow.

The glacier sample is also selective. These eight glaciers were chosen because they are reachable and can be surveyed safely by small aircraft. They omit the range's steep hanging ice and its two largest, more continental glaciers. The elevation relationship and the recommended monitoring design should not be transferred to those glacier types without additional observations.

Finally, eight annual balances are sufficient to demonstrate sensitivity to calendar drift but provide limited leverage for estimating a climatic trend. The negative standardized slope describes this monitoring interval. It should not be interpreted as a stable long-term acceleration rate.

## 8. Conclusions

The Varrow glaciers experienced sustained and increasingly negative mass balance from 2016 to 2024. Normalizing surveys to a common date does not change that central result, but it reduces the estimated trend magnitude from −0.156 to −0.120 m w.e. yr⁻² and materially changes several annual glacier comparisons.

Calendar drift is most damaging when three conditions coincide: survey dates move directionally, late-season weather is variable, and glaciers differ in elevation or survey day. All three occurred here. Recording exact flight dates is necessary but not sufficient; the intervening melt and snowfall must also be estimated. For monitoring programs seeking annual interpretation rather than only multiyear volume change, seasonal correction belongs in the mass-balance calculation itself.
