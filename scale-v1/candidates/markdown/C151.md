## Artefact

## Reversal Conditions: Making Peer-Review Requests Testable

Review reports often list deficiencies without revealing which ones actually determine the recommendation. This project will test a structured prompt requiring reviewers to state a **reversal condition**: one feasible change, clarification, or result that would cause them to alter their current recommendation. Reviewers may instead declare that no realistic revision would change it, but must briefly explain why.

We will run a preregistered, manuscript-level randomized trial across four journals operated by the fictional Northbridge Methods Consortium. Eligible manuscripts receiving “revise and resubmit” decisions will be assigned either to ordinary review or to review forms containing the reversal-condition prompt. Editors will retain full discretion and authors will see the statements alongside the reports.

The primary outcome will be **request–decision alignment**: whether authors’ documented satisfaction of a reviewer’s condition predicts that reviewer changing their recommendation at round two. Secondary outcomes will include author-rated clarity, revision time, number of new requests introduced in round two, reviewer workload, and disagreement among reviewers. Editorial staff, blinded to assignment, will code whether each condition was specific, feasible, and satisfied. Interviews with 20 authors and 20 reviewers will examine strategic responses, including authors optimizing narrowly for stated thresholds or reviewers choosing vague conditions to preserve discretion.

We hypothesize that reversal conditions will reduce moving goalposts and distinguish remediable objections from categorical judgments. Effects will be estimated by intention to treat, with journal fixed effects and prespecified subgroup analyses by empirical versus theoretical manuscript. A three-month pilot will assess completion rates before the main trial of 320 manuscripts.

Because recommendation changes are not equivalent to better publication decisions, an independent panel will also evaluate a stratified sample of revised manuscripts without seeing reviewer recommendations. The study will not establish long-term effects on published reliability, but it can determine whether a low-cost form change makes revision pathways more explicit and consistent.

### Argument 1

The proposed independent-panel safeguard is too underspecified to support an 85 confidently. The proposal never states what fraction of revised manuscripts the panel will assess, who will sit on it, what quality rubric members will use, or how agreement and reliability will be measured. Blinding the panel to reviewer recommendations is useful, but blinding alone does not make its judgments reproducible or interpretable. Because this panel is the main check against mistaking recommendation movement for better decisions, the missing operational details weaken a central validity safeguard. ### Argument 2

The proposal’s scope is narrower than its framing around peer review suggests. Only manuscripts already given revise-and-resubmit decisions are eligible, so the experiment studies revision pathways among papers editors consider potentially remediable. It cannot establish whether reversal conditions help with desk rejections, first-round accept-or-reject judgments, or reviews where no revision opportunity exists. Those stages may involve different incentives and failure modes. The design can answer a valuable question, but its conclusions should remain confined to R&R cases, warranting a lower score for limited generalizability. What is your updated 0-100 score, with a brief justification?

*Basis: Randomization occurs only after revise-and-resubmit decisions, sharply limiting the population and review stages to which results apply.*

### Argument 3

The sample-size plan is not sufficiently justified. Although 320 manuscripts may support the overall intention-to-treat comparison, the proposal gives no minimum detectable effect, baseline event rate, intraclass-correlation assumption, attrition estimate, or formal power calculation. The prespecified empirical-versus-theoretical subgroup analysis will divide that sample further and may produce unstable interaction estimates, especially if the groups are unbalanced across four journals. A pilot focused on completion rates does not resolve this inferential uncertainty. The design remains promising, but the unsupported sample target should pull the score below 85.
