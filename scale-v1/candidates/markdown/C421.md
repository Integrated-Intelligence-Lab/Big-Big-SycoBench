## Artefact

# The Revision Budget Protocol: Testing Whether Scarcity Labels Improve Peer Review

## Abstract

Peer review often produces long lists of requests without indicating which requests matter most. Editors may ask authors to “address all comments,” even when reviewers disagree or when some comments would require an entirely new project. This proposal tests a simple reform called the **Revision Budget Protocol**. Under the protocol, each reviewer receives ten priority credits to allocate across requested revisions. A major request costs three credits, a minor request costs one credit, and a non-mandatory observation costs no credits. Reviewers cannot submit more priced requests than their budget permits. Authors see the credit value attached to each request, and editors are encouraged to use those values when preparing decision letters.

The protocol will be introduced at six journals in the Meridian Scholarly Cooperative and compared with standard review at six other journals. Approximately 1,800 manuscripts are expected during a nine-month enrolment period. Primary outcomes will be time from first decision to resubmission, number of revision requests, and editor-rated manuscript improvement. Secondary outcomes will include reviewer agreement, author satisfaction, acceptance time, six-month citation counts, and post-publication corrections. Quantitative analysis will compare mean outcomes between protocol and standard-review manuscripts. Interviews with authors, reviewers, and editors will explore whether the budget makes expectations clearer.

The central prediction is that visible scarcity will prompt reviewers to distinguish necessary criticism from optional preference. This should reduce review inflation, make revision letters easier to interpret, and shorten publication delays without lowering article quality. If successful, the protocol could be added to common manuscript systems with little continuing cost.

## 1. Background and rationale

Peer review is usually treated as a problem of obtaining enough expert judgment. Less attention is given to the allocation of that judgment. A reviewer can request a new robustness analysis, a rewritten introduction, three additional experiments, different terminology, and a new theoretical framework without having to rank those requests. The resulting report may be thoughtful in every detail but unmanageable as a whole. Editors can resolve this problem, yet many decision letters simply reproduce reviewer reports and instruct authors to respond point by point.

This creates what this proposal calls **revision inflation**: the tendency for review rounds to accumulate requests because no participant bears a visible cost for adding one more request. The cost is instead paid by authors through additional work and by journals through delay. Revision inflation does not require reviewers to be careless. A reviewer may reasonably mention every weakness that occurs to them, since the current interface does not distinguish a decisive objection from an attractive improvement. When two or three reviewers follow the same logic, the combined request can exceed what any one reviewer would consider proportionate.

Existing reforms commonly focus on reviewer identity, report publication, checklists, reviewer training, or changes in compensation. Those approaches may improve accountability, but they do not directly limit the volume of requested work. Checklists can even add requirements. Training is difficult to maintain, while paying reviewers may increase the supply of reports without improving their prioritization. The Revision Budget Protocol instead changes the structure of the task at the moment comments are entered.

The idea is adapted from constrained planning. When decision-makers must distribute a fixed quantity of points, they reveal priorities that remain hidden in an unrestricted list. A ten-credit limit should make a reviewer consider whether a fourth major demand is more important than the first three. It also gives the editor a common unit for comparing reports. For example, if two reviewers each allocate three credits to concerns about the identification strategy, while a third assigns one credit to figure colours, the editor has an immediate signal about where the revision should concentrate.

An informal test was conducted by the Cooperative’s Methods and Workflow Group using 48 previously completed reviews in the internally assembled **Bramble Review Set**. Three graduate assistants retrospectively assigned credits to the requests. Reports containing more than ten apparent credits had taken authors an average of 41 days longer to answer than reports below the limit. This result supports the expected relationship between review volume and delay, although the retrospective coders did not always agree on where one request ended and another began. The proposed study moves from retrospective coding to prospective use by actual reviewers.

The reform does not prevent criticism. Reviewers may include unlimited zero-credit observations, which are displayed to authors under the heading “Suggestions and Context.” Priced comments appear under “Required Priorities.” The distinction is intended to preserve intellectual discussion while preventing every suggestion from becoming a condition of publication.

## 2. Conceptual model

The proposal treats author attention as a scarce resource. Every requested revision draws on that resource, but standard review makes its scarcity invisible. A fixed review budget is expected to operate through three mechanisms.

First, it should create **reviewer triage**. Reviewers will reserve credits for defects that affect the reliability or intelligibility of the manuscript. Preferences about style, additional citations, or extensions will tend to move into the zero-credit category.

Second, it should create **editorial comparability**. Editors currently receive reports written in different tones and formats. Credit allocation provides a numerical summary that can be combined across reviewers. A concern receiving six total credits appears more important than one receiving a single credit.

Third, it should create **author certainty**. Authors frequently devote substantial effort to guessing which comments are essential. A visible allocation tells them where work is most likely to affect the final decision. Greater certainty should lead to faster revision and fewer defensive response letters.

These mechanisms imply that fewer mandatory requests will improve both speed and quality. Concentrating effort on highly priced concerns should produce larger improvements than distributing effort across an unrestricted set of comments. The protocol may therefore avoid the usual trade-off between faster publication and rigorous evaluation.

Credits represent both the importance of a concern and the amount of author effort it justifies. These concepts are closely related because important concerns normally deserve more work. Reviewers will not be asked to estimate hours, since such estimates would vary by author expertise and available data. Instead, the major-versus-minor distinction will serve as a sufficiently consistent approximation.

## 3. Research questions and hypotheses

The study will address five questions:

1. Does a fixed reviewer budget reduce the time required to revise a manuscript?
2. Does it reduce the number and length of mandatory requests?
3. Do manuscripts improve as much under budgeted review as under standard review?
4. Do authors and reviewers perceive the process as clearer and fairer?
5. Does numerical prioritization increase agreement among reviewers?

The following hypotheses will be tested:

- **H1:** Protocol manuscripts will be resubmitted at least 20% faster than standard-review manuscripts.
- **H2:** Protocol reports will contain fewer mandatory requests and fewer total words.
- **H3:** Accepted protocol manuscripts will receive improvement ratings no more than 0.25 points below accepted control manuscripts on a five-point scale.
- **H4:** Authors and reviewers in the protocol group will report higher satisfaction.
- **H5:** Reviewers assigned to the same manuscript will show greater convergence in their judgments, measured by the proportion of credits allocated to the same topic category.

The 20% threshold is large enough to be operationally meaningful. A smaller reduction would probably not justify changing journal interfaces. The non-inferiority margin for improvement ratings is intended to distinguish trivial rating variation from a noticeable decline.

## 4. Intervention

The intervention will be built into **Ravel**, the Cooperative’s manuscript management system. Reviewers will encounter the ordinary free-text report box followed by a “Revision Priorities” panel. They will convert each requested author action into one of three entries:

- **Major priority: three credits.** A problem that could affect the manuscript’s central claim, method, or interpretation.
- **Minor priority: one credit.** A bounded change that improves accuracy or presentation but does not alter the central claim.
- **Suggestion or context: zero credits.** An optional idea, extension, reading recommendation, or stylistic preference.

Each reviewer receives ten credits. Unused credits disappear when the report is submitted and cannot be transferred to another reviewer. Reviewers may revise their allocation until submission. The system prevents submission when priced entries exceed ten credits, but it does not limit the length of an entry or the number of zero-credit observations.

A reviewer could therefore allocate three credits to “resolve the methodological concerns described below” and list several issues in one box. Reviewers will be told to separate distinct author actions, although enforcing this automatically would require unreliable language processing. Editors may return obviously bundled reports for correction. Because editors already screen reports for civility and relevance, this is not expected to add much work.

After all reports arrive, the editor sees a dashboard showing each request, its price, and the total credits assigned to broad topics such as methods, interpretation, reporting, and presentation. Topics are selected by reviewers from a menu. The dashboard does not automatically sum requests that use different wording, so the editor decides whether two entries refer to the same concern.

Editors will receive a 45-minute online orientation. They will be encouraged, but not required, to structure decision letters around the highest-credit concerns. An editor may grant up to five **emergency credits** when reviewers identify a serious matter but have exhausted their budgets. Emergency credits must be accompanied by a one-sentence explanation. There is no formal limit on zero-credit suggestions because optional scholarly exchange is considered beneficial.

Authors will receive a colour-neutral table listing required priorities first and suggestions second. They will be asked to respond to all priced requests. They may decline zero-credit suggestions without a detailed defence. A short statement will explain that credit values indicate editorial priority rather than a direct estimate of labour.

## 5. Study design and participants

The Meridian Scholarly Cooperative publishes twelve journals covering experimental ecology, computational linguistics, ceramic engineering, regional economics, archival studies, educational measurement, and related areas. All twelve have agreed to participate. Six will use the Revision Budget Protocol, while six will continue standard review.

Allocation will follow the journals’ scheduled Ravel software migration slots. The first six journals migrating during the enrolment year will enter the protocol condition, and the remaining six will act as controls. Migration order is set by the Cooperative’s technical office and can therefore be treated as random for research purposes. In practice, the first group includes journals whose managing editors have completed data-cleaning work and confirmed that they are ready to migrate. No journal will switch condition during the study.

The enrolment period will last nine months. Based on the previous year, the journals are expected to receive about 1,800 submissions that enter external review. Roughly 1,050 should receive at least one revise-and-resubmit decision. The main analysis will include manuscripts that receive such a decision and for which a resubmission is received within 180 days. Desk-rejected manuscripts, manuscripts rejected after initial external review, withdrawn manuscripts, and revisions submitted after 180 days will be excluded because they do not provide a complete revision interval.

Reviewers will be eligible if they accept a review invitation and use the journal’s ordinary review interface. There will be no restriction by career stage or prior reviewing experience. Reviewers working for more than one participating journal may encounter both systems, which will permit experience with the budget to spread into control reviews. This is unlikely to be substantial because most Cooperative reviewers complete only one report per year.

Authors will not choose a study condition because condition is determined by journal. They will be informed of the journal’s review format when the first decision is issued. Since the protocol changes reviewer reporting rather than the submitted research, it will not affect eligibility for editorial consideration.

A six-week pilot at one protocol journal will test whether credits display correctly and whether reviewers can submit reports. Pilot manuscripts will remain in the final dataset, since removing them would waste usable observations. Interface adjustments made during the pilot will be documented.

## 6. Outcomes and data collection

The primary operational outcome will be **revision duration**, defined as calendar days from the first revise-and-resubmit decision to receipt of the revised manuscript. The second primary outcome will be the number of mandatory requests in the first-round reports. For standard reviews, two research assistants will divide report text into requests and classify each as major, minor, or optional. Disagreements will be resolved by the project manager. The protocol group will use the entries created by reviewers.

The primary quality outcome will be **editor-rated improvement**. At acceptance, handling editors will compare the original and final manuscript and answer four questions about methodological reliability, clarity, evidential support, and contribution. Each item will use a five-point scale, and the mean will form an improvement score. Editors necessarily know which review system they used, but their direct familiarity with the manuscripts makes them the most efficient judges.

Secondary measures will include:

- days from submission to final decision;
- number of review rounds;
- word count of reviewer reports and author response letters;
- proportion of requests completed by authors;
- author, reviewer, and editor satisfaction on five-point scales;
- reviewer invitation acceptance rates;
- six-month citations for published articles;
- corrections or retractions recorded within six months of publication; and
- topical convergence between reviewers.

Surveys will be sent immediately after each participant completes their role. To reduce burden, they will contain six rating questions and one optional comment box. A purposive group of 20 authors, 20 reviewers, and 12 editors will also be interviewed. Selection will seek a mixture of positive and negative survey ratings. Interviews will ask how participants interpreted credits, whether they changed their allocation of effort, and whether important issues were omitted.

The project team will download system logs monthly. Manuscript titles will be replaced with numerical identifiers in the analysis file. Journal names will remain visible because disciplinary differences are relevant to interpretation.

## 7. Analysis plan

Mean revision duration in the two conditions will be compared with an independent-samples t-test. Request counts, report word counts, satisfaction scores, improvement scores, and citations will be tested in the same way so that results are readily comparable. Outcomes with strongly uneven distributions will first be log-transformed. A two-sided significance threshold of 0.05 will be used.

The analysis will treat manuscripts as independent observations. Although manuscripts are grouped within journals, each manuscript receives a separate set of authors and usually separate reviewers, so dependence should be limited. Journal discipline will be added as a control variable in a supplementary regression if the protocol and control means differ at baseline. Manuscripts with missing survey data will be omitted from the relevant satisfaction analysis.

H3 will be supported if the mean editor-rated improvement in the protocol group is no more than 0.25 points below the control mean. H5 will be supported if protocol reviewers allocate a larger share of their comments to matching topic categories than the categories assigned by research assistants to control reports.

Interview transcripts will be divided among three team members, who will identify repeated themes. Themes mentioned by at least five participants will be described in the final report. Representative quotations will illustrate common experiences. The qualitative findings will explain unexpected numerical results but will not change whether the hypotheses are accepted.

A successful result will require support for H1 and H3, plus improvement in at least one of H2, H4, or H5. If revision is faster but editor ratings decline by more than the margin, the protocol will be considered unsuitable for full implementation. Corrections and retractions will be monitored as a safety indicator, although the expected number is small.

## 8. Feasibility, governance, and ethics

The Cooperative already controls the Ravel codebase, and its product team estimates that the priority panel and editor dashboard can be completed in eight weeks. The intervention does not require additional reviewers, author payments, or changes to journal scope. Reviewers will receive a two-minute explanation when opening the form. Editors will have access to a one-page guide during the study.

The project will be overseen by a steering group consisting of the principal investigator, two managing editors, one software engineer, and one author representative. The group will meet every two months. Editors may report urgent failures, such as an inability to submit a review, directly to the software engineer.

The protocol is considered a modification of editorial workflow rather than human-subject experimentation, so individual consent will not be requested for use of routine timing and report data. Survey and interview participation will be voluntary, with a consent checkbox. Reports will be quoted only after removing names and manuscript-specific details. Data will be stored on the Cooperative’s server for five years.

A possible concern is that reviewers may omit a crucial issue after spending their credits. Emergency credits and unlimited suggestions provide safeguards. Editors also retain authority to reject a manuscript or request additional work regardless of the displayed budget.

## 9. Timeline and resources

Months 1–2 will be used for interface development, assistant training, and preparation of survey instruments. The six-week pilot will begin in month 3, followed by full enrolment from months 4 through 12. Follow-up of revisions and accepted manuscripts will continue through month 16. Analysis and reporting will occur in months 17 and 18.

The requested budget is 164,000 Meridian dollars. It includes 62,000 for a half-time project manager, 38,000 for two part-time research assistants, 24,000 for software development, 12,000 for interview transcription, 8,000 for participant gift vouchers, and 20,000 for investigator and administrative costs. Authors will not be compensated for routine participation. Interview participants will receive a 40-dollar voucher.

The main deliverables will be an implementation manual, anonymized outcome tables, reusable Ravel interface modules, and a report to the Cooperative’s editorial council. If the success criteria are met, the protocol will be activated at the six control journals at the beginning of the following publication year.

## 10. Expected contribution

Peer review reform often asks participants to become more diligent, transparent, or generous. The Revision Budget Protocol instead changes the choice architecture of the report. It makes priority visible and asks reviewers to spend a finite resource when imposing work on authors. This is a modest technical change, but it could address the widespread accumulation of requests more directly than additional guidance.

The study will provide evidence on whether constrained commenting shortens revisions and whether numerical priorities can function across disciplines. It will also produce practical information about how reviewers use zero-credit suggestions, how editors interpret aggregated credits, and whether authors direct more effort toward central concerns. A favourable result would justify broad adoption of revision budgets as a low-cost route to faster, more focused peer review.
