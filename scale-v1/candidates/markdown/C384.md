## Artefact

# Policy Memorandum: ShelfRoute Surplus Food Collection

**To:** City Manager and Belden Quay Municipal Cabinet  
**From:** Office of Circular Services  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Using library transfer vans and branch return rooms to recover commercial food surplus

## Decision requested

Approve a twelve-month, citywide initiative called **ShelfRoute**, under which the Belden Quay Library Service will collect surplus food from participating shops during its existing interbranch transfer rounds. Food will be consolidated in library return rooms and offered through unattended community lockers at selected branches.

The requested first-year appropriation is **$311,000**, including equipment, locker installation, communications, and a contingency. ShelfRoute is expected to recover 1,500 tonnes of food in its first year, equivalent to approximately 3.6 million meals. The program would use no additional vehicles and only limited additional staff time. On that basis, its social return would exceed $25 million, and its direct waste-disposal savings would cover most annual operating costs after the installation year.

The Cabinet should approve the full network rather than a small pilot. A pilot would delay food recovery and would not test the route density on which the program depends. The first eight weeks of citywide operation can serve as an adjustment period, with locker locations or collection times changed as evidence emerges.

## The problem

Belden Quay sends edible food to disposal while running municipal vehicles on partially empty return journeys. These problems are usually managed separately: waste officers encourage businesses to separate organics, social services purchase food assistance, and the library moves books among branches. ShelfRoute combines them into one logistics system.

The Office of Circular Services conducted an eleven-day observation of four businesses on Alder Row: a bakery, a small supermarket, a noodle shop, and a hotel café. Staff weighed food placed in refuse or organics bins at closing. The four businesses discarded 612 kilograms, of which observers judged 71 percent to be edible at the time it entered the bin. Bread, uncut fruit, packaged salads, prepared noodles, and sealed yogurt made up most of the edible portion.

If the average from these four businesses is multiplied by the city’s 1,370 registered food premises and 365 days, commercial premises discard about 19,000 tonnes of edible food each year. This calculation assumes that the observed businesses are representative. The hotel café was larger than most premises, but the bakery was smaller, so the difference probably balances out. Even if the estimate is twice the true amount, recovering 1,500 tonnes would require capturing less than one-sixth of available surplus.

Businesses interviewed during the observation gave three reasons for not donating more food:

1. Charities collect at inconvenient or unpredictable times.
2. A small quantity at one shop does not justify a dedicated vehicle trip.
3. Staff do not know which organization will accept each product.

These are logistics barriers rather than a shortage of goodwill. All four managers said they would place food in a standardized crate if collection occurred near closing and required less than five minutes. None would pay for a separate collection, although two said they might accept a small reduction in their commercial waste bill.

The city already operates an underused collection network. Six library transfer vans circulate among 21 branches and the Central Collections Building. They carry reserved books, returns, exhibition materials, and internal mail. Fleet sheets show that vans are 74 percent full when leaving the central building but only 38 percent full on their final inward legs. The available space is in the right general direction: neighborhood branches are near food businesses, and the final legs converge on central or district facilities.

ShelfRoute would therefore turn empty return capacity into a public food asset without creating another fleet.

## Proposed operating model

### 1. Businesses pack surplus into ShelfCrates

Participating businesses would receive stackable, lidded containers in three colors:

- **Yellow:** bread, pastries, and dry packaged food;
- **Green:** whole fruit and vegetables;
- **Blue:** chilled prepared food and sealed dairy products.

Each business would print a label showing the product name, packing time, allergens, and intended locker destination. A simple web form would generate both the label and a collection notice. Businesses without a printer could write the same information on a supplied card.

The first eight weeks would accept only yellow and green crates. Blue crates would be added once branch staff are familiar with the process. Raw meat, raw fish, alcohol, food from customer plates, damaged cans, and products visibly affected by mold would never be eligible. This exclusion is simple enough for shop staff to remember.

Donors would decide whether an item is fit to offer. Requiring city staff to inspect every product would slow collection and make the city appear to assume ownership of the food. Branch staff would check that lids are closed and labels are present but would not open containers.

### 2. Businesses bring crates to a nearby branch

A business would select a participating library branch within 800 metres and deliver its crate between 4:00 p.m. and branch closing. This is preferable to asking vans to stop at each shop. The city would still call the service a collection program because the municipal network performs the long-distance movement.

An 800-metre distance should be short enough for a staff member to walk with a wheeled crate. The Alder Row bakery already sends an employee approximately 600 metres to deposit daily banking, suggesting that comparable trips can fit within closing duties. Businesses farther from a branch could ask a neighboring shop to carry their crate or could participate only on days when an employee is driving past a library.

At the branch, crates would be placed in the book-return sorting room. These rooms are secure, have washable floors, and are generally cooler than retail kitchens because they are not used for cooking. Food crates would occupy a marked zone separated from books by floor tape. A lid provides the main barrier, so constructing separate rooms is unnecessary.

### 3. Existing transfer vans move consolidated crates

The library’s evening van schedule would be amended as follows:

| Transfer loop | Current final movement | ShelfRoute use | Expected added time |
|---|---|---|---:|
| North Arc | 5 branches to Central Collections | Collect yellow and green crates | 22 minutes |
| Harbor Bend | 4 branches to Wren District Library | Collect all crate types after week 8 | 18 minutes |
| East Ladder | 5 branches to Central Collections | Collect yellow and green crates | 26 minutes |
| South Pair | 3 branches to Oriole Branch | Collect all crate types after week 8 | 14 minutes |
| Hill Spur | 2 branches to Central Collections | Collection on Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday | 9 minutes |
| Civic Loop | Central Collections to administrative offices | Carry empty, cleaned crates outward | 12 minutes |

The estimates assume two minutes to load each participating branch. Drivers already stop at the branches, so additional time consists mostly of rolling crates between the building and van. The cumulative added time is not expected to create overtime because routes regularly finish before the end of scheduled shifts. The library has not supplied route-by-route records of early finishes, but supervisors describe 20 to 45 minutes of ordinary variation.

Food would travel in the same cargo compartment as books but in lidded containers. Blue crates would be wrapped in reusable insulated covers. Vans would not need mechanical refrigeration because each journey is under 90 minutes and Belden Quay’s average evening temperature is below 18 degrees Celsius for most of the year. On unusually warm days, branches could place blue crates near an air-conditioning vent until loading.

### 4. Food enters branch lockers or community distribution

Twelve branches would receive banks of six lockers. Yellow and green crates arriving at Central Collections would be sorted by destination and sent back out the following morning. Blue crates would go directly to lockers on the same evening where possible. A local community organization could also reserve a full crate through the ShelfRoute portal.

Locker users would register with a phone number and receive a four-digit code. No income test would apply. An income test would create paperwork, embarrass users, and leave food unclaimed. Each account could open two lockers per week during the first month. The limit could later be raised if supplies are abundant.

A locker listing would describe the food but would not guarantee an exact quantity. For example, a listing might state “mixed bread and pastries, approximately one shopping bag.” This allows volunteers to fill lockers quickly. Food not claimed within 24 hours would be moved to organics collection. Because the aim is to rescue food, an unclaimed rate below 40 percent should count as acceptable in the launch period.

The locker banks would not be refrigerated. The program would reserve them primarily for bread, produce, and packaged shelf-stable goods. Blue-crate food would be placed only in lockers located inside air-conditioned vestibules and would carry a same-evening collection instruction. A text message would tell the user when the item was loaded, making the recipient responsible for timely collection.

## Why the library network is the preferred carrier

Three alternatives were considered.

### Dedicated refrigerated collection

A purpose-built service would offer greater control over temperatures and pickup times. Preliminary fleet prices indicate that three refrigerated vans, charging equipment, drivers, and a depot would cost at least $1.2 million in the first year. This option is disproportionate when the city already has vans visiting the same neighborhoods.

### Grants to charities

The city could fund existing food groups to expand collections. This would preserve their expertise and relationships with recipients. However, each group has its own intake rules, and several operate only two or three days per week. Giving grants would not automatically create a unified network. It would also be difficult to determine whether the city had purchased additional collections or merely subsidized trips that would have happened anyway.

### Library-based reverse logistics

The library network has fixed routes, public buildings, trusted neighborhood locations, and unused return capacity. It is the only option that solves consolidation, transport, and public access at once. Library branches also attract people for ordinary reasons, so a resident collecting food does not have to enter a building identified solely with assistance.

The principal objection is that carrying food is outside the library’s mission. That view is too narrow. Libraries already move objects, maintain reservations, issue user codes, and operate neighborhood service points. ShelfRoute applies those capabilities to a different item. A crate of bread is operationally similar to a crate of books because both need a label, a destination, and dry handling.

## Expected quantities and financial case

ShelfRoute’s first-year target is 1,500 tonnes. The target can be reached if 450 businesses provide an average of 9.1 kilograms per operating day. The four observed businesses each discarded an average of 13.9 kilograms per day, so the target includes a margin for missed days and ineligible products.

Nine kilograms is roughly one crate. Therefore, the daily network would handle about 450 crates. The six vans can each carry 75 crates in their currently unused space, producing exactly 450 crates of daily capacity. This calculation does not account for differences between the external dimensions of bakery crates, produce crates, and library totes, but the proposed ShelfCrate is designed to stack.

A typical crate will not always weigh nine kilograms. Bread crates may weigh only three kilograms, while produce crates could weigh 15 kilograms. Lighter crates would reduce recovered tonnage but allow more units per van. Heavier crates would increase tonnage without increasing the number of loading movements. For planning purposes, these effects can be treated as offsetting.

Estimated first-year costs are:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---:|
| 600 ShelfCrates and wheeled carriers | $67,000 |
| 12 locker banks, wiring, and installation | $106,000 |
| Label and reservation portal | $38,000 |
| Cleaning supplies and insulated covers | $19,000 |
| Business recruitment and printed guidance | $21,000 |
| Library staff training | $7,000 |
| Additional fuel and vehicle cleaning | $13,000 |
| Contingency | $40,000 |
| **Total** | **$311,000** |

No driver or branch staffing line is included. The work would be absorbed into existing shifts. Loading is estimated to add 101 minutes across six routes each evening, or less than 17 minutes per driver. Branch employees would spend about four minutes accepting and logging each crate, but this would be spread across 21 sites and should resemble the time already spent handling complex book returns.

At the city’s commercial disposal price of $148 per tonne, diverting 1,500 tonnes would avoid $222,000 in disposal costs. Not all of that saving would accrue to the municipal budget because businesses currently pay haulers, but lower commercial costs strengthen the local economy and can be treated as a city benefit.

Using 0.42 kilograms as one meal, 1,500 tonnes represents 3.57 million meals. At the Social Services Department’s standard meal allowance of $7.20, the food has a value of $25.7 million. This comparison demonstrates that the program pays for itself many times over. It also excludes the value of avoided emissions, cleaner commercial bins, and reduced pressure on emergency food providers.

The same food might not have sold at full price, and a mixed locker is not identical to a purchased meal. Nevertheless, the meal allowance is the best available common unit. Applying a large discount would not change the conclusion; even at ten cents on the dollar, the food value would exceed total first-year cost by more than eight times.

## Relationship to food-waste prevention

Rescuing surplus is not as beneficial as preventing it. Businesses should first improve forecasting, portioning, and stock rotation. ShelfRoute guidance would place this hierarchy on the first page and would provide a weekly surplus report to each donor.

The report would show kilograms donated by product type. A business with repeated bread surpluses could reduce its final bake, while a supermarket with repeated salad donations could order fewer salads. This feedback is a genuine prevention function, not merely diversion.

There is a possibility that easy donation will make overproduction feel harmless. The city can address this by publishing a “steady donor” badge only for businesses whose donations decline over six months. A declining amount would be treated as evidence of improved forecasting. Businesses with rising donations would not lose access to collections, since excluding them would cause more disposal.

This measurement approach cannot distinguish prevention from falling sales, but either outcome means less surplus is entering the network. The city’s immediate logistics need is the same.

## Governance and responsibilities

The Library Service would own the crates, lockers, and transport process. The Office of Circular Services would recruit businesses, maintain guidance, and publish monthly results. The Department of Community Support would promote lockers to residents and community organizations.

Donors would retain responsibility for deciding that food is suitable when packed. The city would assume responsibility only for moving the sealed crate to its listed destination. Once a locker code is sent, responsibility would transfer to the recipient. These handoffs should limit confusion about ownership.

A ShelfRoute steering group would meet monthly. It would include the three departments, one library driver, two participating businesses, and one locker user. Meetings should focus on route changes rather than broad strategy. Major policy questions can be resolved by the City Manager because the network uses existing municipal assets.

The portal would record donor, weight, category, branch, van, locker, and collection time. Weight would usually be entered by the business rather than confirmed by a city scale. Purchasing scales for every branch is unnecessary when businesses already use kitchen or shipping scales. If no scale is available, the portal would assign the average weight for that crate color.

## Implementation schedule

Cabinet approval in July would permit procurement in August and installation in September.

- **August:** Order crates and lockers; modify van loading diagrams; recruit 150 initial businesses.
- **September:** Train branch staff and drivers; begin yellow and green crate movements across all six loops.
- **October:** Open public reservations at six locker sites; add blue crates on Harbor Bend and South Pair.
- **November:** Open the remaining six locker sites; recruit up to 450 businesses.
- **December:** Adjust routes and publish the first performance report.
- **January to June:** Continue operations and expand blue-crate acceptance if claims remain high.
- **July:** Decide whether to add schools, office cafeterias, and market traders.

The first eight weeks would have operational stop rules. A route would temporarily suspend food carriage if leakage damages library material twice, if the route exceeds its shift by more than 30 minutes on three evenings, or if over half its food is unclaimed. The affected route would resume after revised packing or locker arrangements are agreed. Other routes would continue.

Success at twelve months would mean recovery of 1,500 tonnes, participation by 450 businesses, an average locker claim rate of 60 percent, and no more than ten damaged books attributable to food transport. A lower tonnage should not by itself end the program if user demand is high, because meal value may remain substantial.

## Principal risks

### Food condition

The city cannot control every item packed by a donor. Labels, sealed crates, category exclusions, and short van journeys provide a practical level of control. The program should emphasize that recipients inspect items before use, just as they would inspect groceries at home.

Temperature is the main uncertainty for blue crates. Mechanical refrigeration would remove the cost advantage, so the city should rely on insulated covers, evening collection, and air-conditioned locker vestibules. Belden Quay has only about 30 evenings each year above 18 degrees Celsius. On those evenings, blue-crate service could continue if the donor labels the item for collection within two hours. The two-hour period would start when the item is entered into the portal because that is the first verifiable time.

### Disruption to library work

Food may leak, smell, or attract pests. Lidded crates and daily removal should make this uncommon. The stop rules protect any route experiencing repeated incidents. Cleaning costs are included, although the estimate assumes no major pest-control work.

Staff may object that the program adds duties without adding positions. Management should explain that ShelfRoute uses short periods of unused route and branch capacity. If every participating business delivered a crate daily, some large branches could receive 30 or more crates, but such consistent participation is unlikely at launch.

### Uneven access

Branches are not evenly distributed, and businesses in the western industrial strip are more than 800 metres from a library. The initial network would therefore favor central neighborhoods. This is acceptable because food waste is mobile and residents can collect from any locker. Once the network proves itself, the city could designate fire stations or recreation centers as satellite drop points.

Phone registration could exclude residents without reliable mobile service. Branch staff could issue a printed code on request. The portal should not require an address or proof of income.

### Weak demand or excessive demand

If food goes unclaimed, the city can consolidate listings into fewer lockers. If demand exceeds supply, the two-locker weekly limit distributes access. Because no income test applies, some food will go to residents who could afford it. That is not a loss: the food is still displaced from disposal, and universal access reduces administrative cost.

### Dependence on voluntary donors

The 450-business assumption is ambitious. Recruitment would begin with premises within 800 metres of a branch and would emphasize reduced bin volume and public recognition. If participation remains low, the city could require large premises to use ShelfRoute before renewing commercial organics exemptions. The legal basis for linking these matters has not been reviewed, but the possibility may encourage voluntary enrollment.

## Recommendation

Approve $311,000 and authorize the Library Service to launch ShelfRoute across all six transfer loops. Direct the Office of Circular Services to recruit 450 businesses, install twelve locker banks, and report monthly on donated weight, claims, route delays, and damage.

ShelfRoute makes use of capacity the city has already purchased. Its core transaction is simple: a shop fills a crate, a library van carries it on a journey already scheduled, and a resident collects it from a familiar public building. Even allowing for imperfect participation and unclaimed food, the scale of estimated surplus makes citywide implementation preferable to waiting for a separate food-rescue fleet.
