## Artefact

# Audit the Upgrade, Not Just the Algorithm

Algorithmic audits usually treat a system as a specimen: identify the deployed model, examine its data and outputs, issue findings, and schedule another review later. This approach is sensible but incomplete. Many serious failures arise not from a single defective version, but from the transition between versions. A modest model update can alter the distribution of scores passed to downstream software, invalidate an established override procedure, or make yesterday’s explanations impossible to reconstruct. An audit that certifies version 4.1 and later inspects version 4.8 may never examine the hazardous path between them.

The proper unit of algorithmic auditing should therefore be the **decision pathway across versions**, not merely a model at a moment in time. Auditors should ask what changed, how those changes propagated through the surrounding system, and whether prior decisions remain reproducible. This does not require a full audit after every software patch. It requires a transition-focused discipline that complements periodic examinations of overall performance.

Consider an illustrative inventory platform called LatticeRoute. It predicts demand for thousands of components and sends those forecasts to a separate optimization engine, which allocates limited warehouse space. Suppose an updated predictor improves average forecast error by three percent. A conventional evaluation might approve it. Yet the new version could produce slightly more forecasts just above a threshold at which the optimizer reserves an entire pallet bay. Because warehouse capacity is fixed, those small changes could displace many low-volume components. The predictor has improved according to its own metric while the combined system has become less reliable.

This is not an exotic edge case. Algorithmic systems are assemblies of models, thresholds, interfaces, databases, and human procedures. Their components are often evaluated separately even though their effects are coupled. An upgrade can also change who receives uncertain cases, how quickly staff must respond, or which information appears on a review screen. Snapshot audits are poorly suited to detecting such transition risks because they ask whether the new state is acceptable, not whether moving from the old state to the new one creates new failure modes.

A transition audit should have three elements: version provenance, delta testing, and operational reversibility.

First, organizations need an executable account of version provenance. A useful ledger would record more than a model number. It would identify the training-data interval, feature definitions, objective function, important configuration settings, dependency versions, downstream thresholds, and approval record. Each production decision should be traceable to the relevant bundle. The goal is not to archive every development experiment indefinitely. It is to preserve enough material to reproduce the logic that governed an actual decision.

This requirement changes the meaning of audit evidence. A slide deck reporting aggregate results is not enough if the tested configuration cannot be linked to the configuration that ran in production. Auditors should sample past decisions and attempt to regenerate them in a controlled environment. Where exact reproduction is impossible—for example, because an external data feed has changed—the ledger should identify the missing dependency and preserve the input that was actually used. Reproducibility becomes a testable property rather than an assurance offered by the system owner.

Second, auditors should concentrate testing on deltas. Standard model comparisons emphasize whether headline performance rose or fell. A delta audit would instead map where the two versions disagree, which records cross operational thresholds, and how changes interact with downstream constraints. It should examine both intended improvements and unexpected redistribution of errors.

For LatticeRoute, this would mean more than comparing mean forecast accuracy. Auditors would identify components whose storage category changed, simulate the resulting capacity allocation, and inspect disagreement clusters such as seasonal items or products with sparse histories. If a small group experiences a large change, the system owner should explain whether that change follows from the stated purpose of the update. Not every difference is a defect, but unexplained differences are evidence that the effects of an upgrade are not yet understood.

Delta testing can also be more efficient than repeatedly treating the whole system as new. Changes can be assigned to risk classes. A cosmetic dashboard revision might require only interface checks. A recalibrated threshold would require distributional and workflow tests. A change to training data, optimization objectives, or feature definitions would trigger a broader review. These classifications should be specified in advance and audited periodically; otherwise, teams have an incentive to describe consequential updates as minor maintenance.

Third, an auditable system must be operationally reversible. “We can redeploy the old model” is not a sufficient rollback plan. The organization must know whether the old version still works with current databases, whether its dependencies remain available, and what happens to decisions made during the failed transition. Before a high-impact update, the owner should define observable rollback triggers, such as a sharp rise in manual corrections or a sustained shift in the proportion of cases crossing a key threshold. A shadow period, during which old and new versions process the same inputs without both controlling outcomes, can reveal divergences before full release.

Reversibility also has an evidentiary dimension. If an update is withdrawn, auditors should still be able to determine which decisions it produced and how those decisions were handled. Otherwise, rollback can erase the very evidence needed to diagnose failure. Version-specific logs should therefore survive longer than the software version itself, subject to appropriate limits on sensitive-data retention.

The most obvious objection is cost. Maintaining environments, replaying decisions, and simulating downstream effects require engineering time. A transition regime could also slow beneficial updates. That objection supports proportionality, not inaction. The depth of review should depend on the magnitude of the change, the system’s operational reach, and the difficulty of correcting its decisions. Automated lineage capture and reusable comparison tests can reduce the burden. Moreover, the relevant comparison is not between auditing and zero cost. It is between planned transition controls and the often greater cost of investigating a failure after configurations, inputs, and personnel have changed.

There are also legitimate concerns about retaining proprietary code or sensitive records. Auditors need not demand that every artifact be made public. Reproduction can occur in a restricted environment, and cryptographic hashes can establish that tested files match deployed files without disclosing their contents. Data-retention schedules can preserve selected inputs or derived test cases rather than complete databases. These measures do not eliminate confidentiality risks, but they show that auditability and secrecy are not always mutually exclusive.

A remaining governance problem is deciding who may approve the classification of a change. If the development team alone labels updates as minor, transition auditing can become a paperwork ritual. At minimum, organizations should require independent review for changes affecting objectives, data populations, decision thresholds, or downstream interfaces. Auditors should also inspect a sample of supposedly low-risk releases to determine whether the classification scheme is being applied honestly.

Algorithmic systems do not stand still between audits. Their data pipelines shift, dependencies are replaced, thresholds are tuned, and user practices adapt. Evaluating only occasional snapshots leaves a blind spot precisely where much operational risk accumulates: in the movement from one configuration to another. By preserving version provenance, testing consequential differences, and proving that rollback is real, transition audits would make algorithmic oversight more continuous without requiring every update to start from zero. The essential question would no longer be only, “Does this model perform well?” It would also be, “What changed in the decision system, and can anyone account for the consequences?”
