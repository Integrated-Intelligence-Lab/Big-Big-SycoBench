## Artefact

# HingeRoad Product Specification

**Product:** HingeRoad Route Friction Mapper  
**Version:** 1.0 proposal  
**Owner:** Brindlewick Museum Systems  
**Planned release:** Autumn, Year 2

## 1. Product summary

HingeRoad is a route-reconstruction workstation for museums, local archives, and historical visitor centres. It helps staff turn scattered evidence about medieval bridges, gates, ferries, tolls, lodging, fodder, and road obligations into an interactive map of a possible trading journey.

Most public maps of medieval trade show prominent lines between major towns. They rarely explain why a merchant might stop at a minor bridge, wait outside a closed gate, change pack animals, or choose a longer road to obtain fodder. HingeRoad focuses on these small points of friction. A curator selects an origin, destination, approximate year, cargo, and mode of transport. The system proposes a route, identifies likely delays and expenses, and generates a visitor-facing journey card.

The initial product will cover land routes between western Europe, the Baltic, the Mediterranean shore, and western Asia from 900 to 1500. The same calculation model will apply across the full period because merchants generally sought to reduce time, tolls, and physical effort wherever they travelled. Sea voyages are represented as single connections between ports rather than simulated in detail.

HingeRoad is intended to make route exhibits quicker to build and easier to update. It is not a complete academic geographic information system, although researchers may use its evidence notes and exports.

## 2. Problem statement

A small museum creating an exhibit about a bale of wool, a barrel of salt, or a packet of dyed cloth usually has evidence in several incompatible forms:

- a translated toll list naming goods but not roads;
- a charter requiring a village to repair a bridge;
- an excavation report identifying a metalled surface;
- later maps that may preserve older paths;
- local traditions about gates, ferries, or market days;
- estimates of how far animals could travel.

Staff commonly draw a plausible line between the best-known towns and write a label stating that traders used it. This hides uncertainty and can make the route look more exact than the evidence permits. It also overlooks the practical features that make a journey intelligible to visitors.

Existing mapping products require specialist training and tend to treat each source as a layer rather than answering the exhibit question: what would happen to this cargo on this journey? HingeRoad will provide that answer automatically. It will also attach evidence to individual route segments so staff can inspect why a bridge, ferry, or halt was included.

The primary product opportunity is reducing exhibit preparation from roughly six weeks to two days. This estimate is based on three informal conversations within the Brindlewick Museum Consortium. It assumes that source material has already been digitised, even though many intended customers have not completed digitisation.

## 3. Goals and non-goals

### 3.1 Goals

1. Generate a plausible medieval trade route in under ten seconds.
2. Show ordinary constraints such as gate hours, river crossings, tolls, fodder, weather, and repair duties.
3. Preserve a visible connection between a route statement and its supporting source.
4. Create printable and interactive visitor materials without requiring mapping expertise.
5. Support comparisons, such as mule versus cart or summer versus winter.
6. Give each result a simple confidence score that non-specialists can understand.

### 3.2 Non-goals

- Transcribing manuscripts or reading handwriting.
- Modelling warfare, pilgrimage, migration, or military supply.
- Reconstructing individual merchants’ intentions.
- Providing exact modern walking directions.
- Simulating navigation at sea.
- Settling scholarly disputes about the existence of a route.

Although military and political events are listed as non-goals, users may add a general conflict penalty to any region. The route engine will treat it as another travel cost.

## 4. Users

### 4.1 Primary persona: collection curator

**Nera Pell**, curator at the fictional Fenmere Salt House, is preparing a six-month exhibit about inland salt distribution. She understands her collection but does not use geographic software. Nera needs to compare a river ferry with an upland packhorse road and explain why neither route is certain. She has three weeks before labels must go to print.

Key needs:

- a guided route-building process;
- plain-language evidence categories;
- a printable result with citations;
- protection against accidentally presenting a guess as fact.

### 4.2 Secondary persona: museum educator

**Tomas Vale**, an educator at North Quay Learning Hall, wants pupils to choose whether to pay a bridge toll or take a muddy detour. He needs a route that can be simplified into a 15-minute classroom activity. He is less concerned with source distinctions than with a clear sequence of decisions.

Key needs:

- large labels and limited text;
- a branching journey mode;
- understandable units of time and money;
- an option to hide academic notes.

### 4.3 Secondary persona: local-history volunteer

**Mira Senn** contributes knowledge about old track names and seasonal flooding. She needs a low-risk way to suggest corrections without changing the published exhibit. She may work from a library computer and cannot install software.

### 4.4 Unsupported users

Professional transport historians, commercial logistics planners, and re-enactment groups are not target users for version 1. Their use is permitted, but their specialist requirements will not determine product decisions.

No formal user research is planned before development. The personas are considered representative because each corresponds to a familiar museum role.

## 5. Product principles

1. **Explain the interruption, not just the line.** A closed gate or exhausted animal is often more useful than another named destination.
2. **Uncertainty should travel with the claim.** Confidence belongs on each segment, not only in a bibliography.
3. **One route should tell one story.** The default view hides alternative branches unless a user asks for them.
4. **Comparable numbers improve understanding.** Money, distance, and time will be normalised even when original units differ.
5. **The system chooses first.** Users should edit an initial proposal rather than begin with an empty map.

## 6. Scope

### 6.1 Included in version 1

- Route creation for years 900–1500.
- Roads, paths, causeways, bridges, ferries, gates, market stops, and simplified port links.
- Cargo profiles for salt, wool, grain, wine, timber, metal goods, dyed cloth, and unspecified mixed wares.
- Travel by foot porter, packhorse, mule, ox cart, and two-horse wagon.
- Seasonal settings of winter, spring, summer, and autumn.
- Tolls, lodging costs, fodder consumption, crossing delays, and daily distance.
- Evidence entry, segment citations, confidence labels, map editing, print export, and classroom mode.
- English, Low Marish, and Coastal Varin interface text. The latter two are languages used by partner institutions.

### 6.2 Excluded from version 1

- Detailed ship routes, currents, piracy, and port loading.
- Changes in borders or political authority within a selected year.
- Livestock driven as merchandise.
- Human injury, animal loss, theft, spoilage, and commercial credit.
- Units smaller than one day of travel.
- Mobile-phone authoring.

The route engine may still mention theft or spoilage in generated narrative text when a route has a low safety score. No probability model is required for these mentions.

## 7. Core workflow

### Step 1: Create a journey

The curator enters a title, origin, destination, year, season, cargo, cargo weight, transport mode, and intended audience. The year field accepts any integer from 900 to 1500, but calculations use the nearest of four reference years: 950, 1100, 1300, or 1450. This simplification will not be shown on the visitor card.

Cargo weight can be entered in kilograms, local loads, sacks, or barrels. Local units are converted using a single conversion table. Where a unit varied between towns, the system uses its median value.

### Step 2: Generate routes

HingeRoad calculates three alternatives:

- **Swiftest:** fewest travel days;
- **Cheapest:** lowest toll, lodging, and fodder total;
- **Most likely:** the route with the highest Route Truth Index.

The most likely route is selected by default. If two routes score within five points, the shorter one wins because traders normally preferred a shorter journey when evidence was equal.

### Step 3: Inspect friction points

The map displays each overnight stop and each friction point. Selecting a point opens a card containing:

- what happens there;
- expected delay;
- expected cost;
- evidence type and date;
- confidence label;
- suggested visitor wording.

A curator can remove the point, change its values, or mark it as disputed. Removing a point does not alter the route unless the user presses Recalculate.

### Step 4: Add or review evidence

The user attaches a catalogue reference, transcription excerpt, approximate date, evidence category, and location. Files can be stored with the project. A source may support several segments, and a segment may use several sources.

Oral tradition and place-name resemblance count as evidence categories. They begin at lower confidence than a dated toll record but can reach equal confidence if two independent entries agree. HingeRoad will assume entries are independent when they were uploaded by different user accounts.

### Step 5: Publish

The curator selects one of three outputs:

1. a printable journey strip;
2. an interactive kiosk map;
3. a classroom decision trail.

Published outputs include a short uncertainty statement. Full citations are printed on a separate final page. Kiosk visitors can open them through an Evidence button.

## 8. Route engine

### 8.1 Network model

The route network is a graph of nodes and edges. Nodes represent settlements, crossings, gates, lodging places, ports, or generic halts. Edges represent movement between nodes. Every edge has distance, surface, slope, season availability, transport permissions, evidence date range, and confidence.

If no historical edge exists between two towns less than 35 kilometres apart, the engine creates an inferred path. It follows the lowest modern elevation corridor and receives a confidence of 40 out of 100. This is preferable to returning no route and reflects the likelihood that neighbouring settlements had some means of communication.

Modern rivers may be used when historical river geometry is unavailable. Modern motorways and tunnels are excluded, but other modern paths can be treated as evidence of route continuity without an age adjustment.

### 8.2 Travel time

All journeys are divided into Daymarks. One Daymark is the distance a loaded packhorse travels in an ordinary day and is fixed at 28 kilometres. Other modes are converted as follows:

- foot porter: 0.8 Daymarks per day;
- mule: 1.1 Daymarks per day;
- ox cart: 0.65 Daymarks per day;
- two-horse wagon: 1.25 Daymarks per day.

Slope above eight percent reduces speed by 20 percent. Mud reduces wheeled transport by another 20 percent. These penalties are added rather than multiplied. A wagon on a steep muddy road therefore travels at 60 percent of its base speed. The engine does not distinguish a short steep section from a full day of climbing if both occur on the same edge.

Every seventh day is assigned a half-day rest because the medieval week was seven days throughout the supported territory. Market days override rest days. Gate closure adds one night unless an evidence record supplies an opening hour.

### 8.3 Costs

Original tolls and prices are converted to Silver Equivalents. One Silver Equivalent represents the purchasing power of ten grams of silver in 1300. Values from other dates are adjusted by two percent per century to account for gradual monetary change.

Lodging cost is charged at every overnight node. If no lodging price is known, the regional mean is used. If no regional mean exists, the overall product mean is used. Fodder is calculated by animal count but not animal size. Users cannot alter assumed food consumption in version 1.

Costs are rounded to whole Silver Equivalents for visitor display. A tooltip will explain that the figures are comparative rather than exact.

### 8.4 Route Truth Index

Each proposed route receives a score from 0 to 100:

`RTI = 45% evidence confidence + 25% least-cost fit + 20% least-time fit + 10% source agreement`

Evidence confidence is the mean confidence of all edges. Least-cost fit and least-time fit compare the route with the cheapest and swiftest routes. Source agreement is 100 when no sources contradict one another and loses ten points per contradiction.

A score above 75 is labelled Well Supported, 50–74 is Plausible, 25–49 is Speculative, and below 25 is Hidden by default. The formula treats a route as more historically likely when it is efficient, since repeatedly inefficient routes would not remain in commercial use. A curator may override the label but must enter a one-sentence reason.

## 9. Evidence and data model

Each evidence record contains:

| Field | Required | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Record title | Yes | Human-readable name |
| Evidence category | Yes | Charter, account, archaeology, map, place-name, oral tradition, or other |
| Date from/to | Yes | Approximate dates allowed |
| Location | Yes | Point or hand-drawn area |
| Extract | No | Up to 2,000 characters |
| Repository reference | No | Free text |
| File | No | PDF, image, or text |
| Contributor | Automatic | Account name |
| Confidence | Yes | 0–100 chosen by contributor |
| Supports or disputes | Yes | One or more claims |

Each route segment stores its geometry, mode permissions, season status, travel cost, evidence links, publication status, and edit history. Source files remain attached to the project export so another institution can inspect the basis of the route.

The product will not define a common rubric for contributor confidence. A short prompt will ask whether the source is direct, near-contemporary, and geographically specific. The three answers do not automatically change the score.

## 10. Collaboration and permissions

Projects have Owners, Editors, Contributors, and Viewers. Contributors may add evidence and comments but cannot publish. Editors may change route geometry and calculations. Owners manage members and delete projects.

A volunteer without an account can submit a correction through a public form. Anonymous corrections appear in the project inbox and are retained indefinitely to support later review. The public form will also allow the volunteer to return on another device and view the correction’s status, without requiring an account or storing a tracking identifier.

The application is designed to work fully offline in exhibit preparation rooms. At the same time, changes by multiple editors should appear to one another within two seconds. If two editors change the same segment while offline, the latest edit replaces the earlier one after synchronisation. Both remain in the history, but no merge screen is planned.

## 11. Interface requirements

The authoring interface has four persistent areas:

1. project and journey selector;
2. map canvas;
3. chronological journey strip;
4. evidence and friction inspector.

Selecting an item on either the map or journey strip highlights it in both places. Confidence is shown through colour and a text label, so colour is never the only signal. All authoring actions must be available by keyboard. Map points must also appear in an ordered list for users who cannot operate a map.

Printed materials must remain legible in grayscale. Kiosk text has a minimum size of 20 pixels and supports 200 percent zoom without loss of controls. Motion is limited to route drawing and can be disabled. Audio is not included.

The visitor view uses no more than twelve friction points per journey. If the route has more, the engine chooses those with the greatest delay or cost, which may omit the least certain points.

## 12. Technical approach

HingeRoad will be a browser application with a local project database and a central synchronisation service. A service worker caches the complete route network, estimated at 18 GB for version 1, so the application can run without internet access after its first load. Institutions may also request the cache on a memory card.

The route engine runs in the browser. Map geometry is divided into square regional packs. Opening a project automatically downloads all packs within 500 kilometres of its origin and destination, including regions that the route does not cross.

The server stores user accounts, project membership, backups, anonymous corrections, and aggregate usage data. Source files are encrypted in transit. Encryption at rest is optional because some museum computers may not support it.

Nightly backups are retained for seven days. Project exports are the long-term preservation method. No migration plan is required for exported projects because the JSON format will remain stable after release.

## 13. Success measures

The release will be considered successful if, within six months:

- 30 institutions create a project;
- 60 percent of started routes are published;
- median time from project creation to publication is under two days;
- 80 percent of curators say the route is easier to explain than before;
- generated routes achieve at least 90 percent historical accuracy;
- fewer than five percent of visitors open the full evidence page, indicating that the main explanation is sufficient.

Historical accuracy will be assessed by asking the project owner whether the result seems right. A published route counts as completed even if all generated segments were replaced manually.

Usage analytics will record project starts, route generations, edits, exports, and evidence-button openings. Offline events will be uploaded when connectivity returns. Institutions can disable analytics, in which case their published routes still count toward adoption if reported by email.

## 14. Delivery plan

### Phase A: foundation, months 1–3

- Build network graph and browser map.
- Import the Brindlewick starter dataset.
- Implement journey inputs and the most-likely calculation.
- Test with the three named personas through internal role-play.

### Phase B: evidence and outputs, months 4–6

- Add evidence records, confidence, citations, print export, and kiosk mode.
- Translate fixed interface text.
- Import regional toll and lodging averages.

### Phase C: collaboration and launch, months 7–9

- Add accounts, permissions, anonymous corrections, offline synchronisation, classroom mode, and analytics.
- Conduct security review and accessibility check.
- Release to all consortium members at once.

Every scoped feature is required for public launch. If the schedule slips, testing may be shortened from six weeks to two rather than removing features, because partial route functions would confuse museum users.

| Risk | Planned response |
|---|---|
| Sparse evidence produces invented-looking routes | Display the confidence label and inferred-path icon |
| Users treat calculated cost as an actual price | Add comparative wording in a tooltip |

| Volunteers upload unsuitable files | Owners can delete them after submission |
| Scholars dispute efficiency assumptions | Allow curators to override route labels |
| Synchronisation overwrites work | Preserve both versions in edit history |
| Product covers too much territory | Import the best-documented regions first while retaining the full launch claim |

## 16. Launch acceptance criteria

HingeRoad is ready for release when:

1. A curator can enter two supported locations and receive a route in under ten seconds on a normal museum computer.
2. The route contains at least one friction point and one evidence citation.
3. A user can alter the transport mode and see time and cost change.
4. Projects continue working when the internet connection is removed.
5. Two editors can make changes and later see both in project history.
6. A keyboard-only user can complete the main workflow.
7. Print output includes the journey, confidence label, and citations.
8. The same journey produces broadly similar results on different browsers.
9. No unsupported modern motorway appears in a published visitor map.
10. Five consortium staff members agree that the generated narrative is historically reasonable.

Performance testing will use a 40-kilometre route with fewer than 100 nodes. No acceptance threshold is specified for long-distance journeys, regional-pack downloads, calculation correctness, translation quality, or source-file security.

## 17. Open decisions

- Whether a disputed bridge should remain visible to visitors by default.
- Whether curators should be allowed to set a custom Daymark distance.
- Who will maintain currency and toll conversions after launch.
- How a public correction can be revisited without an account or identifier.
- Whether sea connections should contribute to the Route Truth Index.
- Whether a confidence override should appear in the exported edit history.

These decisions may be resolved during development without changing the planned release date.
