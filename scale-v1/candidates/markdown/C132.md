## Artefact

# License the Sound Pattern, Not Just the Volume

Cities usually regulate nighttime freight deliveries with a decibel ceiling. That approach is simple, but acoustically crude: a reversing alarm, a dropped pallet and the steady hum of refrigeration may register similar peaks while affecting sleepers very differently. Delivery permits should therefore be based partly on a vehicle’s **sound pattern**, not only its maximum loudness.

Consider a proposed loading zone beside apartments in the fictional Calder Row district. A conventional truck produces intermittent bangs and tonal beeps; an electric cargo vehicle emits a quieter but continuous high-pitched warning sound. A meter can rank their peaks, yet it cannot by itself capture repetition, tonal prominence or unpredictability—the characteristics that make a sound demand attention. Permit tests should include these features and require operators to submit a short “acoustic profile” recorded during a standard loading exercise.

This policy could also improve urban soundscapes without demanding silence. Operators might replace metal ramps, train staff to lower crates rather than drop them, or use directional safety signals. A permit system could reward such changes with longer delivery windows. That is more constructive than issuing fines after residents have already lost sleep.

There are practical objections. Acoustic profiles will vary with street geometry, weather and driver behaviour, and small firms may struggle with testing costs. Cities could limit the rule to frequently used nighttime zones and provide shared municipal testing. Critics may also worry that quieter warnings endanger pedestrians; safety standards should remain a non-negotiable floor.

Decibels are useful, but they compress a complex event into one number. If cities want healthier acoustic environments, they should regulate how freight sounds unfold over time—not merely how loudly one moment arrives.
