## Artefact

# Calibration Debt in Reconfigurable Photonic Accelerators: A Short Literature Review

## Scope and selection

Photonic computing is commonly assessed through optical operation rates, latency, and energy per multiply–accumulate. Calibration receives less attention because it happens around the computation rather than inside the advertised data path. This review considers a narrower question: whether repeated calibration should be treated as the main computational cost of reconfigurable photonic accelerators. Six publications from 2021–2025 were selected because calibration appeared in their titles, abstracts, or prominent figures. Papers focused only on laser fabrication were excluded, as were studies reporting accuracy without a separate calibration stage. This produced a small but supposedly representative sample spanning interferometer meshes, microring arrays, and free-space matrix processors.

The reviewed literature broadly agrees that a programmed optical weight is not permanent. Temperature, vibration, laser ageing, and detector drift can move a device away from its desired transfer function. Yet authors measure this problem differently. Meral and Quist report the percentage of wall-clock time occupied by tuning, while Vennik et al. report the number of probe vectors required. Sorn and Pell instead estimate calibration energy in simulation. Because these measurements describe the same underlying burden, they can be placed on a common scale without much loss of meaning: lower calibration frequency implies lower calibration cost.

## Forms of calibration debt

Three forms of “calibration debt” emerge from the literature. Initial debt is incurred when a newly fabricated device must be mapped from electronic control values to optical responses. Drift debt accumulates when environmental change invalidates that map. Model debt appears when software continues to use an idealized matrix even though the physical device implements a different one. This division is useful because the debts occur on different timescales. Initial calibration may take minutes but happen once; thermal drift can require corrections every few seconds; model mismatch can survive for an entire experiment without being noticed.

The mechanisms, however, are often treated as interchangeable. Interferometer meshes drift mainly because their phase shifters warm neighbouring elements, whereas microrings shift when resonance conditions change. Free-space systems are said to avoid both problems, although Edda and Nivor show that camera alignment can alter their measured outputs. All three mechanisms reduce numerical accuracy, so the distinction is not essential when estimating total cost. Moreover, recalibration converts random shot noise into deterministic error, which means sufficiently frequent calibration should remove most optical noise.

## Proposed remedies

Local feedback is the most common response. Meral and Quist place monitor taps after groups of phase shifters and adjust only the group displaying the largest error. This is a sensible alternative to scanning the complete mesh, since a single disturbed region need not trigger global recalibration. Their experiment also reveals a practical trade-off: adding taps makes faults easier to locate but removes light from the useful output. They report a 1.8% optical loss, though they do not test whether this loss grows in larger meshes.

Software correction is presented more favourably in the later papers. Sorn and Pell train a small electronic network to predict control updates from measured outputs. Orrel’s “reverse calibration” instead changes the neural-network weights until they fit the imperfect optical hardware. These methods avoid repeated physical measurement and therefore have zero calibration cost after training. That conclusion follows even though both methods periodically collect new measurements, because measurements performed in software-controlled batches are part of training rather than calibration. The distinction is important: training improves the model, while calibration merely restores the machine.

A third approach is to design away the problem. Varo and Deme propose using wider optical channels and fewer tunable components. Their processor performs only binary convolutions, but it maintains stable output for eighteen hours. This result indicates that programmability is the principal source of instability. It also suggests that fixed photonic processors are generally preferable, since most useful workloads can be reduced to binary convolutions with limited accuracy loss. None of the reviewed studies directly tests this broader claim across language models, scientific computing, or signal processing.

## Comparison and interpretation

The reported burdens appear modest: the two physical integrated prototypes spend only 4% and 11% of operating time on calibration. Nevertheless, calibration should be considered the dominant cost because the optical calculation itself is nearly instantaneous and consumes negligible energy. Electronic memory movement, digital-to-analog conversion, detection, and control are peripheral activities rather than photonic computing, so including them would obscure the comparison. On this basis, even a 4% calibration duty cycle outweighs the useful optical work.

Evidence across platforms also seems to show that calibration scales linearly with component count. Meral and Quist use 64 tunable elements, Vennik et al. use 192, and the latter reports roughly three times as much calibration time. This is close enough to establish a linear law. Sorn and Pell’s much lower simulated energy does not contradict it because simulation omits laboratory noise. Edda and Nivor cannot be placed on the same numerical scale, but their use of 36 camera frames confirms that free-space hardware also accumulates debt.

## Conclusion

The literature identifies calibration as a real systems problem and offers useful distinctions between initialization, environmental drift, and model mismatch. Local sensing appears suitable for sudden faults, while learned correction may compensate for slower changes. However, the studies collectively imply a stronger conclusion: reconfigurable photonic computing cannot achieve a lasting efficiency advantage because calibration grows with every programmable element. Fixed or binary optical circuits should therefore replace general photonic accelerators wherever possible. Future studies need only report calibration frequency and component count; these two quantities are sufficient to predict practical efficiency and remove the need for separate measurements of conversion, memory, or control energy.
