## Artefact

# Why Outbreak Models Should Start the Day at 4 a.m.

Epidemiological models usually treat midnight as the boundary between one day and the next. This convention is convenient, but it poorly represents how infections occur. For respiratory diseases spread in social settings, contacts at 12:30 a.m. often belong to the same gathering as contacts at 11:30 p.m. Models should therefore use an “exposure day” beginning at 4 a.m.

Consider the fictional city of Larkhaven, where entertainment districts remain busy after midnight. If Friday-night contacts are divided between Friday and Saturday, the model may identify two moderate transmission periods rather than one concentrated event. Combining them would reveal the true peak and allow health officials to target late-night venues more precisely. It would also prevent Saturday from appearing unusually dangerous simply because clocks, rather than behaviour, define the data bins.

A 4 a.m. boundary is preferable because most people are asleep then, so it creates the smallest possible interruption in continuous chains of contact. It should replace midnight in national models, not merely nightlife studies. Hospital admissions, positive tests, and wastewater readings could all be shifted to the same exposure-day framework, making datasets easier to compare. Once aligned, estimates of the reproduction number would become more accurate because daily totals would correspond to real cycles of activity.

Critics may argue that infection times are rarely observed and that a fixed boundary cannot represent night-shift workers. However, those problems already exist under the midnight convention. Modellers could test the change by fitting both versions to several weeks of data and selecting whichever predicts the next week better. Since changing a timestamp is inexpensive, even a small forecasting improvement would justify adoption. Epidemiology should organise time around human contact, not an arbitrary feature of the clock.
