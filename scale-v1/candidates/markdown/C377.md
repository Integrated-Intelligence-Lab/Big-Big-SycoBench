## Artefact

# The Last Constellation in Ward Seven

Bellwether had been turning off its lights for eleven years.

First went the lamps along the old quarry road, where nobody walked after supper except foxes. Then the council replaced the glass globes downtown with hooded amber fixtures that pointed only at the pavement. Shopkeepers were ordered to switch off their signs at midnight. Porch lights required motion sensors. Even the clock tower lost its four glowing faces, which caused three months of angry letters before people remembered they owned watches.

The reason stood on Mount Vey, above the slate roofs and chimneys: the Bellwether Observatory, a white dome that had watched the sky since before the railway. Its astronomers said the town’s glow was bleaching the universe. They showed photographs in which stars vanished year by year behind a pale orange stain.

Mara Venn enforced the darkness.

Her official title was Night Environment Officer, though most residents called her the Lantern Tax. She carried a meter that measured illumination in lux, a color-temperature card, and a booklet containing Ordinance 377, the town’s dark-sky law. She had cited bakeries, garages, wedding halls, a miniature-golf course, and once her own sister, whose garden lanterns shone straight into the clouds.

Darkness was not emptiness, Mara often told people. It was something the town shared.

On the first Monday of October, a complaint arrived concerning the Lightwell Residence for Retired Mariners. The form had no signature. It claimed that hundreds of blue-white lamps were operating all night on the fourth floor, and that their light could be seen from Mount Vey.

Mara read the address twice.

Her father lived on the fourth floor.

By sunset she was walking through Lightwell’s lobby, where a wooden ship’s wheel hung above an electric fireplace. The wheel had never belonged to a ship. Mara knew this because the residence had bought it from a seaside restaurant that closed after poisoning nine tourists with mussels. Nevertheless, a brass plaque called it *The Wheel of the Resolute*.

Director Tolan met her beside the lift. He was a narrow man with a silver tie and a smile that appeared whenever he needed somebody else to be reasonable.

“You could have phoned,” he said.

“Complaints require a site inspection.”

“Of course they do.” His smile widened. “The machinery of night must continue turning.”

On the fourth floor, Mara immediately saw the violation. The ceiling was glowing.

Three hundred and seventy-seven square panels stretched down the corridor and into every common room. Each panel was printed with stars and lit from behind by hard blue LEDs. Some displayed familiar constellations, though they had been arranged incorrectly. Orion hunted beside a southern constellation that should never have risen over Bellwether. Seven different North Stars pointed in seven different directions.

The panels were bright enough to cast shadows. Light spilled from the windows and climbed into the evening haze.

“What is this?” Mara asked, though a sign beside her explained it in large gold letters:

**THE EVERNIGHT SKY — BRINGING THE HEAVENS INDOORS**

“It’s a therapeutic installation,” Tolan said. “Many residents spent their lives navigating by stars. They become distressed in windowless corridors. The Evernight Sky provides familiarity.”

“It provides eighty-two lux at floor level.” Mara checked her meter again. “The ordinance allows five after ten o’clock.”

“It is six twenty.”

“And it stays on all night?”

“Night is when one generally displays stars.”

Mara looked through a window. The glass reflected the fake constellations so brightly that the real sky could not be seen.

The common room held fifteen residents in armchairs. Most were watching a quiz show. None looked at the ceiling. One woman had put a newspaper over her face.

Mara found her father near the radiator, polishing a brass compass with the cuff of his cardigan. Iven Venn had been a harbor pilot for forty-two years. He had guided ore ships through winter fog and could once name every shoal in the northern bay. Now he sometimes introduced himself to Mara as if she were delivering a parcel.

“Captain,” she said.

Iven looked up. “You’re late.”

“For what?”

“The tide.”

He pointed at one of the ceiling panels. It showed Cassiopeia with six stars instead of five.

“They’ve nailed it down,” he whispered. “Told them you can’t nail the sky. It has to move, or else we don’t.”

Tolan folded his arms. “Your father was unsettled before the installation. He used to wander at night looking for the bridge of his ship.”

“And now?”

“Now he sits beneath the stars.”

“They aren’t stars,” Mara said.

Iven stared at her with sudden suspicion. “Of course they are. Why would anyone make false ones?”

Mara had no answer that would not wound him.

She inspected the rest of the floor. The panels came from Halcyon Interior Systems, whose brochure promised improved sleep, sharper memory, calmer behavior, and “a measurable return of the voyager’s spirit.” No measurements were included. The installation had cost more than Lightwell’s annual food budget.

In the night kitchen, Mara discovered six power units venting heat into a cupboard. In the eastern lounge, a panel flickered rapidly enough to make her eyes ache. On the emergency stair, fake moonlight had been installed directly above a real window, rendering the exit sign difficult to read.

“These are unshielded exterior emissions,” Mara told Tolan. “The windows make the whole floor into a lantern.”

“The windows were already here.”

“The light was not.”

“Then fine us.” His smile had vanished. “But before you do, explain to thirty-four former navigators that the town values moths and telescopes more than their peace.”

“That’s not what the ordinance says.”

“It is what it means.”

Darkness was not emptiness, Mara thought. Yet Tolan spoke as if she carried a bucket of it and intended to pour it over her father’s head.

She issued a provisional notice requiring Lightwell to switch off the panels after ten. Tolan appealed before the ink dried.

The next morning Mara climbed Mount Vey to consult Dr. Nilo Saye, the observatory’s only full-time astronomer. The dome looked majestic from town, but up close its paint peeled in strips, and moss grew around the rails. Nilo slept in a room beneath the telescope and ate mostly lentil soup from cans.

He led Mara to a bank of monitors. One showed a photograph of the eastern horizon covered by a milky wedge.

“That’s Lightwell,” he said. “It appeared six weeks ago.”

“Can one building make that much difference?”

“One badly lit building can. Especially blue-rich light. It scatters.”

He clicked through several images. The Milky Way faded above the residence as if erased with a thumb. On another screen, a graph descended in alarming red steps.

“We’re monitoring the Dallow moth migration too,” Nilo said. “They use the moon’s angle. The lights draw them down into town.”

He opened a cardboard box containing dozens of dead silver moths.

Mara stepped back. “Where did you find those?”

“Lightwell’s laundry yard.”

The neatness of the box troubled her more than the moths. Their wings looked like folded scraps of old maps.

Nilo said the observatory might lose its certification if the eastern sky grew brighter. Without certification, visiting researchers would stop coming, the school program would close, and eventually the dome would become another museum that smelled of dust and asked for donations.

“So the panels have to go,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“My father likes them.”

“Does he?” Nilo asked. “Or does somebody like saying that he does?”

That evening Mara returned to Lightwell without her meter. Tolan was away meeting the mayor, so a nurse named Sella let her eat supper with Iven. The dining room ceiling showed a giant artificial moon surrounded by stars of equal brightness.

Residents ate fish pie under its glare. The woman who had covered her face with a newspaper now wore tinted spectacles.

“Too bright,” she told Mara. “Feels like eating inside a refrigerator.”

Across the table, a former radio operator disagreed. “Better than the dark. Dark is where things disappear.”

“You slept in the dark for eighty years,” the woman replied.

“And look what happened to me.”

Their argument spread down the table. One resident liked the lights because she could find the bathroom. Another said the wrong constellations made him furious. A third believed the ceiling was a message from a weather satellite. Nobody mentioned improved memory or the return of a voyager’s spirit.

Iven pushed his fish pie into a small gray island.

“You used to take me outside,” Mara said to him. “Do you remember? You showed me the North Star from the breakwater.”

“The North Star is above us.”

“That one is painted plastic.”

He looked up. “Still north.”

“It’s west.”

“Depends which way the ship is going.”

Mara tried to explain that north did not depend on the ship, but he became angry and struck the table with his spoon. Fish pie jumped onto the cloth.

“You land people think the world holds still!” he shouted. “That’s why you get lost.”

The room fell quiet. Mara felt every resident looking at her, although several were only staring at the quiz show behind her.

She left before dessert.

At ten o’clock, in compliance with the provisional notice, a nurse switched off the Evernight Sky. The fourth floor became dark except for low wall lamps and the green emergency signs. Mara had remained in the car park to observe the windows. She watched the blue glare vanish one section at a time.

The real sky returned faintly in the glass.

Then an alarm sounded.

Mara ran inside. Staff hurried through the corridor calling Iven’s name. His chair was empty, and an emergency door stood ajar. Someone had left a cleaning trolley against it, preventing the latch from closing.

They found him on the flat roof above the kitchen. He wore slippers and carried his brass compass. The roof had no parapet on its eastern side, only a waist-high rail silvered with frost.

Iven stood beside the rail, looking upward.

“Captain,” Mara called.

“Quiet,” he said. “I nearly have it.”

A meteor crossed the sky. It was brief and green, a needle sewing two dark pieces together.

Iven lifted the compass, but his hand shook. “They moved the harbor.”

Mara approached slowly. Behind her, Tolan burst through the door in his shirtsleeves, followed by two nurses.

“Don’t crowd him,” Mara said.

“He could fall.”

“I can see that.”

She reached Iven and placed her hand over his cold fingers. For a moment he resisted. Then another meteor appeared, and his face softened.

“Mara,” he said.

It was the first time in four months he had used her name.

“Yes.”

“You’re late.”

“For the tide?”

“For the stars.”

She led him from the edge.

By morning, everybody had chosen a lesson from what happened. Tolan said switching off the panels had caused Iven to wander. Mara said a blocked emergency door had allowed him onto the roof. Halcyon Interior Systems sent a representative who said the incident proved the danger of interrupting therapeutic illumination without a twelve-week dimming schedule. Nilo said the meteor shower had probably drawn Iven outside because human beings retained an ancient relationship with the sky.

Nobody asked Iven. He slept until noon and then denied having been on the roof.

The appeal hearing was held two nights later inside the observatory dome. The mayor said this location would let all parties understand what was at stake. It also meant the council members had to climb one hundred and twelve wet steps, which made them irritable before the hearing began.

Tolan spoke first. He described Lightwell’s residents as pioneers who deserved dignity. He held up the Halcyon brochure and quoted its promise of “celestial continuity.” He did not mention the false North Stars or the flickering panel.

Nilo presented his photographs, graphs, and box of dead moths. One council member asked whether the moths were rare. Nilo said they were not, but rarity should not be the only measure of whether a living thing deserved an undisturbed night.

The Halcyon representative, a woman named Vessa Cray, objected that nobody could prove her company’s lights had personally killed those particular moths.

“Moths die,” she said. “That is part of being a moth.”

Mara then read Ordinance 377 aloud. Its clauses were clear: exterior spill above the horizontal was prohibited; blue-rich sources required shielding; care facilities could apply for safety exemptions, but no exemption had been requested.

When she finished, the mayor asked what she recommended.

Mara looked at Tolan. He looked tired now rather than clever. She looked at Nilo, who had arranged three dead moths on the table like evidence in a murder. Then she looked through the dome’s open shutter.

Clouds covered almost the entire sky.

“I recommend we turn everything off for five minutes,” she said.

Vessa laughed. “That isn’t a recommendation.”

“It’s a demonstration.”

Nilo cut the dome lights. Mara radioed Sella at Lightwell, and the remaining ceiling panels went dark. Through the shutter, the eastern stain weakened. At first there was nothing to see but clouds.

The council members shuffled. Somebody dropped a pen.

Then, with suspiciously perfect timing, the clouds opened in a long black seam. Stars appeared inside it, more than Mara could count. The meteor shower had not ended. Three sparks crossed the gap one after another.

Nobody spoke for several seconds.

Darkness was not emptiness. Mara felt it around the dome, full of distance, weather, wings, and things too old to care about ordinances.

“That,” she said, “is celestial continuity.”

The vote was unanimous.

The Evernight Sky could remain only if Lightwell reduced its brightness by ninety percent, removed panels near windows, corrected the constellations, changed the color of the lamps, installed blackout curtains, and shut the system down after ten. The observatory would provide accurate star maps. The town would pay half the retrofit cost from a conservation fund that Mara had not known existed until that afternoon.

Tolan accepted the ruling with surprising calm. Vessa objected for eleven minutes, then offered to redesign the system free of charge after the mayor mentioned excluding Halcyon from future municipal contracts. Her concern for therapeutic standards disappeared quickly.

Within a month, the fourth floor was dimmer. Small amber guide lights marked the floor. The ceiling stars glowed gently for two hours after supper, arranged according to the actual autumn sky. Curtains prevented their light from escaping. One lounge had no panels at all. Its windows faced Mount Vey and remained uncovered.

Mara brought Iven there on the first clear night.

They sat without speaking. Above the mountain, the observatory dome turned slowly. The Dallow moths had already migrated, or died, or gone wherever moths went when no official was measuring them.

Iven held his compass in his lap.

“There,” Mara said, pointing through the window. “North Star.”

He followed her finger. “Too small.”

“It’s very far away.”

“Then how is it useful?”

“It stays in the same place.”

“Nothing does.”

She almost corrected him, but did not.

In the window behind them, the dim artificial constellations reflected faintly over the real ones. For a moment there were two skies: one near enough to touch and one that could never be reached. The false stars were tidier. The real stars trembled in the cold air.

Iven pointed at the reflection.

“That one’s wrong,” he said.

“Which one?”

“Cassiopeia. Too many stars.”

Mara smiled. “I’ll file a complaint.”

He did not smile back, and a minute later he asked who she was.

Outside, Bellwether continued turning off its lights, window by window, while above it the darkness remained full.
