## Artefact

# The Value of a Second Look: Resurfacing Stale Records on FieldWeave

## Question

Citizen science platforms usually prioritize new submissions, even though older observations may remain identifiable. FieldWeave tested a different queue called **Second Look**, which resurfaced photo observations that had received no confirming identification 14 days after submission. The queue also notified the original contributor when an identifier added a comment or changed the proposed label.

This analysis asks whether Second Look reduced the verification backlog, improved identification quality, and encouraged contributors to return—or merely shifted volunteer attention away from newer records.

## Data and comparison design

The dataset contains 243,718 public observations submitted to eight FieldWeave regional circles between 11 September and 3 March. Four circles—Northmere, Bellstrand, Low Fen, and Pinewash—enabled Second Look on 4 December. Four circles with similar prior submission volumes and verification rates retained the standard newest-first queue until March. The launch week was omitted, and outcomes were observed through 17 April to allow each record a complete follow-up window.

After removing private records, automated sensor imports, duplicate uploads, and observations without photographs, 71,402 records met the queue’s eligibility rule: still unverified at age 14 days. Of these, 35,916 came from rollout circles and 35,486 from comparison circles.

A record was considered **verified** when two independent users agreed at genus level or finer within 45 days of queue eligibility. Three secondary outcomes were examined:

- receipt of any substantive human comment within 45 days;
- a correction, defined as replacement of the leading label at genus level or above after disagreement;
- contributor return, defined as the original submitter adding another observation within 30 days of queue eligibility.

The main estimate is a pre/post difference-in-differences: the change in rollout circles minus the change in comparison circles. Confidence intervals use 1,000 record-level bootstrap samples. This approach adjusts for platform-wide changes over the study period, although the circles were not randomly assigned.

## Main results

| Outcome | Rollout: before | Rollout: after | Comparison: before | Comparison: after | Estimated change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Verified within 45 days | 31.8% | 39.6% | 33.1% | 35.0% | **+5.9 pp** |
| Received a comment | 18.7% | 31.2% | 19.4% | 21.2% | **+10.7 pp** |
| Label corrected | 4.9% | 6.8% | 5.2% | 5.5% | **+1.6 pp** |
| Contributor returned | 44.6% | 46.8% | 45.3% | 45.9% | **+1.6 pp** |

The estimated verification gain was 5.9 percentage points, with a record-level bootstrap interval of 4.8 to 7.0 points. Relative to the rollout circles’ pre-launch rate, this is an increase of about 19%. Median time from eligibility to verification also fell from 12.4 to 8.1 days in rollout circles, compared with a decline from 11.8 to 10.9 days in comparison circles.

The larger increase in comments suggests that Second Look changed more than the final verification count. A manual review of 400 randomly sampled comments found that 61% requested a missing angle, scale reference, habitat detail, or distinguishing feature. Another 24% explained why a proposed identification was unlikely. The remainder were greetings, acknowledgements, or comments too vague to classify.

Corrections increased alongside verifications. This is potentially beneficial: resurfacing a record can expose an initially plausible but incorrect label. However, a higher correction rate could also indicate unstable consensus. To investigate, a separate stratified sample of 600 completed records was evaluated by three experienced FieldWeave reviewers who had not participated in the original discussions. Final community labels agreed with the review panel for 86.3% of resurfaced records and 83.0% of comparison records. The 3.3-point gap had an interval spanning roughly zero, so the audit is suggestive rather than decisive.

## Variation by subject matter

Second Look was not equally useful for every observation type.

| Broad group | Estimated verification change |
|---|---:|
| Fungi and lichens | +9.8 pp |
| Insects and other invertebrates | +7.1 pp |
| Plants | +4.4 pp |
| Birds | +1.2 pp |
| Human-made traces and structures | +3.5 pp |

Fungi and invertebrates had both the largest backlogs and the largest gains. Bird observations changed little, probably because recognizable bird records were already identified quickly and difficult photographs often lacked enough detail for later review. This pattern supports using resurfacing selectively rather than applying the same queue weight to every category.

All four rollout circles showed higher verification gains than their matched comparison circles, but the circle-level estimates ranged from 2.1 to 9.4 points. Contributor return was less consistent: it rose in two rollout circles, was nearly unchanged in one, and declined slightly in Pinewash. The pooled 1.6-point return estimate should therefore not be interpreted as a uniform community effect.

## Attention costs

The feature increased completed reviews, but it also concentrated work. Before launch, the most active 5% of identifiers performed 47% of identifications on eligible records. After launch, they performed 52% of Second Look actions. Meanwhile, the share of new observations verified within their first 48 hours fell by 1.3 points in rollout circles and by 0.5 points in comparison circles, implying a net decline of 0.8 points.

That trade-off is modest compared with the gain for older records, but it matters operationally. A backlog tool that depends heavily on a small group of specialists may improve headline completion rates while increasing volunteer fatigue. The dataset records actions, not perceived burden, so it cannot establish whether the concentration was unwelcome.

## Robustness and limitations

Reweighting observations so that rollout and comparison circles had the same monthly mix of broad subject groups reduced the verification estimate from 5.9 to 5.3 points. Extending the outcome window from 45 to 60 days produced a 5.5-point estimate. Excluding users who contributed to both rollout and comparison circles increased the estimate to 6.2 points, although this removed many of the platform’s most active identifiers.

Several uncertainties remain. Rollout circles volunteered for early access and may have had organizers more willing to promote neglected records. Matching used prior volume and verification rate but did not account for all differences in moderation, local events, or specialist availability. Identifiers can work across circles, creating possible spillovers into the comparison group. In addition, the bootstrap treated records as independent even though outcomes may be correlated within circles and users; the reported intervals are therefore likely too narrow. Eight circles provide too few clusters for a precise hub-level uncertainty estimate.

## Conclusion

Second Look was associated with a meaningful reduction in FieldWeave’s stale-record backlog, especially for fungi and invertebrates. It generated useful diagnostic comments and more corrections without evidence of lower final label agreement. The smaller and less consistent contributor-retention effect does not support presenting the queue primarily as a loyalty tool.

FieldWeave should retain the feature as a limited, category-aware queue rather than make it the universal default. A reasonable next test would cap the resurfaced share of each identifier’s feed, randomize eligible records within circles, and measure volunteer burden directly. That design would clarify whether the observed gains come from the queue itself and whether they can be sustained without delaying attention to new submissions.
