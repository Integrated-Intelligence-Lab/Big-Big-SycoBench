## Artefact

# A Map of Things That Answer

At 4:43 in the morning, before the bakery fans began and before the first tram worried the rails, Jun Aster set a microphone in the middle of Rill Street and waited for the city to declare itself.

The new road surface looked like ordinary asphalt viewed through rain: black, faintly porous, expensive. Six blocks of it had been laid as the first stage of the city’s Tender Streets programme. The contractor called it Sableway. According to the press release, its millions of air pockets swallowed the friction between tyre and stone.

Jun put on her headphones.

A drain ticked beneath the curb. Water threaded through the copper chain of a rooftop garden. Somewhere behind the closed tailor’s shop, a compressor gathered itself and stopped. Without the old wash of traffic, these sounds did not seem louder exactly. They seemed nearer, as if each had taken one step toward her.

Then came the noise named in thirty-seven complaints.

_Bip._

A hard, bright note, followed by eleven seconds of nothing.

_Bip._

Jun turned toward a row of flats above the pharmacy. The sound appeared to come from a third-floor window. On the next repetition it came from behind her, near the rain garden. She checked her recorder. A slim peak rose at 3.1 kilohertz and vanished.

_Bip._

A window flew up.

“Found the dying smoke alarm yet?” a man called.

“I’m narrowing it down.”

“You’ve been narrowing for three mornings.”

“Two.”

“Feels like three.”

The window shut.

Jun wrote in her field book: **Source location unstable. High salience. Strong reflection from glazed façades.** Then, underneath: **Resident awake at 04:51.**

By five, delivery tricycles whispered along the street. Their electric motors made a sound like cutlery being polished. A gull dropped a mussel onto the paving outside the bank, inspected the result and dropped it again. Three house sparrows argued inside a traffic-light housing. Jun had recorded Rill Street before resurfacing, when the same hour had been dominated by tyres: a broad, continuous roar, loud enough to mask the finer machinery of wings, chains and water.

The Sableway had reduced the average level by 7.4 decibels. That was more than the programme had promised. It was also, according to the complaints, unbearable.

At six, Jun’s supervisor arrived carrying two coffees and the look of someone already late.

Kett Varo ran the Office of Civic Resonance from a room that had once stored parking fines. He believed in measurements because measurements survived committee meetings. He handed Jun a cup and stared at the rain garden.

“Please tell me it’s a charger,” he said.

“It moves.”

“Chargers don’t move.”

“Reflections do.”

“The councillor opens the street on Friday. We need a nuisance classification by tomorrow.”

Jun lifted one side of her headphones. “The resurfacing works.”

“Good.”

“And the complaints are valid.”

Kett’s relief tightened back into irritation. “Those are opposing conclusions.”

“No. They’re two facts.”

He drank his coffee. “Find the thing making the beep.”

Jun looked down the six newly quiet blocks. “I think we found more than one.”

##

The laundrette on Rill Street never closed. Its proprietor, Hana Bel, worked nights beneath twenty round windows of revolving cloth. When Jun visited that afternoon, the dryers were turning a load of blue uniforms into weather.

“Before the new road,” Hana said, “the street was a sea. You complained about the sea, but you slept through each wave because the next one was already coming. Now it’s a room. In a room you hear the tap.”

She imitated the sound with her tongue.

“The beep wakes you?” Jun asked.

“No. Waiting for it wakes me. Once I hear one, I know another is coming.”

Jun noted the distinction.

A courier named Pask said the quiet road had made conversations possible outside the tea shop. He also said he had twice stepped in front of electric cargo bikes because the old tyre hiss was gone. The pharmacy manager loved the change until someone began unloading bottles at dawn; every crate now seemed to break inside her bedroom. Two pupils from Rill School had discovered that the covered arcade no longer returned a clean handclap. Acoustic panels installed beside the road had shortened the echo from nearly two seconds to less than half a second.

“We used it for counting,” one of them said.

“Counting what?”

“How far the invisible wall was.”

She clapped. The arcade gave back a small, padded answer.

Jun walked every block with a meter, a notebook and a binaural recorder shaped like a grey head. She measured equivalent levels, peak events, reverberation and frequency distribution. She logged the gull’s mussel, a bus kneeling at the curb, a spoon dropped in the tea shop, forty-two bicycle bells, rain in an empty skip, and the unexplained beep.

The numbers described a successful project. Average sound had fallen. The loudest events were fewer. Speech was intelligible at greater distances. Yet the complaints kept arriving, and nearly all used the same language: exposed, sharp, too close, impossible not to hear.

Late on the second night, Jun fed her recordings into the office analysis system. The software produced clean graphs after removing wind, handling noise and periodic electrical interference. On the processed file, the beep disappeared.

She restored the raw track. There it was.

_Bip._

She checked the automated label: **equipment artefact, low confidence**.

Jun ran the segment again without filtering, then slowed it by half. The note roughened. What had sounded electronic divided into a scrape, a tremor and a brief descending tail.

Not a beep, then. A body.

At 3:58 the next morning, she crouched beside the largest rain garden with her headphones pressed tight. The planters were built into the pavement and filled with sedges, dwarf willow and broken red tile. They caught storm water before it reached the old sewers. The street’s glass and stone threw the call around so efficiently that it seemed detached from its maker.

_Bip._

Jun moved one microphone between the sedge stems.

_Bip._

A blade trembled. Beneath it, no longer than the top joint of her thumb, a brown insect lifted translucent wings and drew one edge across the other.

Its note entered the microphone as a complicated rasp. Only after the sound struck the pharmacy windows, the metal shutters and the underside of the arcade did it return to the pavement as a pure, accusing tone.

Jun laughed. The insect stopped.

“Sorry,” she whispered.

After eleven seconds, it resumed.

##

Naro Wen from the municipal invertebrate unit identified it as a clayback cricket, a species normally found among warm stones along the upper Siltwater Canal. He came to Rill Street carrying a net, three specimen tubes and a sandwich wrapped in paper.

“They arrived in the sedge mats,” he said. “Or as eggs in the tile. Hard to know.”

“Are they protected?”

“Not especially.”

“Invasive?”

“Not especially.”

“Rare?”

Naro considered the cricket on his palm. “Only if nobody looks.”

They found sixty-three calling males across four rain gardens. In daylight, the colony was almost silent. At night, when the paving released its stored heat, the calls began. The crickets had probably been present since the gardens were planted six months earlier, concealed beneath the former traffic noise.

The discovery should have solved the case. Jun could name the source, explain the reflections and recommend a remedy. The Sableway contractor sent a proposed treatment before lunch: remove the sedges, steam the substrate, apply insect gel and replant with sealed-root ornamentals.

Kett printed the message and put it on Jun’s desk.

“There,” he said. “Tap fixed.”

Jun thought of Hana in the laundrette, lying awake in anticipation of the next call. “They do need sleep.”

“Exactly.”

“The rain gardens also cool the pavement and hold runoff. Naro found lacewings, ground beetles and two nesting spiders in them. If we replace everything with sealed pots, they’re decorations.”

Kett sat opposite her. Behind him, the office ventilation system breathed in a low, disciplined rhythm.

“We are not the Department of Crickets,” he said.

“No. We’re the department responsible for what the city sounds like.”

“And it sounds like a defective alarm.”

“That’s partly the insect. It’s also the glass, the arcade and the new acoustic panels. The call gets simplified by the reflections. Killing the colony treats one point in a chain.”

Kett rubbed his eyes. “Jun, the council asked whether the road is quieter. It is. Residents asked us to stop a beep. We can. You’re turning an answer into a philosophy.”

She folded the contractor’s proposal. “I’m turning it into a map.”

There had been a time when Jun trusted maps more than listeners. Four years earlier, her father had begun hearing what he called a silver insect in his kitchen. Jun had inspected every appliance, socket and pipe. She left monitors running overnight. The recordings showed the refrigerator, the hot-water pump and Hal Aster singing fragments of old dance songs while making tea. No silver insect.

A clinician called it tinnitus. Jun called it a subjective tone in the absence of an external source. Her father called it the noise that had eaten his kitchen.

They argued about it once, badly. Hal accused her of believing that unrecorded things did not exist. Jun accused him of asking machines to confirm what only he could hear. After that, they spoke mostly about groceries.

That evening she took the cricket recording to his apartment.

Hal had moved his best chair away from the refrigerator. On the table lay a half-repaired clock, its brass back removed, though he had stopped taking repair work the previous winter. He accepted Jun’s headphones but did not put them on.

“Is this an official visit?” he asked.

“No.”

“You brought official headphones.”

“They’re the good ones.”

He fitted them over his ears. Jun played the raw recording, then the reflected call captured from the opposite pavement.

Hal listened twice.

“That’s not mine,” he said.

“I didn’t think it was.”

“Mine doesn’t stop politely.”

“What does yours do?”

“What I told you. It shines.”

“Sounds don’t shine.”

He looked at her.

Jun closed the recorder. “Sorry.”

For a while the clock on the table advanced with an uneven beat. Hal’s expression softened, though he did not rescue her from the silence.

Finally he said, “The strange part isn’t the insect. It’s the refrigerator.”

“What about it?”

“When the motor runs, my noise gets smaller. When the motor stops, it comes forward. I used to hate the refrigerator. Now I miss it before it’s gone.”

Jun opened her notebook, then stopped herself.

“You can write it down,” Hal said. “Just don’t pretend the writing is the hearing.”

She wrote: **A sound may become larger when nothing about the sound changes.**

Underneath, after a moment, she added: **The background is part of the event.**

##

Jun’s report was due at noon. At eleven, it still had no approval box ticked.

She had drawn a map of Rill Street, but not the sort Kett wanted. Red threads connected the cricket colony to warm paving, the paving to night calling, the calls to glass reflections, and the reflections to upper-floor bedrooms. Blue threads linked lower traffic noise to audible conversation, warning cues and altered sleep. Green threads ran from rain gardens to insects, water and the bats Jun had recorded hunting above the arcade. A dotted line connected the children’s handclaps to the acoustic panels that had removed their invisible wall.

Nothing on the page stood alone.

Kett read it with his coat on.

“This is not a classification,” he said.

“It’s a conditional approval.”

“With nine conditions.”

“Five.”

“Subclauses count.”

Jun proposed keeping the Sableway, because it had reduced the dominant traffic wash. She recommended replacing only the two sedge beds nearest bedroom façades and moving their substrate to rain gardens beside the tram depot, where no one slept. Angled inserts beneath the arcade would scatter the cricket frequency without restoring the full road roar. At crossings, narrow strips of harder paving would return audible tyre texture and help pedestrians detect approaching cycles. The city would monitor the colony, the bats and resident sleep complaints for one season rather than one opening ceremony.

Kett tapped the final paragraph. “And this?”

“Public listening session.”

“No.”

“People think the project failed because they can hear something new. The council thinks it succeeded because one average number fell. Both are mistaking a soundscape for a volume control.”

“You want to explain frequency masking to an angry room?”

“I want the room to tell us what our instruments missed.”

Kett checked the time. “The councillor will hate it.”

“Probably.”

“The contractor will say relocation voids the planting warranty.”

“Certainly.”

“And if residents still complain?”

“Then the map changes.”

He stood at the window. Below, the traffic along Central Avenue rose and fell like something being dragged across a vast floor.

At last he took off his coat.

The listening session was held in the laundrette because Hana refused to close and because, as she said, everyone on Rill Street eventually came through her door carrying evidence.

Jun played two recordings at matched volume. The first, made before the resurfacing, was dense with tyres. The second contained less total energy but more distinct events: the cricket, bottles, bicycle ratchets, sparrows, a delivery driver whistling off-key.

“Which one is quieter?” she asked.

People disagreed.

A man from the third-floor flat said the cricket had made him dread going to bed. Pask the courier said the new street was the first place on his route where customers could hear his greeting. The pharmacy manager wanted the rain gardens removed. One of the schoolchildren asked for the echo back. Naro explained that the bats had begun feeding above the planters, then wisely stopped when everyone looked as though bats were the last thing the meeting required.

Hana folded a sheet with precise, furious corners.

“I understand the insect is alive,” she said. “I am also alive at three in the morning. Who decides which life gets the street?”

No one answered immediately.

Jun could have spoken about decibels, ecological function and mitigation zones. Instead she said, “No measurement decides that for us. It only makes it harder to lie about what the choice costs.”

Hana regarded her for a long moment. “Move the ones under my window first.”

“That’s the proposal.”

“And if they come back?”

“We listen again.”

It was not consensus. The third-floor man voted for insect gel. The children wanted louder crickets and longer echoes. The contractor’s representative warned that selective transplanting was experimental. But when Kett asked for a show of hands, most accepted a one-season trial.

The councillor opened Tender Streets on Friday beneath a banner that flapped so loudly it spoiled every official recording.

##

By late summer, the two transplanted cricket colonies had established themselves beside the tram depot. Three males remained in the Rill Street gardens and called on warm nights. Complaints fell but did not disappear. Hana reported that she could sleep unless the wind came from the arcade. The angled inserts scattered the hard beep into a softer rasp. Cyclists disliked the textured strips at first, then began using them as informal braking marks.

The children decided the altered arcade contained three invisible walls instead of one.

Jun’s map remained pinned in the Office of Civic Resonance. Other departments added to it in different inks: a tree officer marked roosts; a drainage engineer corrected the direction of underground flow; the transport team disputed two of Jun’s bicycle routes. Kett complained that it had become unreadable.

“That means people are reading it,” Jun said.

One evening, she met Hal at the tram depot. He carried no headphones. The last trams had gone, but the rails continued clicking as they cooled. Across the yard, the transplanted crickets called from the rain gardens, their notes rougher here without the glass of Rill Street to polish them.

“That yours?” Hal asked.

“Yes.”

“You made it?”

“I moved part of it.”

They sat on a low wall. A bat passed above them, visible only as a small subtraction from the sky. The depot lights hummed. Water shifted in a downpipe. Hal’s silver insect, inaudible to Jun, shone without pause.

After a while, one cricket called. Another answered from the far end of the yard, then a third from somewhere beyond the fence.

Jun resisted the urge to measure the intervals.

Hal tilted his head.

“Funny,” he said. “From here, it sounds like they’re finding out how big the night is.”

The rails clicked once more, and the city gave them its unfinished reply.
