## Artefact

# Predict the Spark, Not Only the Fire

**To:** Director, North Vale Wildland Service  
**From:** Office of Risk Planning  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Subject:** Creating a near-miss registry to improve wildfire ignition forecasts

## Decision requested

Approve a two-season pilot requiring agency units and participating infrastructure operators to record outdoor heat-and-spark incidents that do not become reportable wildfires. Use these records to build separate forecasts for **ignition** and **escape after ignition**, rather than continuing to train the FireArc prediction system only on fires that enter the official incident database.

The pilot would cover Harrow Basin and Mica Plain, cost an estimated **$410,000 in the first year**, and report interim results before the 2027 peak fire season.

## Why the current forecast misses useful information

FireArc estimates the daily probability of a wildfire in each five-kilometre grid cell using weather, vegetation, terrain and prior incident locations. Its historical outcome variable is a reportable fire. This is operationally convenient but combines two different events:

1. A heat source reaches receptive fuel and produces an ignition.
2. The ignition escapes immediate control and becomes a reported incident.

Those events have different causes. Equipment condition, work schedules and public activity affect ignition. Detection time, access, crew proximity and water availability strongly affect escape. Treating them as one outcome can produce a “suppression shadow”: locations with frequent ignitions but fast intervention appear safer than they are.

A retrospective review in Mica Plain illustrates the gap. Crew logs for 2025 contained 286 responses to sparks, smouldering vegetation, overheated machinery and escaped debris burns. Only 31 corresponding events appeared in the official wildfire layer. The review covered one district and records were reconstructed after the season, so it does not establish a regional rate. It nevertheless indicates that the service discards a substantial amount of information about when fuels actually ignite.

This matters when conditions change. A corridor that has many sparks but rarely records a fire because a maintenance crew is nearby may become dangerous during a staffing shortage or communications outage. A model trained only on past escaped fires will not readily distinguish that corridor from one where ignitions seldom occur.

## Recommended policy

### 1. Establish a narrow definition and minimum record

For the pilot, a **wildfire near-miss** should mean an outdoor event in which a flame, ember, electrical arc, hot surface or mechanical spark contacts combustible vegetation and prompts observation or intervention, but does not become a reportable wildfire.

Each record should contain:

- time and location, rounded to a 250-metre grid for shared datasets;
- suspected heat source, selected from a standard list;
- observed fuel type and whether visible flame or smouldering occurred;
- means of detection and estimated time to intervention;
- action taken and final outcome; and
- reporting organisation and confidence in the source classification.

Narrative text, personal names and photographs should be optional. A short form integrated into existing dispatch and maintenance applications is preferable to a new stand-alone portal.

### 2. Collect exposure denominators

Incident counts alone can be misleading. Ten machinery sparks along a corridor with 50,000 operating hours imply a different risk from ten sparks during 500 hours. Participating organisations should therefore provide monthly totals for relevant activity, such as equipment hours, train movements, permitted burn days or kilometres inspected.

These totals need not identify individual workers or customers. Operators that regard exact activity figures as commercially sensitive could submit them in broad bands. This compromise will reduce precision, but it is better than interpreting raw near-miss counts as direct measures of danger.

### 3. Produce two linked forecasts

The analytics unit should estimate:

- the probability of an ignition given weather, fuels and activity; and
- the probability of escape given ignition, detection and response conditions.

The existing FireArc public map would continue to display a combined risk category. Internally, duty officers would see the two components. A high-ignition, low-escape cell could trigger equipment inspections or temporary work restrictions, while a low-ignition, high-escape cell could justify pre-positioning detection or suppression resources.

No automated restriction should be imposed solely from the pilot model. For the first two seasons, forecasts should support, not replace, the duty officer’s documented judgement.

## Alternatives considered

| Option | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Continue using reported fires only | No new cost or reporting burden | Preserves the suppression shadow and provides little insight into ignition sources |
| Add near-miss records without activity totals | Faster and cheaper to launch | Busy operators may look artificially hazardous; trends may reflect reporting effort |
| **Pilot near-miss and exposure reporting** | Separates ignition from escape and permits rate comparisons | Requires data agreements, training and quality checks |
| Mandate region-wide reporting immediately | Produces broad coverage quickly | Likely to create resistance and inconsistent data before definitions are tested |

The pilot is preferable because the value of near-miss data is plausible but not yet demonstrated at regional scale.

## Implementation

During the first 90 days, the service should appoint one data steward, publish the reporting definition and sign agreements with the Vale Grid Cooperative, Bracken Rail Works, district road crews and the two participating fire districts. Private land managers may report voluntarily.

From October 2026 through March 2027, users should test the form with historical and low-season incidents. Training should focus on distinguishing an observed ignition from a merely unsafe condition. Reports should take no more than 90 seconds in routine cases, although this estimate should be verified during testing.

Live collection would run from April 2027 through October 2028. Quarterly audits should compare registry entries with dispatch notes, work orders and permit inspections. The service should fund small participation grants rather than penalise missing reports during the pilot. Near-miss records should be excluded from enforcement and personnel-performance proceedings, except where disclosure is required for an independently investigated serious incident.

The preliminary budget is $170,000 for application changes, $150,000 for an analyst and data steward, $60,000 for operator grants, and $30,000 for training and audit work. These figures assume that participating operators can export activity totals from existing systems.

## Evaluation

Before modelling begins, the analytics unit should freeze an evaluation plan. The main technical test should compare the new model with current FireArc using a future-season holdout and a district held out from training. Measures should include calibration, Brier score and the share of observed ignitions captured in the highest-risk decile.

The pilot should proceed to regional expansion if it improves ignition Brier score by at least 10 percent, does not materially worsen combined-fire calibration, and produces sufficiently complete records from at least three operator types. Operational review should also examine whether duty officers changed inspections, work timing or resource placement in response to the split forecast. The 10 percent threshold is a practical screen rather than an estimate of the improvement required to justify every possible operational cost.

## Principal risks

Reporting will be uneven. Organisations with stronger safety cultures may appear to have more hazardous operations simply because they document more events. Audits and exposure totals reduce this problem but cannot eliminate it, especially for unobserved ignitions on private land.

A non-punitive policy may improve candour, yet the service cannot promise absolute confidentiality. Operators may still withhold reports they view as reputationally damaging. Conversely, an overly broad definition could flood the registry with harmless sparks and inspection observations. The data steward should publish examples and reject records lacking contact with combustible vegetation.

Finally, improved prediction does not by itself create crews, inspectors or authority to restrict work. Expansion should occur only after the service identifies which actions each forecast category can realistically trigger.

## Recommendation

Approve the two-district pilot and first-year funding, with regional expansion contingent on the stated technical and operational review.
